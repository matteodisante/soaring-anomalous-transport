\chapter{Flight phases}
\label{ch:phases}

Chapter~\ref{ch:global} establishes which class of transport the archive exhibits, but it
cannot say which flight behaviour produces it, since every observable there averages over
climbs, glides and searches together. Disentangling them requires segmenting each flight
into its phases. This chapter defines the continuous-observation hidden Markov model
(HMM) used for that purpose, the reproducible route from a processed trajectory to phase
labels, and the measurements made possible by those labels.

The distinction is worth stating plainly, because it sets what a segmentation is allowed
to claim. Pre-segmentation determines \emph{which} class of transport is present;
segmentation explains \emph{which mechanism} produces it. A segmentation explains an
exponent, it does not measure one --- and if the two disagree, the disagreement is
evidence about the segmentation rather than about the transport.

\section{Segmentation into flight phases}
\label{sec:segmentation}
Building on the previous work of Vilpellet et al.~\cite{vilpellet2026}, each flight is
segmented into the three phases of the soaring cycle:
\begin{description}
  \item[Transition (glide).] The inter-thermal cruise: a near-straight, energy-efficient
        glide toward the next source of lift (sinking, large directed horizontal
        displacement). This is the directed ``step''.
  \item[Search.] The exploratory phase entered when no thermal is found at a comfortable
        altitude: gentle turns and probing manoeuvres to sample the air mass and locate the
        next thermal while limiting altitude loss. It mediates the passage from one step to
        the next trap.
  \item[Climb (thermalling).] Circling within rising air to regain altitude: persistent
        turning with small net horizontal displacement. This is the localized ``trap''.
\end{description}
Vilpellet et al.~\cite{vilpellet2026} use an HMM~\cite{rabiner1989} with binary
trajectory-derived observations: the sign of the vertical velocity, curvature-sign
persistence, and an indicator of straightness. Binary summaries are deliberately robust,
but discard the amplitude of climbing, speed and turning. Here we retain the same
three-state interpretation but use continuous kinematic observations and a Gaussian HMM.
This makes the within-phase variability visible, avoids thresholds that would be specific
to one aircraft type, and lets correlated features be modelled jointly. A model is fitted
separately for paragliders and hang gliders: their speeds and turning radii are physical
properties of different aircraft, not variation that a single emission distribution should
absorb. This three-state description refines the two-state caricature underlying
continuous-time random walks and L\'evy walks.

\subsection{From a trajectory to continuous observations}
\label{sec:phasefeatures}
The input is the final table of Chapter~\ref{ch:dataset}: within every retained segment it
contains a uniform clock $t$, locally Cartesian position $(E,N,z)$, and the
Savitzky--Golay velocity and acceleration components. It is important that ``segment''
has the same strict meaning here as in the preprocessing: a long gap or an impossible fix
ends one observation sequence and begins another. Neither a feature window nor an HMM
transition is ever allowed to bridge that gap.

The raw archive uses several logger cadences. A discrete-time transition matrix has a
physical interpretation only when one transition represents the same elapsed time in every
flight, so each eligible preprocessed segment is evaluated on an internal grid of
$\Delta=\SI{10}{\second}$. A segment logged more sparsely than this is not upsampled and
is left unclassified. At faster native cadences the already smoothed kinematics are
interpolated only to evaluate this common decision grid; no position is invented across a
preprocessing boundary. Thus a phase boundary is resolvable only to $\Delta$, not to the
nominal native cadence.

At a decision time $t$ the observation is the four-dimensional vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf X_t =
  \left(\overline{v_z},\,\overline{v_h},\,
        \overline{\lvert\omega\rvert},\,C_\omega\right)_t .
  \label{eq:phasefeatures}
\end{equation}
Here $v_h=(v_E^2+v_N^2)^{1/2}$ and the signed horizontal turning rate is
\begin{equation}
  \omega = \frac{v_Ea_N-v_Na_E}{v_E^2+v_N^2}.
  \label{eq:turnrate}
\end{equation}
The denominator is evaluated only above a conservative horizontal-speed floor, so a
nearly stationary numerical heading cannot create a spurious turn. Bars in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:phasefeatures} are time averages over the centred window
$[t-W/2,t+W/2]$, with $W=\SI{30}{\second}$, approximately one thermalling revolution
\cite{vilpellet2026}. The fourth feature is the continuous direction-coherence statistic
\begin{equation}
  C_\omega(t) =
  \frac{\left|\int_{t-W/2}^{t+W/2}\omega(u)\,\mathrm du\right|}
       {\int_{t-W/2}^{t+W/2}|\omega(u)|\,\mathrm du},
  \qquad 0\le C_\omega\le1,
  \label{eq:turncoherence}
\end{equation}
with $C_\omega=0$ when the denominator vanishes. A sustained circle has high
$\overline{|\omega|}$ and $C_\omega\simeq1$; a search with reversals has lower coherence;
and a transition has a small turn rate. The mean vertical velocity separates rising
thermalling from the other behaviours, while the horizontal speed preserves information
about directed gliding. A full $W$-second window is required: the first and last
$W/2$ seconds that cannot support it are retained as explicit \emph{unclassified feature
edges}, rather than being padded with another segment or silently assigned a phase.
The preprocessing quality flags are also part of this contract. A feature window that
touches a reconstructed-altitude sample is excluded, so that $\overline v_z$ is never
based on an invented vertical channel; one that touches an off-centre
Savitzky--Golay derivative at a segment edge is excluded as well. These interior
non-classifiable windows split the HMM sequence just as an acquisition gap does, rather
than becoming a numerical bridge between two reliable observations.

\subsection{Gaussian hidden Markov model}
\label{sec:gaussianhmm}
Let $Q_t\in\mathcal S=\{\mathrm T,\mathrm S,\mathrm C\}$ denote the unobserved phase
(transition, search or climb) at decision time $t$, and let $\mathbf X_t$ be the observed
feature vector of Eq.~\eqref{eq:phasefeatures}. An HMM separates the state process from
the observation process, an especially useful construction when behavioural phases are
not directly observed from telemetry~\cite{langrock2012,mcclintock2020}. Its first-order,
time-homogeneous state process is specified by
\begin{equation}
  \pi_i=\Pr(Q_1=i),\qquad
  a_{ij}=\Pr(Q_{t+1}=j\mid Q_t=i),\qquad
  \sum_j a_{ij}=1.
  \label{eq:hmmtransition}
\end{equation}
The diagonal of $A=(a_{ij})$ captures persistence; its off-diagonal elements describe the
empirical organisation of the soaring cycle.

Conditional on $Q_t=i$, the adopted emission density is the multivariate Gaussian
\begin{equation}
  b_i(\mathbf x)=p(\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x\mid Q_t=i)
  =\frac{\exp\!\left[-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_i)^\mathsf T
       \Sigma_i^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_i)\right]}
       {(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma_i|^{1/2}},\qquad d=4.
  \label{eq:gaussianemission}
\end{equation}
Each state has its own unrestricted covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$. Consequently the model
does \emph{not} assume that vertical speed, horizontal speed and turning are independent;
their within-phase covariance is a parameter to estimate. For numerical conditioning all
four columns are standardized using only the mean and standard deviation of the training
flights. The fitted transformation is then applied unchanged to validation, test and
deployment flights, so information from held-out trajectories cannot enter the model.

The HMM assumptions are compactly expressed by the joint density of a state path
$q_{1:T}$ and observations $\mathbf x_{1:T}$,
\begin{equation}
 p(q_{1:T},\mathbf x_{1:T})=
 \pi_{q_1}b_{q_1}(\mathbf x_1)
 \prod_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{q_tq_{t+1}}b_{q_{t+1}}(\mathbf x_{t+1}).
 \label{eq:hmmjoint}
\end{equation}
Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:hmmjoint} over all $3^T$ paths gives the likelihood of an
unsegmented trajectory. The forward recursion evaluates that sum in $O(T|\mathcal S|^2)$,
rather than enumerating paths:
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(\mathbf x_1),\qquad
 \alpha_{t+1}(j)=b_j(\mathbf x_{t+1})\sum_i\alpha_t(i)a_{ij},\qquad
 \mathcal L=\sum_i\alpha_T(i).
 \label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
The corresponding backward recursion gives the posterior occupation probability
$\gamma_t(i)=\Pr(Q_t=i\mid\mathbf x_{1:T})=\alpha_t(i)\beta_t(i)/\mathcal L$.
It quantifies uncertainty even where a single phase label is ultimately required.

\subsection{Estimation, decoding and state names}
\label{sec:hmminference}
The parameters $\lambda=(\boldsymbol\pi,A,\{\boldsymbol\mu_i,\Sigma_i\})$ are fitted
without phase labels by maximum likelihood. Baum--Welch~\cite{baum1970}, the HMM instance
of expectation--maximization~\cite{dempster1977}, alternates two steps. In the E step,
forward--backward supplies $\gamma_t(i)$ and the two-time posterior
$\xi_t(i,j)=\Pr(Q_t=i,Q_{t+1}=j\mid\mathbf x_{1:T})$. In the M step these expected hidden
counts update, for example,
\begin{equation}
 a_{ij}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\xi_t(i,j)}
                 {\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_t(i)},\qquad
 \boldsymbol\mu_i=\frac{\sum_t\gamma_t(i)\mathbf x_t}{\sum_t\gamma_t(i)},
 \label{eq:baumwelch}
\end{equation}
with $\Sigma_i$ the analogue weighted covariance around $\boldsymbol\mu_i$. The likelihood
does not decrease at each EM iteration, but can have local optima; ten fixed-seed
initialisations are therefore fitted and the one with the largest training likelihood is
retained. Sequence lengths are passed explicitly to the fitting routine, so the final
point of one flight or preprocessing segment contributes no transition to the first point
of the next.

Numerical HMM states have no intrinsic order. Complete train-only manual annotations map
the three components to $\{\mathrm T,\mathrm S,\mathrm C\}$ by the one-to-one permutation
that maximizes their agreement; this is a naming operation, not supervised fitting. The
mapping is fixed before validation and is never informed by test annotations.

For a new feature sequence, the reported hard segmentation is the \emph{entire} most
probable path, found by the Viterbi recursion~\cite{viterbi1967},
\begin{equation}
 \delta_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(\mathbf x_1),\qquad
 \delta_{t+1}(j)=b_j(\mathbf x_{t+1})\max_i\{\delta_t(i)a_{ij}\},
 \label{eq:viterbi}
\end{equation}
followed by backtracking through the maximizing predecessor of every $\delta_{t+1}(j)$.
This differs from assigning $\arg\max_i\gamma_t(i)$ separately at each time: Viterbi
chooses a globally coherent state sequence and incorporates the transition penalties. The
output retains both the Viterbi phase and all three posterior probabilities, so an
apparently sharp boundary can be distinguished from an uncertain one without smoothing
or manually merging short runs afterwards.

The ordinary HMM has limits that the output must not conceal. It assumes stationary
emissions within a phase and conditional independence of observations given $Q_t$; rolling
features make the latter only approximate. It also imposes a geometric dwell-time law,
\begin{equation}
 \Pr(D_i=d)=a_{ii}^{d-1}(1-a_{ii}),\qquad
 \mathbb E[D_i]=\frac{\Delta}{1-a_{ii}}.
 \label{eq:hmmsojourn}
\end{equation}
Observed run durations and their left/right censoring at feature edges are therefore
reported beside Eq.~\eqref{eq:hmmsojourn}. A systematic mismatch is evidence for a
hidden semi-Markov extension, not a reason to alter labels post hoc
\cite{langrock2012}.

\subsection{Reproducible protocol and validation}
\label{sec:phasevalidation}
For each discipline the retained flights are deterministically split at the \emph{flight}
level into 70\% training, 15\% validation and 15\% test sets. The Gaussian HMM sees only
unlabelled training observations; the in-memory fitting sample is selected deterministically
from whole, contiguous sequences and is capped only to bound memory. The train annotations
name the resulting components. Validation annotations are used to diagnose the feature and
model assumptions. After the protocol is frozen, the held-out test annotations provide the
single final estimate of generalization.

Annotations record a source, flight and preprocessing-segment identifier, half-open
interval bounds $[t_{\mathrm{start}},t_{\mathrm{end}})$,
phase, split and annotator. They are deliberately interval-level judgements: ambiguous
boundaries can be omitted rather than falsely turned into pointwise ground truth. Evaluation
reports accuracy and precision, recall and F1 for each phase, together with macro-F1 and a
95\% bootstrap interval that resamples flights rather than temporally correlated points.
The result is supplemented by trajectory maps coloured by inferred phase, the transition
matrix, Gaussian emission parameters, posterior uncertainty, phase occupancy and duration
distributions. No universal pass/fail accuracy or expected time budget is imposed; those
quantities are empirical results of this archive.

The two persistent artefacts are a point table, carrying the decision time, four features,
Viterbi phase and three posteriors, and a run table, carrying each consecutive phase,
its duration and its boundary-censoring flags. They are written separately from
\path{fixes.parquet}, together with the model, scaler, state mapping, configuration and
flight-split manifest. The conversion from an unsegmented trajectory to the three phase
sequences is therefore reproducible and auditable rather than a visual classification.

\section{Phase-resolved observables}
\label{sec:phaseobs}
The segmentation gives access to a second group of observables, defined per phase occurrence and
then as ensemble distributions. These are the building blocks of an intermittent-transport
description and, unlike those of Section~\ref{sec:obs-global}, cannot be obtained without
first segmenting the flight.
\begin{description}
  \item[The waiting phase: climb and search.] Between two consecutive glides the flier
        advances little horizontally: it searches for a thermal and climbs in it. For a
        transport model these two phases together form the \emph{waiting time} between
        directed steps, so we treat them on an equal footing and build three distributions --
        the climb duration $\tau_c$, the search duration $\tau_s$, and their sum
        $\tau_w=\tau_s+\tau_c$, the total time spent essentially stationary between glides.
        The distinction matters: Vilpellet et al.~\cite{vilpellet2026} report the
        search times to be broadly \emph{power-law} distributed while the climb times are
        closer to \emph{exponential}, so that the heavy tail of the combined waiting time
        $\psi(\tau_w)$, the quantity that determines whether trapping drives
        sub-diffusion, would be inherited from the search phase. Whether this still holds on
        the larger dataset is itself one of the things to re-check once the segmentation is in
        place. We therefore report $\psi(\tau_w)$ together with the separate $\psi(\tau_c)$ and
        $\psi(\tau_s)$. Alongside the durations we record the climb kinematics (altitude gained
        $\Delta z$, mean climb rate, thermalling radius and period) and the search geometry
        (path length, tortuosity, net displacement) -- the search being the phase in which
        prior work locates the signature of pilot skill.
  \item[The directed step: glide (transition).] Each glide contributes a horizontal step of
        length $\ell$ and duration $\tau_g$, with ground speed, glide ratio (horizontal
        distance per unit altitude lost) and sink rate. Because a glide is close to ballistic,
        $\ell\approx v_g\,\tau_g$, step length and duration are expected to be nearly
        proportional; we test this directly through the joint law of $(\ell,\tau_g)$ -- the
        $\ell$--$\tau_g$ scatter, the conditional mean $\langle\ell\mid\tau_g\rangle$, and the
        spread of the ratio $\ell/\tau_g$ (the glide speed). Both marginals matter: the tail of
        the step-length distribution $\lambda(\ell)$ controls super-diffusive contributions, so
        we compute the distributions of $\ell$ and $\tau_g$ separately as well as their joint
        law.
  \item[Anatomy of a flight.] Number of thermals (climbs) per flight, inter-thermal distances,
        and the fraction of both time and along-track distance spent in each phase: the coarse
        composition that sets the weight of each phase in the global transport.
  \item[Angular diffusion between glides.] The turning angle between the heading of one
        transition segment and the next -- the segment-level counterpart of the turning angle
        of Section~\ref{sec:obs-global}. How fast successive glide directions decorrelate is
        the key ingredient of the minimal model below.
\end{description}

Two couplings then decide which transport model applies, and are stated here as explicit
objects of study. The first is the \emph{step--waiting coupling}: whether a long glide tends
to be followed by a long wait, i.e.\ whether $\ell$ and $\tau_w$ are correlated. A genuine
coupling is the defining feature of a L\'evy walk and is what separates it from a decoupled
CTRW, in which jumps and waiting times are drawn independently.

The second is the \emph{memory in the phase sequence}. A flight is a discrete sequence over
the three states $\{\text{transition},\text{search},\text{climb}\}$, and memory can hide in
two places. (i) The \emph{order} of the phases, summarised by the transition matrix
$P_{ij}=\Pr(\text{next}=j\mid\text{current}=i)$: a perfectly cyclic flight
($\text{T}\!\to\!\text{S}\!\to\!\text{C}\!\to\!\text{T}$) gives a near-deterministic matrix,
whereas aborted climbs, repeated searches or skipped phases appear as off-cycle probabilities
and reveal how stereotyped the soaring cycle actually is. (ii) The \emph{durations}, through
correlations between consecutive phase durations -- for instance whether a long climb tends to
precede a long glide. Both probe the \emph{renewal} assumption underlying the textbook CTRW
and L\'evy-walk results, in which each step and wait is independent of the history: if the
transition matrix is structured or the durations are correlated, the process is non-renewal
and those formulas must be amended. These measurements determine whether a memoryless
model is admissible.

\section{Reproducing the reference results}
\label{sec:reproducing}
A first objective of the analysis, undertaken once the segmentation is in place, is to check
whether the enlarged---and eventually multi-source---dataset reproduces the quantitative and
graphical results of Vilpellet et al.~\cite{vilpellet2026}. That study covers paragliders,
hang gliders and sailplanes; the present data cover the first two (Chapter~\ref{ch:dataset}),
so both the paraglider and the hang-glider comparisons are within reach, while the sailplane
figures become reachable once that source is added. Two figures are the main targets.

The first is the per-phase conditional MSD (their Fig.~3): the transition segments should
stay ballistic ($\alpha=2$, i.e.\ $H=1$) across time lags; the search phase should be
strongly sub-diffusive ($H\approx0.3$ for paragliders and hang gliders, $\approx0.2$ for
sailplanes); and the climb phase should be quasi-ballistic at long lags but with an effective
velocity about two orders of magnitude smaller, including the transient anti-correlation near
the $\sim\!30$\,s thermalling period.

The second is the set of per-phase descriptive statistics (their Fig.~4): the glide ratios
($\approx 8.5$, $10$, and $25$ for paragliders, hang gliders, and sailplanes), the
horizontal-velocity statistics, the heavy-tailed distribution of transition times, the
search-time fraction and the effective search radius, the vertical climb velocities, the
thermalling radii, and the thin, near-exponential distribution of climb times---together with
the global time budget ($\approx\!60\%$ transition, $\approx\!35\%$ climb, the remainder
searching). The global exponent is not on this list: it needs no segmentation, and
Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-verdict} has already put this archive's value beside the reported
$H\approx0.88$.

Recovering these results would validate the acquisition pipeline and the segmentation and
would test the universality of the reported behaviour on a substantially larger sample;
systematic departures would be equally informative, pointing to dataset- or method-dependent
effects to investigate. This comparison is therefore the bridge between the present
data-acquisition stage and the original analysis that follows.

\section{Modelling and simulation}
\label{sec:modelling}
The aim is a \emph{minimal} stochastic model that reproduces the asymptotic anomalous
scaling reported for soaring (a Hurst exponent $H \approx 0.88$) with as few ingredients as
possible. A preliminary working hypothesis---to be made precise, and possibly revised, once
the empirical phase statistics are available---is that the transport is carried mainly by
the transition (glide) phases. The model would therefore resolve the transition segments
explicitly while merging search and climb into a single effective non-transition state, so
that the soaring cycle reduces to a sequence of directed glides interrupted by
reorientations. Anomalous transport would then emerge from the directed transition segments
combined with an \emph{angular diffusion} of the heading from one transition to the next:
successive glide directions decorrelate gradually rather than abruptly, producing a
correlated random walk whose persistence controls the long-time exponent. Simulated
ensembles would be compared with the data to test whether these ingredients suffice. These
are baseline ideas, to be refined---or changed---as the analysis progresses.
