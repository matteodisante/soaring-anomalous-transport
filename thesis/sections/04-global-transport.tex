\chapter{Global transport}
\label{ch:global}

\begin{revblock}
This chapter measures the transport of the retained ensemble without segmenting a single
flight. What can be established that way is a \emph{class} of transport: how far a wing
gets in a given time, how that growth is distributed, how long the motion remembers its
direction, and how many distinct regimes the answer needs. What cannot be established is
which flight behaviour produces it, and that is Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}.

The measurements are made on the analysis ensemble assembled in Chapter~\ref{ch:dataset}
and defined at the close of Sec.~\ref{sec:pipelinemap}: \num{\StatMsdParaFlights}
paraglider and \num{\StatMsdHangFlights} hang-glider flights, holding
\num{\StatMsdParaSegments} and \num{\StatMsdHangSegments} retained segments. Every
statistic below is computed within a retained segment and never across the gap that ends
one (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}): a boundary removes the pairs of fixes that straddle it
rather than joining them, and a flight answers a given lag only when it holds a fix within
half its own native interval of that lag \impldetails{impl:global}.

Section~\ref{sec:prelim} constrains how that ensemble may be averaged, in two ways that
hold throughout. The horizontal components are not equivalent: the ratio of their mean
squares sits at \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMedian\ for paragliders over the lags read here,
between \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMin\ and \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMax\ and never touching
unity, and at \StatPrelimHangIsoRatioMedian\ for hang gliders. The two marginals are
therefore measured separately, and a pooled one-dimensional marginal is never substituted
for the two-dimensional propagator.

The second constraint is heterogeneity within a discipline. Read against the pooled
ensemble MSD over the same lags, the typical wing class departs by
\SI{\StatPrelimParaClassTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} and the typical orographic group by
\SI{\StatPrelimParaGroupTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent}, against
\SI{\StatPrelimParaSeasonTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} across seasons. Wing class and
terrain are relevant strata and season is not, so the two disciplines are never pooled,
every exponent is reported by stratum as well as pooled, and a pooled figure is read as an
average over populations known to differ rather than as a property of a homogeneous one.

Over \SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, on an estimator that
annihilates any polynomial trend in the trajectory, cross-country soaring gives
$\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ for paragliders and
$\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo\pm\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErr$ for hang gliders. It is not the
Lévy walk this thesis was framed around: the spectrum of moments is straight, $\nu(q)$
moving by \StatShapeParaNuSpread\ and \StatShapeHangNuSpread\ across the range of $q$ it
is read over, and the run lengths such a description would rest on have no scale, their
tail index falling from \StatShapeParaBetaStraight\ to \StatShapeParaBetaLoose\ as the
threshold that declares a turn is scanned. It is not Gaussian either: the non-Gaussian
parameter is $\StatShapeParaNonGaussMedian$ for paragliders and
$\StatShapeHangNonGaussMedian$ for hang gliders in the median, rising to an interior maximum
near \SI{\StatShapeParaNonGaussPeakS}{\second}, so one exponent governing every moment
states how the increment distribution scales and nothing about its shape.
\end{revblock}

\section{Displacement from take-off measures the launch geometry}
\label{sec:obs-global}
\begin{revblock}
Every flight starts at the origin, $\mathbf{r}(0)=\mathbf{0}$, so the position after an
elapsed time $t$ and the displacement from take-off are the same vector. Its mean square
admits two averages, and they are not two attempts at one quantity. The ensemble average
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MSD}(t)=\big\langle\,|\mathbf{r}(t)|^2\big\rangle ,
  \label{eq:ea-msd}
\end{equation}
taken over flights at a fixed elapsed time, answers how far from its launch site the
population has got after flying for $t$. Its growth law $\mathrm{MSD}(t)\sim t^{\alpha}$
fixes the transport regime --- $\alpha=1$ Brownian, $\alpha>1$ super-diffusive, $\alpha<1$
sub-diffusive, with $H=\alpha/2$ --- and it is the observable the anomalous-transport
question is normally posed with. The time average
\begin{equation}
  \overline{\delta^2(\tau)}
  =\big\langle\,|\mathbf{r}(t_0+\tau)-\mathbf{r}(t_0)|^2\big\rangle_{t_0} ,
  \label{eq:ta-msd}
\end{equation}
taken over starting times $t_0$ inside one record, answers how far a wing travels in a
window of duration $\tau$ placed anywhere in its flight.

The ensemble average is synchronised at take-off, so a feature it carries at a fixed
elapsed time is a property of that common origin. The time average slides its window along
each record and meets a lag wherever it occurs, with no privileged origin. A feature the
first shows and the second does not belongs to the origin; a feature both show is a
property of the motion. Neither is a proxy for the other, and their difference is a
measurement in its own right.

Both are computed on the analysis ensemble defined at the close of
Sec.~\ref{sec:pipelinemap} and characterised in Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}:
\num{\StatMsdParaFlights} paraglider and \num{\StatMsdHangFlights} hang-glider flights,
\num{\StatMsdParaSegments} and \num{\StatMsdHangSegments} retained segments. Every flight
enters at the elapsed times its retained segments cover, with no re-zeroing at a split
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}); the time average is taken inside a segment and never across the
gap that ends one. A flight contributes to a lag only when it holds a fix within half its
own native interval of it, so a slow logger says nothing about a lag below its own cadence
rather than answering with its fix at $t=0$. Figure~\ref{fig:msd} shows both estimators.

\paragraph{The residual each fit leaves.}
Least squares returns a slope whatever the curve underneath it does, so each fit is
reported with the residual it leaves. Over the range each estimator is fitted on, the
ensemble curve departs from its own fitted power law by
\SI{\StatAuditParaEaResidPct}{\percent} rms for paragliders and
\SI{\StatAuditHangEaResidPct}{\percent} for hang gliders; the time-averaged curve departs
from its own by \SI{\StatAuditParaTaResidPct}{\percent} and
\SI{\StatAuditHangTaResidPct}{\percent}.

The size of a residual is half of what decides whether an exponent describes a curve. The
other half is whether it exceeds the noise on the points, and here it does by a wide
margin. Each lag's value is a mean over the flights covering it, so it carries a standard
error that the stored inter-decile band and contributing count give directly. Over the
fitted range the residual is \num{\StatAuditParaEaResidNoiseRatio} times that error on the
paraglider ensemble curve and \num{\StatAuditHangEaResidNoiseRatio} times on the
hang-glider one, against \num{\StatAuditParaTaResidNoiseRatio} and
\num{\StatAuditHangTaResidNoiseRatio} on the two time averages. A departure from a fitted
line that exceeds the error on each point many times over is not scatter about a power
law; it is a shape. Those ratios are the evidence that these curves are not power laws.

The arrangement of the residuals is an arch. The ensemble curve leaves
\num{\StatAuditParaEaResidLags} of them in \num{\StatAuditParaEaResidRuns} sign runs where
independent signs would give \num{\StatAuditParaEaResidRunsExpected}: they go negative,
positive and negative once, which is a bend with the fitted line drawn as a chord across
it. That count is a description of the arrangement and not a test. A Wald--Wolfowitz $z$
presumes independent signs, and every lag of these curves averages the same flights, which
inflates the rejection rate of that rule on an exact power law to most of its draws
\impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{The shape of the ensemble curve.}
The shape is definite and reproducible. The local slope falls to a minimum of
\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMin\ near \SI{\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMinAt}{\second}, rises to
\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMax\ near \SI{\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second}, and settles back
towards $2$ beyond it; the hang gliders do the same between \StatAuditHangEaSlopeMin\ and
\StatAuditHangEaSlopeMax. The maximum is the informative part, because it lies \emph{above}
the ballistic value. No sum of power laws with positive coefficients and exponents at or
below $2$ can produce a local slope above $2$: for $f=\sum_i a_i t^{\alpha_i}$ with
$a_i>0$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}\log f}{\mathrm{d}\log t}
  =\frac{\sum_i a_i\alpha_i t^{\alpha_i}}{\sum_i a_i t^{\alpha_i}}
  \label{eq:weighted-slope}
\end{equation}
is a weighted mean of the $\alpha_i$ and cannot exceed the largest of them. A slope above
$2$ requires the ballistic component to switch on at a finite time rather than to be
present from $t=0$.

The arch is larger than the estimator's own uncertainty. The local slope is not a
difference between neighbouring lags but a Theil--Sen regression over a window, reported
with the scatter of that window about its own line; over the fitted range that scatter
sits well below the lag-to-lag variation of the slope itself \impldetails{impl:global}. The
curve moves more between neighbouring lags than the noise inside a window accounts for.

\paragraph{Three controls on that shape.}
Three mechanisms would produce the same shape without any of it belonging to the motion,
and each is measured rather than argued away.

The first is the coverage rule meeting a changing population: the ensemble behind
$\mathrm{MSD}(t)$ grows as slow loggers reach their own cadence and shrinks as flights
land, and an average taken over a drifting population can bend. Splitting the archive by
native cadence removes the drift, since within one cadence class every flight answers every
lag above that cadence. Across the \StatAuditParaCadenceCohorts\ paraglider classes the
local slope at a given lag spreads by \StatAuditParaCadenceSpread, against the
\StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing\ the shape itself covers. The hang-glider classes are far
smaller and their spread, \StatAuditHangCadenceSpread\ against a swing of
\StatAuditHangEaSlopeSwing, settles nothing; that archive's version of this control is the
next one, whose cohorts are larger.

The second is survivorship: flights that last longer are not a random sample, and if they
also travel faster the curve steepens for a reason that is not motion. Fixed-duration
cohorts control it, since within a cohort of flights lasting at least $T$ every member is
present at every lag up to $T$. Inside a cohort the same minimum and the same overshoot are
there, and the local slope spreads across cohorts by \StatAuditParaDurationSpread\ and
\StatAuditHangDurationSpread. A synthetic ensemble in which every flight is exactly
Brownian and only the \emph{speed} is correlated with the duration reproduces neither
feature: its pooled exponent is biased upwards while every cohort of it recovers the
Brownian value \impldetails{impl:global}.

The third is a heading shared across the ensemble, which would make
$\langle|\mathbf{r}(t)|^2\rangle$ grow ballistically for a trivial reason. It is bounded by
the part of the mean square the mean vector carries,
$|\langle\mathbf{r}(t)\rangle|^2/\langle|\mathbf{r}(t)|^2\rangle$, which over the fitted
range never exceeds \SI{\StatAuditParaDriftPct}{\percent} for paragliders and
\SI{\StatAuditHangDriftPct}{\percent} for hang gliders. The flights do not share a heading.
They share a starting point.

\paragraph{The origin of the crossover.}
A shared starting point is enough to produce it. A wing does not leave its launch site
immediately: it climbs out, often circling close to it, and only then commits to a course.
Until it does, its distance from take-off is bounded by the size of the local soaring area
however fast it is flying, which is what depresses the local slope at intermediate lags;
once the population has committed, the distance grows with the flown speed, which is what
returns the slope towards $2$. Because the commitment happens at a spread of times rather
than at $t=0$, the transition overshoots.

The time at which it happens is measured rather than fitted. Taking the first crossing of
\SI{\StatAuditParaDepartureRadiusKm}{\kilo\metre} from take-off as the departure onto the
cross-country leg --- well beyond a thermalling excursion, well inside the median flown
path of \SI{\StatPipeParaMedianPathKm}{\kilo\metre} ---
\SI{\StatAuditParaDepartureLeavePct}{\percent} of paraglider flights depart, with a median
departure time of \SI{\StatAuditParaDepartureMedianS}{\second} and an interquartile range
of \SIrange{\StatAuditParaDepartureIqrLowS}{\StatAuditParaDepartureIqrHighS}{\second},
against a slope peak at \SI{\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second}. For hang gliders
\SI{\StatAuditHangDepartureLeavePct}{\percent} depart, at a median of
\SI{\StatAuditHangDepartureMedianS}{\second} against a peak at
\SI{\StatAuditHangEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second}. In both disciplines the overshoot sits on the
scale of the departure and slightly before it, which is where a transition driven by a
spread of onset times should sit, the slope being steepest while most of the population is
still crossing. The radius is chosen rather than fitted, so the agreement corroborates the
account and does not measure it.

Two consequences follow. The ensemble MSD about take-off is a statement about the geometry
of the flight relative to its launch site as much as about the transport, and over the
decade where the overshoot sits it is mostly the former. And an exponent fitted across that
crossover is a straight line through a bend: over
\SIrange{\StatMsdParaFitMinS}{\StatMsdParaFitMaxS}{\second} least squares returns
$\alpha_{\mathrm{EA}}=\StatMsdParaAlpha$ and $\StatMsdHangAlpha$, and that is what a chord
across a bend gives rather than a growth law the motion obeys.

\paragraph{The same displacement, averaged over starting times.}
Panel~(b) is Eq.~\eqref{eq:ta-msd} on the same ensemble. Over
\SIrange{\StatMsdTaParaFitMinS}{\StatMsdTaParaFitMaxS}{\second} and
\SIrange{\StatMsdTaHangFitMinS}{\StatMsdTaHangFitMaxS}{\second} it gives
$\alpha_{\mathrm{TA}}=\StatMsdTaParaAlpha$ ($H=\StatMsdTaParaHurst$) for paragliders and
$\StatMsdTaHangAlpha$ ($H=\StatMsdTaHangHurst$) for hang gliders, both well above the
Brownian $\alpha=1$ and below the ballistic $\alpha=2$. The residual it leaves is the
smaller one quoted above and its local slope covers \StatAuditParaTaSlopeSwing\ and
\StatAuditHangTaSlopeSwing, against \StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing\ and
\StatAuditHangEaSlopeSwing\ on the ensemble curves. That is enough to make an exponent a
useful summary and not enough to make it a description, so both figures are reported as
\emph{effective} exponents over a stated range.

The arch is absent. The ensemble slope peaks at an interior lag and falls back; the
time-averaged slope reaches its minimum at \SI{\StatAuditParaTaSlopeMinAt}{\second} and
\SI{\StatAuditHangTaSlopeMinAt}{\second} and rises from there to the end of its range,
with no interior maximum at all. Since the two are the same statistic on the same
ensemble, differing only in what they average over, a feature that survives one average and
not the other belongs to what distinguishes them. The crossover is a property of the common
origin, and its absence from panel~(b) is what identifies it rather than a defect of either
estimator.

\paragraph{The gap between the two averages.}
Fitted over their respective ranges the ensemble slope exceeds the time-averaged exponent
by \num{\StatMsdParaAlphaGap} for paragliders and \num{\StatMsdHangAlphaGap} for hang
gliders, and a systematic gap of that kind is one of the signatures of a non-ergodic,
aging process~\cite{he2008}. It is not evidence of one here, and this observable as
computed cannot be made into evidence of one: reading a gap between two exponents
presupposes that both are exponents, and the ensemble figure is a chord across a curve
whose local slope covers \StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing, so changing the fitted range moves it
by more than the gap. What the measurement supports is weaker --- displacement measured
\emph{from take-off} grows faster, over the upper part of the fitted range, than
displacement over a window of the same duration placed anywhere in the flight --- and
separating aging from launch geometry needs the phase decomposition of
Sec.~\ref{sec:segmentation} and the ergodicity-breaking parameter of
Appendix~\ref{app:ctrw}, which compares the \emph{distribution} of $\overline{\delta^2}$
across flights rather than two fitted slopes and so needs neither curve to be a power law.

\paragraph{What the two estimators return.}
The two estimators return two results of different kinds. From the time average, an
effective exponent, on a curve whose departure from a single power law is small in size and
systematic in arrangement. From the ensemble average, a timescale and not an exponent:
displacement from take-off crosses over near \SI{\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second} for
paragliders and \SI{\StatAuditHangEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second} for hang gliders, where the
population leaves its launch area, and no single power law spans that crossover. The
mean-squared displacement about the origin, which is the quantity the anomalous-transport
question is normally put in, does not deliver an exponent on this archive.

Neither figure carries an interval. A bootstrap over flights would treat the archive's
records as independent, which they are not, and it would report the sampling term alone,
where on the estimator for which both have been measured the movement of the exponent with
the fitted range is far the larger. A curve that is a systematic arch has, in any case, no
single exponent for a sampling error to be an error \emph{of}. The resampling unit and the
range dependence are both measured below, on the estimator that replaces this one, and the
exponents that carry an interval in this chapter are that estimator's.

\paragraph{The two ends of the range.}
Below the foot of every fit range the curves carry two artefacts, both measured. The first
is the coverage rule meeting a lag grid of consecutive integers. Each flight's lag is
measured from its own first fix, so a class of loggers of interval $\Delta t$ answers every
lag with a fix at a multiple of $\Delta t$: at a lag that is not one, every flight of the
class is off in the same direction by up to $\Delta t/2$, and the error alternates with the
lag rather than averaging out. Splitting by cadence measures it --- \SI{2}{\second} loggers
alternate by \SI{\StatAuditParaCadenceTwoSecAlternationPct}{\percent} between even and odd
lags and the mixture by \SI{\StatAuditParaCadenceMixedAlternationPct}{\percent}, while
\SI{1}{\hertz} loggers, which meet every integer lag exactly, return
\SI{\StatAuditParaCadenceOneSecAlternationPct}{\percent}. That last figure is the control
on the measurement rather than a result of it: a class that meets the grid exactly should
show no alternation, and it returns a value smaller than the affected class by a factor of
thirty.

The second is the smoothing evaluated off-centre at a segment's ends, which the table flags
\path{edge}. Those samples are \SI{\StatEdgeParaSamplePct}{\percent} of the fixes and the
ensemble estimator, unlike the dataset verifier, does not qualify by the flag. Recomputing
the curve without them on \num{\StatEdgeParaFlights} flights moves it by at most
\SI{\StatEdgeParaShortPct}{\percent} below \SI{\StatEdgeParaShortLagS}{\second} and by at
most \SI{\StatEdgeParaLongPct}{\percent} above \SI{\StatEdgeParaLongLagS}{\second} \impldetails{impl:global}: two orders of magnitude
between the two regions, which is what a defect of the segment ends should look like, since
the first samples of a record are exactly the flagged ones. Nothing below
\SI{\StatMsdParaFitMinS}{\second} in Figure~\ref{fig:msd} is read as motion.

At the other end the ceiling is set by coverage. The ensemble holds
\num{\StatAuditParaFlightsFitLag} flights at
\SI{\StatAuditParaFlightsFitLagS}{\second} and
\num{\StatAuditParaFlightsWideLag} at \SI{\StatAuditParaFlightsWideLagS}{\second}, so past
the fitted range the average is a statistic over a survivorship-selected minority, which is
why each fit stops well inside the plotted range. One limitation belongs to the ensemble
estimator alone. A segment keeps its parent's origin (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), so a
boundary at a re-acquisition offset leaves an unknown instrument constant in every
subsequent absolute position; it enters $\langle|\mathbf{r}(t)|^2\rangle$ and cancels out
of $\overline{\delta^2(\tau)}$, and it is carried by \SI{\StatVerifyParaOffsetPct}{\percent}
of paraglider and \SI{\StatVerifyHangOffsetPct}{\percent} of hang-glider flights
\impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{Three contaminants of a displacement statistic.}
Three things contaminate a displacement statistic on this archive, and one estimator
disposes of all three.

The first is the common origin, measured above: over the decade where the crossover sits,
$\langle|\mathbf{r}(t)|^2\rangle$ is governed by where flights start rather than by how
they move.

The second is each flight's own course. A wing that crosses country has a mean velocity,
and a mean velocity contributes $|\mathbf{v}_d|^2\Delta^2$ to any mean square displacement
whatever the underlying process. Here it does not simply inflate: the CFD scores closed
courses and \SI{\StatVarParaClosedPct}{\percent} of retained paraglider flights fly a
triangle, which returns towards its start, so the displacement saturates at lags set by the
scoring rule rather than by the air.

The third is the population. Section~\ref{sec:prelim} measured the strata of this archive
as incompatible on the ensemble MSD, by
\SI{\StatPrelimParaClassTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} across wing classes and
\SI{\StatPrelimParaGroupTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} across orographic groups. A
superposition of ordinary processes with different persistence times imitates a single
anomalous one, which is the most economical way a measurement of this kind can be wrong.

None of the three is repaired by subtracting an estimate of it. That is the specification
the next section meets, by filtering.
\end{revblock}

\section{The filtered variation}
\label{sec:variations}
\begin{revblock}
The displacement statistic leaves a specification: a quantity measured from no common
origin, requiring no estimate of the flown course, and not turned into a single anomalous
law by a mixture of populations. The first two are met by construction, the third by
measurement in the section that follows.

\paragraph{Filtering rather than subtracting.}
The course can be subtracted rather than filtered, and subtraction fails on this archive in
a way that is measured rather than argued. A wing that crosses country has a mean velocity
$\mathbf{v}_d$, and a mean velocity contributes $|\mathbf{v}_d|^2\Delta^2$ to any mean
square displacement whatever the process underneath it, so removing it looks like a
precondition for reading an exponent at all. Removing it means estimating it. The natural
estimate $[\mathbf{r}(T)-\mathbf{r}(0)]/T$ is built from the record's own endpoints, and
subtracting it turns the trajectory into a bridge whose increments must sum to zero by
construction. On synthetic fractional Brownian motion of known exponent the operation
returns a value well below the truth, and there is no lag at which the distortion is
absent: the centred variation already sits below the raw one at the shortest lags of a
record and falls far below it towards the record's own length \impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{The estimator.}
A finite difference removes a trend without estimating it, because it annihilates the trend
identically. Writing $A_p$ for the difference of order $p$ taken at spacing $\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
  A_p\,\mathbf{r}(t;\Delta)
  =\sum_{k=0}^{p}(-1)^{k}\binom{p}{k}\,\mathbf{r}\bigl(t+(p-k)\Delta\bigr),
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
the operator vanishes identically on every polynomial trajectory of degree $p-1$, and the
\emph{filtered variation} is its mean square,
\begin{equation}
  V_p(\Delta)=\Bigl\langle\,\bigl|A_p\,\mathbf{r}(t;\Delta)\bigr|^{2}\Bigr\rangle
  \;\sim\;\Delta^{\alpha_p},
  \label{eq:variation}
\end{equation}
the average running over every admissible $t$ inside a segment and over every flight, and
the modulus taken on the two horizontal components of one vector rather than by pooling the
two marginals, which Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} forbids. Summing the components leaves $V_p$
invariant under a rotation of the frame, so an anisotropic ensemble does not return a
different exponent for having been measured with north somewhere else. For a self-similar
process with stationary increments $\alpha_p=2H$ at every order, with only the prefactor
depending on $p$. What differs between orders is what each removes before measuring: $p=1$
is the plain increment and is blind to the origin, $p=2$ to a constant velocity, $p=3$ to a
constant acceleration. No drift is estimated at any order, so none can be estimated badly.
The order is not free, in that a difference of order $p$ spans $p\Delta$ of record: a
segment answers at a lag only when it is longer than that, so each order costs coverage at
the long end of the grid, and the scan stops at $p=3$.

The scan over orders is a measurement and not a check on one. The difference
$\alpha_1-\alpha_2$ is the part of the apparent exponent that was the flown course, read off
directly rather than modelled, and the agreement between $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_3$ is the
statement that nothing polynomial is left for a higher difference to find. On synthetic data
with a constant course imposed on a process of known exponent, order~1 returns a value
displaced from the truth and orders~2 and~3 both recover it \impldetails{impl:global}.

Everything below is computed inside a retained segment, never across the gap that ends one
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), and never on displacement from take-off. The pass keeps one curve
per flight, so every stratification of this section and of the next is a row selection
rather than a further traversal of the fix table \impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{The fitted range.}
\SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, which is
\StatVarParaFitDecades\ decades, declared in advance and used for both disciplines and all
three orders. Both ends are set by the system rather than chosen to suit the curve. Below,
the Savitzky--Golay window has a floor of \num{\PreprocSavgolMinWindow} samples
(Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}), so a trajectory has been made smooth over a window of
\num{\PreprocSavgolMinWindow} of its own logger's sampling intervals, and
\SI{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\second} sits above that window for the slowest logger retained.
Above, the declared task takes over: the scoring rule rewards a closed course, a closed
course comes home, and it comes home at a time set by the task rather than by the air. Every
exponent below is quoted with the range it was fitted over.

The fit is ordinary least squares on $\log V_p$ against $\log\Delta$ across the lags of that
range, applied to the mean of the per-flight curves rather than to per-flight exponents
averaged afterwards. A flight therefore contributes to every lag its segments cover, and no
flight contributes an exponent of its own.

\paragraph{The order scan.}
Table~\ref{tab:orderscan} gives the scan and Figure~\ref{fig:transport} the curves behind
it. At second order the archive returns
$\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ for paragliders and
$\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo\pm\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErr$ for hang gliders, over
\SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second} in both. The two disciplines
agree inside their intervals, which was not guaranteed, since they differ in speed, in glide
ratio and in where they fly.

Figure~\ref{fig:transport} carries the local slope of $V_2$ and that of the ensemble MSD on
one pair of axes, over the lags both cover. The arch that dominates the ensemble curve near
\SI{\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second}, and that carries its local slope through a swing
of \StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing\ for paragliders and \StatAuditHangEaSlopeSwing\ for hang
gliders, is three times smaller in the filtered variation and of the opposite sign. The
local slope of $V_2$ swings by \StatVarParaSlopeLocalSwing\ and
\StatVarHangSlopeLocalSwing\ inside the fitted range, and it turns twice: a maximum of
\StatVarParaSlopeLocalMax\ near \SI{\StatVarParaSlopeLocalMaxAtS}{\second}, a minimum of
\StatVarParaSlopeLocalMin\ at \SI{\StatVarParaSlopeLocalMinAtS}{\second}, and a rise
after it. Where the ensemble curve is an arch the filtered variation is a dip, so the two
are not the same structure attenuated but different structures --- which is what the removal
of the common origin and the flown course was supposed to achieve. How large that swing is,
in exponent units, is the range dependence reported in the next section.

\paragraph{Removing the course raises the exponent.}
$\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ differ by $\StatVarParaDriftShare$ for paragliders and
$\StatVarHangDriftShare$ for hang gliders, and the sign is the opposite of the one a
residual drift would leave. A drift inflates a displacement statistic; on this archive the
declared course \emph{suppresses} it. The CFD scores closed tasks, and
\SI{\StatVarParaClosedPct}{\percent} of retained paraglider flights and
\SI{\StatVarHangClosedPct}{\percent} of hang-glider flights fly a triangle. A triangle
returns towards its start, so displacement saturates by construction, and it saturates at
lags set by the task rather than by the air. The plain increment sees that and reads an
exponent that is too low; the second-order variation does not see it at all.

The reading is confirmed where it can be tested directly rather than inferred from the
scan. Fitting closed and open tasks separately, the gap in the exponent, taken as open minus
closed, is $\StatVarParaTaskGapOrderOne$ at order~1 for paragliders and
$\StatVarHangTaskGapOrderOne$ for hang gliders --- closed tasks reading lower, which is what
a course that comes home requires --- and it reverses to $\StatVarParaTaskGapOrderTwo$ and
$\StatVarHangTaskGapOrderTwo$ at order~2. Inside the declared range the two orders separate
the two populations by comparable amounts, and what discriminates between them is the
behaviour of each gap when the range is extended past its ceiling: the order-1 gap grows
substantially and the order-2 gap does not move. A difference of curvature does not care
where the course ends.

The scan therefore fixes the sign of a comparison made later in this chapter and by anyone
comparing an exponent measured elsewhere with the one measured here. An exponent read off
displacement with no trend removed is, on this archive, an exponent read partly off the
scoring rule, and it is expected to sit below one read with the course filtered out, by the
difference between the two orders: $\StatVarParaDriftShare$ for paragliders and
$\StatVarHangDriftShare$ for hang gliders.

\paragraph{Orders two and three agree.}
By \StatVarParaOrderAgreement\ for paragliders and \StatVarHangOrderAgreement\ for hang
gliders, well inside the interval either exponent carries. Nothing polynomial survives the
second difference for the third to remove, so the exponent is not the residue of a trend of
any degree.

\paragraph{Order one reproduces the time-averaged MSD.}
It should: the first-order filtered variation \emph{is} $\overline{\delta^2(\Delta)}$ under
another name. The comparison is a check on two implementations and not on the physics, and
it is worth making because the two were written separately, traverse the fix table
separately, and are fitted over different ranges ---
\SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second} here against
\SIrange{\StatMsdTaParaFitMinS}{\StatMsdTaParaFitMaxS}{\second} for the time average. They
return $\alpha_1=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOne$ against
$\alpha_{\mathrm{TA}}=\StatMsdTaParaAlpha$ for paragliders and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderOne\
against \StatMsdTaHangAlpha\ for hang gliders: closer, in both disciplines, than either
moves when its own range is halved.

\paragraph{What an exponent near two does not mean.}
$\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ is $2H$ with $H=\StatVarParaHurstOrderTwo$, at the upper
boundary of the self-similar family and marginally past it. The reading that suggests itself
is that the flight has gone locally straight over the range, and the estimator settles it
against two calibrations of its own.

A plain increment cannot report above the ballistic value on any process with stationary
increments: the displacement over $n$ steps is a sum of $n$ increments, Minkowski's
inequality bounds its norm by the sum of theirs, and $V_1(n\Delta)\le n^2V_1(\Delta)$
follows, so $\alpha_1\le2$ whatever the motion does. An estimator sitting at its own ceiling
says nothing, and the second-order variation has no ceiling at that value. On a
differentiable trajectory
$A_2\,\mathbf{r}\simeq\ddot{\mathbf{r}}\,\Delta^2$, so $V_2\sim\Delta^{4}$: the second-order
variation has room to report twice the ballistic value, and it reports it when the motion is
smooth. Run on a persistent random walk over lags well inside its own correlation time,
where the trajectory is differentiable at every lag it is read at, the same code returns an
exponent between the ballistic value and that limit; run on a straight line it vanishes
identically, the second difference annihilating a linear path exactly.

Read against that pair, $\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ and $\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo$ say
that the increments stay correlated at every lag of the range and that the trajectory is
rough at every lag of it. The wing turns at every scale the estimator reads, with a bias in
the turning that has not decayed by the top of the range; it is persistent, and it is not
straight. A Hurst exponent above unity has no meaning for an exactly self-similar process
with stationary increments, and the figure is quoted as an effective exponent over a
declared window for that reason: how far the archive is from exact self-similarity is
measured later in the chapter, on the distribution of the increments rather than on its
second moment.

What the interval attached to each exponent is made of, and what the exponent does when the
ensemble is cut by cadence, by wing class, by season and by declared task, is measured next.
\end{revblock}

\section{The uncertainty on the exponent}
\label{sec:transport-measure}
\begin{revblock}
The archive holds \num{\StatMsdParaFlights} paraglider and \num{\StatMsdHangFlights}
hang-glider flights, and \num{\StatVarParaClusters} and \num{\StatVarHangClusters} occasions
on which one site was flown on one day. An interval on $\alpha_2$ built on the first pair of
counts is not an interval built on the second, so which of them carries the independent
measurements has to be settled before either is quoted.

\paragraph{The resampling unit.}
The quantity clustered is one number per flight: the logarithm of that flight's own
first-order variation at a lag in the middle of the grid, which is the per-flight input every
fit in this chapter averages over. Its intraclass correlation at a given level is the share of
its variance sitting \emph{between} clusters rather than within them. Near zero the clustering
is irrelevant and flights may be resampled directly; near one a cluster is effectively a single
measurement however many flights it holds.

For paragliders the ladder reads \StatVarParaIccFlight\ at the flight, \StatVarParaIccDay\ at
the calendar day, \StatVarParaIccPilot\ at the pilot and \StatVarParaIccDaySite\ at the day and
site; for hang gliders \StatVarHangIccDay, \StatVarHangIccPilot\ and \StatVarHangIccDaySite.
The first entry is not a measurement. Taking each record as its own cluster gives zero by
construction, and it is in the ladder because it is the assumption a per-flight bootstrap
makes. The ordering of the other three is the physical one. A date on its own pools every site
in the country flying that day, and most of what two flights share washes out of it. A pilot
carries a wing and a home site, which is worth more, and worth more again on the smaller
archive, where a pilot returns to it more often. The air over one site on one day is what two
flights actually have in common, and it is the level at which the coefficient is largest in
both disciplines. Every bootstrap in this chapter therefore resamples days and sites, of which
the archive holds \num{\StatVarParaClusters} and \num{\StatVarHangClusters}.

The correction it buys is in the expected direction and is small. Resampling flights returns
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOneErrNaive\ on the paraglider $\alpha_1$ against
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOneErrSampling\ from the clustered resample, and
\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErrNaive\ against \StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErrSampling\ on the
hang-glider $\alpha_2$. Both figures sit below the last digit of the exponent they qualify.
The ladder settles which of the two applies; neither of them is what the interval is made of.

\paragraph{The sampling term and the range term.}
The two are reported separately. On $\alpha_2$ the day-and-site bootstrap returns
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrSampling\ for paragliders and
\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErrSampling\ for hang gliders. Refitting each half of the fitted
window and halving the difference between the two exponents returns
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrRange\ and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErrRange. The sampling term is
negligible against the range term in both disciplines, and the quoted
$\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ and $\pm\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErr$ are the two combined in
quadrature, which is the range term with the sampling term invisible inside it.

The same construction at the other orders gives \StatVarParaAlphaOrderOneErrRange\ at order one
and \StatVarParaAlphaOrderThreeErrRange\ at order three, so the systematic grows with the order
of the filter, as a higher difference of a noisier curve should. Nothing here turns on the
third decimal of any of them. They are quoted to say that the second decimal of the exponent is
not determined, and a number carrying the bootstrap alone would claim four significant figures
on a curve that does not have two.

\paragraph{What the least-squares error measures.}
A third figure is available and measures something else. The standard error that least squares
returns on the fitted slope is \StatMsdTaParaAlphaErrOls\ on the paraglider time-averaged
displacement, \StatMsdParaAlphaErrOls\ on the paraglider ensemble fit and
\StatMsdHangAlphaErrOls\ on the hang-glider one. It asks how well a \emph{straight line}
describes the fitted points, and it treats each residual as an independent draw from a common
noise. The residuals of these curves are neither. They are not independent, because every lag
is an average over the same flights, which is what the effective count below puts at
\StatVarParaDof\ points; and they are not noise, because the local slope drifts across the
fitted decades. So the least-squares figure is dominated by the curve's departure from a power
law, which is why it is larger on the ensemble fit than on the time-averaged one and tracks the
residuals rather than the sample size. Which of it and the bootstrap comes out larger depends
on the size of the ensemble, and a synthetic ensemble separates the two
\impldetails{impl:global}.

Neither candidate interval is an interval on the displacement exponents quoted earlier in this
chapter. The bootstrap over flights treats \num{\StatMsdParaFlights} records as independent,
which the ladder above refuses, and returns \StatMsdParaAlphaErr\ for a curve that is not a
power law; the least-squares figure measures the arch rather than the sampling. On a curve
whose local slope covers \StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing\ across the range it is fitted on there is
no exponent for either to be an error \emph{of}, and attaching a $\pm$ would assert that there
is one.

\paragraph{What sets the two ends of the window.}
Both ends come from the system rather than from the fit. Below, the smoothing of
Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol} has a window floor of \num{\PreprocSavgolMinWindow} samples, so the scale
over which a trajectory has been made smooth is that many intervals of the logger that recorded
it, and it is longest for the slowest loggers retained. The floor of the fitted range,
\SI{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\second}, sits above that scale for every cadence in the archive;
nothing under it is read as motion.

Above, the declared task takes over. The CFD scores closed courses, and
\SI{\StatVarParaClosedPct}{\percent} of retained paraglider flights and
\SI{\StatVarHangClosedPct}{\percent} of hang-glider flights fly one; a course that comes home
suppresses any displacement statistic at lags set by the task rather than by the air. Splitting
the ensemble on it and comparing local slopes measures where that begins to matter
(Figure~\ref{fig:transport}). Closed and open courses separate by
\num{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapLow} in the local slope at
\SI{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapLowS}{\second} and by \num{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapHigh} at
\SI{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapHighS}{\second}, the hang gliders from
\num{\StatVarHangTaskSlopeGapLow} to \num{\StatVarHangTaskSlopeGapHigh} over the same lags. The
separation grows smoothly, so there is no lag above which the measurement becomes clean, and
any threshold placed on it picks a point on a smooth curve at a place that moves with the
sample size. The ceiling of the fitted range, \SI{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, is set where
the separation is still a small fraction of what it reaches, and the window that survives both
ends is \StatVarParaFitDecades\ decades wide.

\paragraph{Four cuts of the ensemble.}
Four stratifications ask whether the exponent belongs to the motion or to who is in the
ensemble. The pass keeps one curve per flight, so each is a row selection on a matrix already
in memory rather than another traversal of the fix table \impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{The task stratum and the range dependence are one term.}
The task gap at order two, $\StatVarParaTaskGapOrderTwo$, and the range dependence,
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrRange, are the same size. They are plausibly the same effect, and
therefore one term rather than two to be added.

Both measure how much of the fitted exponent is fixed by the long end of the window. The range
term is what the exponent does when the window is halved and each half fitted alone. The task
gap is what it does when the ensemble is split on the one attribute whose influence is confined
to the long end: the local slopes of the two task populations are separated by
\num{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapLow} at the bottom of the fitted range and diverge monotonically
above it. A systematic sourced where the scoring rule governs the trajectory is seen by a shift
of the window and by the split alike, and its coming out the same size in the two is what a
single term predicts. The signs are not comparable, one figure being a difference between two
populations and the other a half-difference between two halves of a window; only the magnitudes
are set against each other.

The quoted $\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ already carries this systematic through its range
half, so entering the task stratum as a fifth robustness term and adding it in quadrature would
inflate the interval by counting the scoring rule twice. For hang gliders the same two figures
are $\StatVarHangTaskGapOrderTwo$ and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErrRange, of the same order and
not of the same size, so the identification is weaker on that archive and the interval there is
left as the range term alone.

One measurement would settle it and is not made: the range dependence inside the open-course
population by itself. If the scoring rule is the whole of the systematic, that population's
exponent is the one that should not move when the window does. It is a row selection on curves
already stored.

\paragraph{How many exponents the range supports.}
The same question is put at its limit by fitting a continuous piecewise-linear curve to
$\log V_2$ against $\log\Delta$, with the number of breaks chosen by an information criterion.
The positions are searched exhaustively on the lag grid, so there is no local optimum to fall
into, and the penalty counts the effective rather than the nominal number of points, so a curve
whose residuals are correlated cannot buy a break with points it does not independently have.

Selection returns \StatVarParaBreaks\ break for paragliders, at
\SI{\StatVarParaBreakOneS}{\second}, with a slope of \StatVarParaSlopeLow\ below it and
\StatVarParaSlopeHigh\ above; the two bracket the single-exponent value
$\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$, and their separation is of the order of the range
dependence, which is the same curvature seen as a shape instead of as an interval. Hang gliders
take \StatVarHangBreaks\ breaks. A break here would be a genuine change of regime in the
transport rather than the crossover of the ensemble curve under another name, that one being
fixed at an elapsed time by a common origin this estimator does not have.

The archive cannot declare one. Run on surrogates that are a single power law plus the measured
sampling noise --- \StatVarParaNoiseSigmaDex\ dex per lag for paragliders and
\StatVarHangNoiseSigmaDex\ for hang gliders, carrying the measured lag-to-lag correlation ---
the same procedure returns a break \SI{\StatVarParaFalsePositivePct}{\percent} and
\SI{\StatVarHangFalsePositivePct}{\percent} of the time \impldetails{impl:global}. A rule that
invents a break that often on a curve that has none cannot be quoted as having found one, and
neither count is established.

The reason is the resolution of the data rather than the fitting rule. The residuals of any fit
to these curves are correlated across lags, because every lag is an average over the same
flights, and the usual first-order correction puts \num{\StatVarParaFitLags} lags at no more
than \StatVarParaDof\ independent points, which is the floor the estimator will report and is
reached in both disciplines. On that budget one break sits at the edge of what is estimable and
two are past it. What the range supports is one effective exponent over a declared window,
carrying an interval set by where that window ends.
\end{revblock}

\section{The exponent from the increment quantiles}
\label{sec:transport-propagator}
\begin{revblock}
Every exponent quoted so far is a second moment of a displacement, and this archive has
already shown what a second moment can measure instead of the motion: averaged about a
common origin, it returned the geometry of the launch site. What is wanted is a route to
the exponent that uses neither a moment nor a common origin. The first-passage time was
tried for that role and withdrawn.

\paragraph{First-passage times, tried and withdrawn.}
The first-passage time $T(L)$ is the first time a flight is a distance $L$ from take-off,
and for a self-similar process $\langle T(L)\rangle\sim L^{1/H}$, so the slope of
$\log\langle T\rangle$ against $\log L$ gives the exponent in the space domain with no
moment fitted over lags~\cite{redner2001}. The audit arrays hold each flight's position at
every lag, so it costs no further traversal of the fix table, and it was computed as a
cross-check on $\alpha_2$.

Read naively it returns an exponent above the ballistic value, which no process with
stationary increments can give. The cause is right censoring: at the larger radii only a
minority of paraglider flights arrive, the ones that do not are the slow ones, so a median
taken over arrivals alone is biased down and the fitted slope with it. Treating non-arrival
as censored and taking a Kaplan--Meier median instead removes the bias, and returns values
for both disciplines inside the paraglider interval
$\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ they were meant to
test. Refitting over sub-ranges of radius then moves the paraglider value across a spread
several times that interval \impldetails{impl:global}.

The diagnosis is the ensemble average's, transposed. $T(L)$ is measured from take-off, so
it carries the same origin into the space domain: at small $L$ the wing is still climbing
out inside its launch area, and at large $L$ the censored median extrapolates past the
duration of most flights. It is the crossover of Figure~\ref{fig:msd} read along the space
axis instead of the time axis, and it is not an independent check on anything. It is
withdrawn. The failure survives the change of axis, which places it on the common origin
the two estimators share rather than on the elapsed-time axis only one of them uses.

\paragraph{The propagator of the increments.}
Write $\Delta x(t_0,\Delta)=x(t_0+\Delta)-x(t_0)$ for the increment of one horizontal
component over a lag $\Delta$, taken inside a retained segment and never across the gap
that ends one (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). Pooled over starting times and over flights, its
distribution is the propagator of the increments,
\begin{equation}
  P(x,\Delta)\,\mathrm{d}x=\Pr\!\bigl(\Delta x\in[x,\,x+\mathrm{d}x]\bigr),
\end{equation}
and for a self-similar process with stationary increments it takes the scaling form
\begin{equation}
  P(x,\Delta)=\Delta^{-H}\,F\!\left(\frac{x}{\Delta^{H}}\right),
  \label{eq:increment-scaling}
\end{equation}
where $F$ is a fixed density of unit integral, independent of the lag. It is defined and
estimated on one component: pooling the two would presuppose isotropy, which
Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} rejects on this ensemble.

Equation~\eqref{eq:increment-scaling} makes every quantile of $|\Delta x|$ grow as
$\Delta^{H}$. Writing $q_p(\Delta)$ for the $p$th quantile, the scaling form gives
$q_p(\Delta)=\Delta^{H}q_p(1)$, so $\log q_p$ against $\log\Delta$ is a straight line of
slope $H$ at every $p$, and the four lines are parallel. A quantile is an order statistic:
it is fixed by the middle of the distribution, where every flight contributes, and a heavy
tail moves it not at all. Neither of the two defects of the earlier estimates enters here:
there is no moment, so no small set of large increments can set it, and no absolute
position, so the launch site cannot.

The four levels are the lower quartile, the median, the upper quartile and the ninetieth
percentile. The last is far enough into the flank to move if the shape of $F$ changes with
the lag and far enough from the tail that the largest increments do not set it. The
estimator is one streaming pass keeping a histogram of $|\Delta x|$ per lag, per component
and per native cadence, from which the quantiles are read \impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{What it returns.}
$H=\StatPropParaHurst$ for paragliders and $\StatPropHangHurst$ for hang gliders, over
\SIrange{\StatPropParaFitMinS}{\StatPropParaFitMaxS}{\second}, from
\num{\StatPropParaIncrements} and \num{\StatPropHangIncrements} increments in
\num{\StatPropParaCadences} and \num{\StatPropHangCadences} native cadence classes. Doubled
into the units the filtered variation is quoted in, $\alpha=\StatPropParaAlpha$ and
$\StatPropHangAlpha$.

The smoothing window has a floor of \num{\PreprocSavgolMinWindow} \emph{samples}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}), so the scale below which the pipeline has made the trajectory
smooth is set by the logger's own cadence, and the paraglider classes span a factor of ten
in it. Across them the estimate moves between \StatPropParaHurstMin\ and
\StatPropParaHurstMax. The smoothing scale therefore does not set the estimate.

\paragraph{Three routes, and what they bracket.}
Table~\ref{tab:threeroutes} sets the quantile exponent beside the two second moments. They
bracket it: $H$ lies between \StatVarParaHurstOrderOne\ and \StatVarParaHurstOrderTwo\ for

The gap between the two moments is the flown course, measured by the order scan and read
here from the other side. It also fixes which row the published value is to be compared
against. Vilpellet et al.~\cite{vilpellet2026} report a global $H$ for human soaring flight

the sign of the gap is the one the order scan predicts: the archive's closed tasks suppress
displacement rather than inflate it, because a triangle comes home, so an exponent measured
without removing the course is expected to fall below one measured with it. Which of the
two answers a given question depends on the question, and the reference value is reproduced
by the route that measures the same thing.

The bracket is the result rather than any of its decimals: two second moments taken in
different frames and one statistic that is no moment at all, none of them measured from
take-off.

\paragraph{What the quantiles cost the scaling form.}

histograms test the rest of it: the four quantiles must give the \emph{same} slope. They do
not. Fitted separately, $H$ falls from \StatPropParaHurstBulk\ at the lower quartile and
the median to \StatPropParaHurstFlank\ at the upper quartile and the ninetieth percentile
for paragliders, and from \StatPropHangHurstBulk\ to \StatPropHangHurstFlank\ for hang
gliders (Figure~\ref{fig:propagator}b). The fall runs in the same direction in both
disciplines, on both components and at every cadence, which is what makes it a property of
the increments rather than of one histogram.

Its size is read against a calibration measured the same way. The spread across the four
quantiles is \StatPropParaQuantileSpread\ and \StatPropHangQuantileSpread, against
\StatPropCalibSpread\ on an exactly self-similar process of $H=\StatPropCalibHurst$ fitted
over the same window with less data \impldetails{impl:global}. The collapse says the same
in the other panel: rescaling the histograms by $\Delta^{H}$ leaves
\StatPropParaCollapseDex\ and \StatPropHangCollapseDex\ dex of scatter through the bulk,
with visible separation where the flanks fall away (Figure~\ref{fig:propagator}c).

So the scaling form is not exactly satisfied over the range this chapter reads. The centre
of the increment distribution spreads faster than its flanks, which is a distribution
changing shape with the scale and not a single self-similar one.

That qualifies the exponent and does not refute it. The distance from the bulk value
\StatPropParaHurstBulk\ to the flank value \StatPropParaHurstFlank\ is small beside the
distance of either from the Brownian value, and no reading of these curves puts the
transport near it. It is a second reason, independent of the movement of $\alpha_2$ when
the fitted range is halved, why a single exponent quoted to three figures would claim more
than this archive supports.

\paragraph{The anisotropy reaches the exponent.}
Section~\ref{sec:prelim} measures the two horizontal components as incompatible in
amplitude. They differ in exponent as well: \StatPropParaHurstEast\ east against
\StatPropParaHurstNorth\ north for paragliders, and \StatPropHangHurstEast\ against
\StatPropHangHurstNorth\ for hang gliders, where the separation is the wider. Pooling the
components would average that away. It is why they are kept apart here and in
Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}, and why Eq.~\eqref{eq:increment-scaling} is written for one
component directly rather than derived from a two-dimensional propagator through an
isotropy substitution this ensemble does not support.
\end{revblock}

\section{The archive is not a L\'evy walk}
\label{sec:transport-shape}
\begin{revblock}
An exponent fixes one moment of one distribution, and two processes can agree on it and be
different physics. A correlated Gaussian motion and a L\'evy walk both give
$\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^2\rangle\sim\Delta^{2H}$; what separates them is the rest of the
moment spectrum and the distribution of the durations over which the motion holds a
heading. Both are measured on the same unsegmented trajectories, with no estimator in
common, and both refuse the L\'evy walk.

\paragraph{The moment spectrum.}
Write
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}(\Delta)|^{q}\big\rangle\sim\Delta^{\,q\nu(q)}
  \label{eq:moment-spectrum}
\end{equation}
and read $q\nu(q)$ as a function of $q$. A self-similar monofractal process gives a straight
line through the origin with $\nu(q)$ constant: one exponent governs every moment. A L\'evy
walk---flight durations drawn from a power-law tail, each traversed at finite speed---gives a
bilinear one, with a knee at its tail index and a right-hand branch of slope one, the
ballistic front carried by the longest flights. The discrimination is between two shapes
rather than between two fitted values, and it is computed on the whole archive rather than
on a subsample of it.

Over \SIrange{\StatShapeParaFitMinS}{\StatShapeParaFitMaxS}{\second}, on
\num{\StatShapeParaLags} lags and for $q$ from \StatShapeParaQMin\ to \StatShapeParaQMax, the
spectrum is straight (Figure~\ref{fig:shape}a). For paragliders $\nu(q)$ varies by
\StatShapeParaNuSpread\ across the whole range of $q$, between \StatShapeParaNuMin\ and
\StatShapeParaNuMax, about a single fitted $\nu=\StatShapeParaLinearSlope$; for hang gliders
it varies by \StatShapeHangNuSpread\ about $\nu=\StatShapeHangLinearSlope$. The rms departure
of $q\nu(q)$ from a straight line through the origin is \StatShapeParaLinearDeparture\ and
\StatShapeHangLinearDeparture. Neither archive shows strong anomalous diffusion.

Those departures are read against two references, and the two are not the same quantity. The
first is what a L\'evy walk leaves on this lag grid and this range of $q$ when its spectrum
is fitted the same way: its knee is a gross feature of the shape, and the measured
departures sit an order of magnitude below what it produces. The second is the gate at which
the fitting procedure declares a knee at all, set low so that a weak bend is not missed; the
measured departures sit below that as well, by a smaller margin. Both references are stated
in \impldetails{impl:global}, and it is the first that carries the refusal: the archive is
not merely below the threshold of declaration, it is far from the shape the model predicts.

The moments are estimable rather than merely computed. The share of each moment carried by
the largest hundredth of its increments reaches \SI{\StatShapeParaTailMaxPct}{\percent} at
worst for paragliders and \SI{\StatShapeHangTailMaxPct}{\percent} for hang gliders, and
\SI{\StatShapeParaTailAtQMaxPct}{\percent} and \SI{\StatShapeHangTailAtQMaxPct}{\percent} at
the top of the range, $q=\StatShapeParaQMax$ (Figure~\ref{fig:shape}b). A moment more than
half of which is carried by that hundredth is a statement about a few flights rather than an
estimate; none of these is near it.

The L\'evy walk is the model this thesis was framed around. On the whole trajectory,
unsegmented, the archive does not support it, and what the spectrum leaves standing is a
monofractal process: a single exponent governing every moment over the range read.

\paragraph{The spectrum against the quantile spread.}
The previous section fitted the quantiles of $|\Delta x|$ separately and found that they do
not share a slope: $H$ falls from \StatPropParaHurstBulk\ at the lower quartile and the
median to \StatPropParaHurstFlank\ at the upper quartile and the ninetieth percentile, and
the same fall appears for hang gliders. The scaling form is therefore only approximately
satisfied, while Eq.~\eqref{eq:moment-spectrum} has just been reported as straight. The two
are compatible, and what reconciles them is what each averages over. A moment of order $q$
integrates the whole increment distribution, so a local exponent drifting between the centre
and the flanks enters $\nu(q)$ weighted by where that moment takes its mass, and the drift
partly cancels in the integral. A quantile fit reads the same collapse at four fixed
probability levels and averages nothing.

The measured sizes are ordered as that argument requires. The spread across the four
quantiles is \StatPropParaQuantileSpread\ for paragliders and \StatPropHangQuantileSpread\
for hang gliders, against spreads of $\nu(q)$ across the whole range of $q$ of
\StatShapeParaNuSpread\ and \StatShapeHangNuSpread: the quantity that averages is the smaller
in both disciplines. The two are read over ranges sharing a lower end and differing at the
top, \SIrange{\StatPropParaFitMinS}{\StatPropParaFitMaxS}{\second} against
\SIrange{\StatShapeParaFitMinS}{\StatShapeParaFitMaxS}{\second}. What is not established is
the bound: the drift measured at four probability levels has not been propagated through the
moment integral to a predicted variation of $\nu(q)$, so the claim is the ordering and not
that the one accounts for the other. The refusal above is untouched either way, since the
departure a knee would leave exceeds both.

\paragraph{The persistence runs.}
The second route uses no moment. Each flight is decomposed into non-overlapping stretches
whose sinuosity---arc length over chord---stays below a threshold, and the durations of
those stretches are the geometric counterpart of a waiting time, the quantity whose
power-law tail Appendix~\ref{app:ctrw} takes as the input of a continuous-time random walk.
A L\'evy walk ties the tail index $\beta$ of that distribution to the transport exponent, so
the description needs a run length that exists independently of how a turn is declared.

Scanning the sinuosity threshold across three values, from nearly straight to loosely
sinuous, moves $\beta$ from \StatShapeParaBetaStraight\ through \StatShapeParaBetaGentle\ to
\StatShapeParaBetaLoose\ for paragliders, a spread of \StatShapeParaBetaSpread, and from
\StatShapeHangBetaStraight\ through \StatShapeHangBetaGentle\ to \StatShapeHangBetaLoose\ for
hang gliders, a spread of \StatShapeHangBetaSpread\ (Figure~\ref{fig:shape}f). The
decomposition moves with it: \num{\StatShapeParaRunsStraight} paraglider runs of median
\SI{\StatShapeParaMedianStraightS}{\second} at the tightest threshold against
\num{\StatShapeParaRunsLoose} of median \SI{\StatShapeParaMedianLooseS}{\second} at the
loosest. The survival curves at the three thresholds are not parallel
(Figure~\ref{fig:shape}e), which is the same statement before any tail is fitted.

A run length whose tail index moves by \StatShapeParaBetaSpread\ with the angle at which a
turn is declared has no scale of its own. That is the moment spectrum's result reached
through the geometry instead of through the moments: there is no distinguished flight-leg
duration for a L\'evy description to rest on.

The runs are decomposed without overlap, and the alternative changes the answer rather than
refining it. Sampling the run that contains each instant returns the length-biased
distribution, whose tail index is one below the truth \impldetails{impl:global}; at the
values measured here that is not a bias to correct but a different regime.

The scan is also a prediction about Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}. A segmentation into climbs and
glides declares a threshold of the same kind on the same trajectories, and a tail index that
moves by \StatShapeParaBetaSpread\ across three sinuosity thresholds is the measured warning
that the phase statistics will move with theirs. That threshold must be scanned and the
movement reported rather than chosen once.

The two routes share no estimator, and neither supports a L\'evy walk on the unsegmented
trajectory. What survives is a monofractal process, one exponent for every moment over the
range read, which fixes how the increment distribution scales and says nothing about its
shape.
\end{revblock}

\section{The propagator is not Gaussian}
\label{sec:transport-gaussian}
\begin{revblock}
A straight moment spectrum says that one exponent governs every moment of the increment
distribution. It fixes how that distribution \emph{scales} and says nothing about its
\emph{shape}, so the natural next step---calling the process a correlated Gaussian
motion---does not follow from it. The fourth and second moments already accumulated settle
the shape directly, through the non-Gaussian parameter
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_2^{\mathrm{NG}}(\Delta)=
  \frac{\big\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}(\Delta)|^{4}\big\rangle}
       {2\,\big\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}(\Delta)|^{2}\big\rangle^{2}}-1 ,
  \label{eq:nongauss}
\end{equation}
evaluated on the increment over a lag $\Delta$ rather than on the displacement from
take-off, in keeping with how the propagator is defined above. The normalisation is the
two-dimensional Gaussian value of
$\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^{4}\rangle/\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^{2}\rangle^{2}$, so
$\alpha_2^{\mathrm{NG}}$ vanishes for a Gaussian propagator and is positive for
heavier-than-Gaussian tails. The superscript separates it from the filtered-variation
exponents $\alpha_p$: the literature writes the non-Gaussian parameter $\alpha_2$ as well,
which at filter order $p=2$ is the symbol this chapter's exponent already carries. The two
are unrelated quantities.

Over \SIrange{\StatShapeParaNonGaussMinS}{\StatShapeParaNonGaussMaxS}{\second} the median
value is \StatShapeParaNonGaussMedian\ for paragliders and \StatShapeHangNonGaussMedian\
for hang gliders. Neither is flat across it. Both rise to an interior
maximum and fall back: \StatShapeParaNonGaussPeak\ at
\SI{\StatShapeParaNonGaussPeakS}{\second} for paragliders, against
\StatShapeParaNonGaussAtFloor\ at the lower end of the window and
\StatShapeParaNonGaussAtCeiling\ at the upper, a factor of
\StatShapeParaNonGaussPeakRatio; and \StatShapeHangNonGaussPeak\ at
\SI{\StatShapeHangNonGaussPeakS}{\second} for hang gliders. The rise and the fall are each
monotone over seven or more consecutive lags, so this is a shape and not scatter.

A maximum at two to four hundred seconds is a scale, and the chapter has otherwise found
none: the moment spectrum is straight, the run-length tail index has no stable value, and the
exponent is read as a single effective number over the whole window. What the maximum says is
narrower than a scale in the transport --- it is the lag at which the \emph{shape} of the
increment distribution departs furthest from Gaussian, with the departure smaller both below
it and above it. Below, the increments are averages over a smoothing window and a few
heading changes; above, a central-limit Gaussianisation begins to act. Reporting the two ends
of that curve as a difference, which an earlier version of this section did, is the chord of
an arch --- the error diagnosed for the ensemble MSD in Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global} and
repeated here.

\paragraph{What those values are worth.}
A number of this size is readable only against what the same estimator returns on processes
whose shape is known, and the reference has to be read at the sample size the archive is
measured at. On an exact fractional Brownian motion the estimator returns a small positive
value over the modest sample the calibration uses, and that value collapses towards zero,
sign included, as the sample is grown; the offset is finite-sample bias and not a floor of
the estimator. On a L\'evy walk it returns a figure far above either archive's and does not
move with the sample \impldetails{impl:global}. Against that pair both archives are
non-Gaussian and neither is close to L\'evy. The hang-glider figure sits well clear of the
fractional-Brownian offset; the paraglider figure is a small multiple of it.

\paragraph{The disciplines are not equally non-Gaussian.}
They are not averaged either. Over the window the hang-glider parameter never falls below
\StatShapeHangNonGaussMin\ and the paraglider parameter never rises above
\StatShapeParaNonGaussMax, so the two do not overlap at any lag in it, and the separation
between them is larger than the movement either curve shows from one end of the window to
the other. Whatever makes these increments heavier-tailed than Gaussian acts more strongly
on the smaller and slower discipline, by the ratio of \StatShapeHangNonGaussMedian\ to
\StatShapeParaNonGaussMedian. That asymmetry is a property of the two archives rather than
scatter between them, and any mechanism Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} proposes for the shape of
the increment distribution has to reproduce it.

\paragraph{Beyond the fitted range.}
$\alpha_2^{\mathrm{NG}}$ climbs, to \StatShapeParaNonGaussBeyond\ and
\StatShapeHangNonGaussBeyond\ at the longest lags of panel~(c) of Figure~\ref{fig:shape}.
Those values are reported and not quoted, for the two reasons that bound every fitted range
in this chapter. They lie where the declared task governs the trajectory rather than the
air, and a fourth moment of a displacement the scoring rule bounds is a statement about the
scoring rule; and they lie where that moment is carried by the fewest flights, since the
ensemble contributing to a lag thins as the lag approaches the flown durations.

The process is therefore monofractal and its propagator is not Gaussian. One exponent for
every moment fixes how the increment distribution scales, and leaves the shape it scales as
heavy as it is measured to be.
\end{revblock}

\section{The memory of the heading}
\label{sec:transport-memory}
\begin{revblock}
Writing $\delta\mathbf{v}$ for the horizontal velocity fluctuation about a record's own
mean, the velocity autocorrelation is
\begin{equation}
  C(\tau)=\frac{\bigl\langle\,\delta\mathbf{v}(t_0)\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}(t_0+\tau)\,\bigr\rangle_{t_0}}
               {\bigl\langle\,|\delta\mathbf{v}(t_0)|^{2}\,\bigr\rangle_{t_0}},
  \label{eq:vacf}
\end{equation}
normalised so that $C(0)=1$, with the flown course, which would otherwise hold every lag
up, removed before the average is taken. It is computed within a retained segment and never
across the gap that ends one (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), by the Fourier identity rather than
by a double loop, and averaged across records at each lag of the grid the moments use
\impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{Positive at every lag, and small at the first.}
Two things come off the measured curve directly (Figure~\ref{fig:shape}). It is positive
throughout and changes sign nowhere on the grid: $C=\StatShapeParaVacfAtFloor$ for
paragliders at the floor and $\StatShapeHangVacfAtFloor$ for hang gliders, decaying from
there without a zero crossing. A sign change is what a circling wing produces, since half a
turn later its velocity points the other way, and there is none. At the ensemble level the
circling does not survive the average, which is the first constraint on
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}: a phase decomposition has to \emph{produce} the anti-persistence
of a climb, because no statistic on the unsegmented trajectory carries it.

The second is that $C$ is already small at the bottom of the grid. It falls below $1/e$ by
\SI{\StatShapeParaVacfDecayS}{\second} for paragliders and
\SI{\StatShapeHangVacfDecayS}{\second} for hang gliders, within the first lags of it. That
figure is a property of the pipeline as much as of the flying: the grid starts at
\SI{\StatShapeParaFitMinS}{\second} because that is the smoothing scale of the slowest
logger retained (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}), so the decay of $C$ over the first decade is not
measured here. What survives above the floor is small.

\paragraph{Integrability rather than size.}
That last statement and the exponent look incompatible. A memory spent by
\SI{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\second} should leave the motion diffusive over a range reaching
to \SI{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, and the filtered variation says the increments are
still correlated across all of it, at $\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ and
\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo. Both cannot hold if the size of $C$ is what sets the growth law,
and it is not.

For a stationary velocity the Green--Kubo relation
\begin{equation}
  \bigl\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}(\Delta)|^{2}\bigr\rangle
  =2\!\int_{0}^{\Delta}\!(\Delta-\tau)\,C(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
  \label{eq:green-kubo}
\end{equation}
makes the growth law a statement about the convergence of an integral. A tail
$C(\tau)\sim\tau^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma<1$ has no finite integral, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:green-kubo} then gives
$\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^{2}\rangle\sim\Delta^{\,2-\gamma}$ across the window however
small $C$ has already become; with $\gamma>1$ the integral converges and the motion is
diffusive beyond the correlation time whatever $C$ is worth at the floor. Size and
integrability are independent properties of the same curve, and it is the second that
Green--Kubo turns on: multiplying $C$ by a constant moves every value on it and leaves
$\gamma$ where it was.

Over \SIrange{\StatShapeParaVacfTailMinS}{\StatShapeParaVacfTailMaxS}{\second}, which is
\num{\StatShapeParaVacfTailLags} lags and the same lags in both disciplines, the measured
tail is a power law of $\gamma=\StatShapeParaVacfTailGamma$ for paragliders and
\StatShapeHangVacfTailGamma\ for hang gliders. Neither is integrable. The memory is not
spent at the bottom of the grid, only small there, and the objection does not stand.

\paragraph{The comparison is one-sided.}
Two features of the estimate push $\gamma$ up, and both are known in sign. The correlation
is taken about each record's own mean velocity, and a mean estimated from the same record
pulls every lag down by about the record-mean of $C$; that is negligible where $C$ is large
and is most of it where $C$ is small, which is the tail, so the fitted decay is steeper
than the true one. And the first decade of the integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:green-kubo} lies
below the grid, so the fit reads the tail without the stretch that carries the largest part
of the weight. The exponent $2-\gamma$ implied for the displacement is therefore a floor
rather than an estimate: \StatShapeParaVacfImpliedAlphaFloor\ and
\StatShapeHangVacfImpliedAlphaFloor.

The quantity that has to clear it is the plain increment, which is the displacement
statistic Eq.~\eqref{eq:green-kubo} predicts, with no trend removed:
$\alpha_1=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOne$ for paragliders and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderOne\ for
hang gliders. Both clear their floors. That is a consistency between two channels rather
than a second determination of the exponent, since a bound cleared from above fixes
nothing. What it excludes is a velocity memory integrable over this window, which would
have contradicted the position statistic outright.

\paragraph{The turning angles.}
The persistence has a microscopic form and it is measured, in the same units a walker
model would use: the angle between successive horizontal displacement vectors, taken at a
stride of \num{\StatKinParaAngleStrideSamples} samples so that it is the flying and not the
smoothing that is read. The stride is a count of samples rather than an interval on purpose:
the smoothing window is five samples at every retained cadence \impldetails{impl:global}, so
a five-sample stride leaves the two displacement vectors sharing none, which an interval in
seconds would not do at the coarse end. Its median is
\SI{\StatKinParaAngleMedianDeg}{\degree} for paragliders, over \num{\StatKinParaAngles}
angles, and \SI{\StatKinHangAngleMedianDeg}{\degree} for hang gliders over
\num{\StatKinHangAngles}; \SI{\StatKinParaAngleForwardPct}{\percent} and
\SI{\StatKinHangAngleForwardPct}{\percent} of them fall within $\pi/6$ of the previous
heading.

Those two medians are not read at the same interval, because the disciplines do not carry the
same loggers: \SI{\StatKinParaAngleOneHzSharePct}{\percent} of paraglider angles come from
\SI{1}{\hertz} segments against \SI{\StatKinHangAngleOneHzSharePct}{\percent} of
hang-glider ones, so part of the gap between them is a gap between logger populations.
Restricted to \SI{1}{\hertz} segments, where the five samples are \SI{5}{\second} and the
comparison is matched, the medians are
\SI{\StatKinParaAngleMedianOneHzDeg}{\degree} and
\SI{\StatKinHangAngleMedianOneHzDeg}{\degree}. The difference survives the matching and
loses about a quarter of its size. Memoryless reorientation is the
reference, and it is nowhere near: its median is a right angle, and the share it leaves
within $\pi/6$ is the fraction of the half-turn that cone spans.

A wing that changes heading by \SI{\StatKinParaAngleMedianDeg}{\degree} at a time is a
correlated walk and not a memoryless one. A positive $C(\tau)$, a tail too slow to be
integrable, and increments still correlated at \SI{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second} are the
ensemble consequences of the same forward peak. It is also the warrant for the persistent
random walk of Sec.~\ref{sec:modelling}, which needs the angle distribution as an input
rather than as a check.

The distribution is not that peak alone, and the remainder is the second constraint on
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}. A wing turns when it climbs, and Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} measures a
paraglider climbing \SI{\StatKinParaClimbingPct}{\percent} of its time and a hang glider
\SI{\StatKinHangClimbingPct}{\percent}; the angles outside the forward cone are a minority
of the same order. Pooled, the two components give one forward-peaked distribution and a
$C(\tau)$ with no sign change in it, which is why the circling is invisible to both
statistics here and not absent from the flying. The segmentation of
Sec.~\ref{sec:segmentation} separates them, and the turning angles conditioned on phase are
where this result has to reappear.
\end{revblock}

\section{Speed and vertical velocity}
\label{sec:transport-kinematics}
\begin{revblock}
The horizontal speed and the vertical velocity are marginals of the smoothed kinematics of
Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}, taken over every retained in-segment fix. They need no lag grid and
no scaling argument, and they are accumulated in the same streaming pass that builds the
increment histograms of Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator} \impldetails{impl:global}. Each
is read against a reference fixed outside the archive: the bounds under which the cleaning
admitted the data, and the altitude a flight of several hours must return to.

\paragraph{Horizontal speed.}
The median is \SI{\StatKinParaSpeedMedianMs}{\metre\per\second} for paragliders and
\SI{\StatKinHangSpeedMedianMs}{\metre\per\second} for hang gliders, with ninetieth
percentiles of \SI{\StatKinParaSpeedNinetyMs}{\metre\per\second} and
\SI{\StatKinHangSpeedNinetyMs}{\metre\per\second} and ninety-ninth percentiles of
\SI{\StatKinParaSpeedNinetyNineMs}{\metre\per\second} and
\SI{\StatKinHangSpeedNinetyNineMs}{\metre\per\second}. The disciplines separate at every
percentile quoted, and the hang-glider median lies above the paraglider ninetieth
percentile.

Both ninety-ninth percentiles sit well inside the fix-level speed bounds of
Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}, \SI{\PreprocMaxHSpeedParaMps}{\metre\per\second} for paragliders
and \SI{\PreprocMaxHSpeedHangMps}{\metre\per\second} for hang gliders. That margin is a
check on the cleaning: a retained population pressed against the threshold that admitted it
would mean the bound had cut into the fast tail of the flying rather than into instrument
artefacts. The comparison is one-sided, since the bound acts on the inter-fix speed and the
percentiles are of the smoothed velocity, which is the smaller of the two.

\paragraph{Vertical velocity.}
The vertical velocity is centred just below zero, at
\SI{\StatKinParaVerticalMedianMs}{\metre\per\second} and
\SI{\StatKinHangVerticalMedianMs}{\metre\per\second}, with a \SIrange{10}{90}{\percent}
band of
\SIrange{\StatKinParaVerticalTenMs}{\StatKinParaVerticalNinetyMs}{\metre\per\second} for
paragliders and
\SIrange{\StatKinHangVerticalTenMs}{\StatKinHangVerticalNinetyMs}{\metre\per\second} for
hang gliders, inside the fix-level bound of \SI{\PreprocMaxVSpeedMps}{\metre\per\second} at
both ends --- though a decile band says less about the cleaning than the ninety-ninth
percentile of the speed does.

Paragliders climb \SI{\StatKinParaClimbingPct}{\percent} of the time and hang gliders
\SI{\StatKinHangClimbingPct}{\percent}. A flight that ends near the altitude it began at
divides its time between climbing and sinking in the inverse ratio of the two rates, and the
band above makes those rates comparable, so over the median flight of
\SI{\StatPrelimParaMedianDurH}{\hour} and \SI{\StatPrelimHangMedianDurH}{\hour} recorded in
Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} the climbing share is required to be near a half, which both measured
shares are. Both fall short of a half, which is the same fact as the negative median: the
wing spends more of its time sinking, so its mean rate of climb exceeds its mean rate of
sink.

The sign of the vertical velocity is the coarsest climb--glide rule available, so these two
shares are what a segmentation in Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} must return as its threshold goes
to zero, and this is the distribution any such threshold cuts.

\paragraph{The return probability.}
The return probability is the increment propagator at its centre,
$P_0(\Delta)\equiv P(\mathbf{0},\Delta)$; the scaling form of
Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator} makes it decay as $\Delta^{-dH}$ in $d$ dimensions and
as $\Delta^{-H}$ on one component, so its slope is another reading of $H$. It is defined
here and not measured.

That reading would not repeat the one already made.
Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator} reads $H$ at four fixed probability levels and finds
that they do not share a slope, by \StatPropParaQuantileSpread\ and
\StatPropHangQuantileSpread; the peak reads the same $F$ at a fifth level, inside the
innermost of the four. The ordering measured there predicts an exponent at the peak at or
above \StatPropParaHurstBulk, and a value below it would place the drift in the estimator
rather than in the distribution. It is also the cheapest check remaining on the headline
exponent: the streaming histograms of $|\Delta x|$ per lag, per component and per native
cadence that supply those quantiles hold their own centre as well, so $P_0$ costs a fit and
no further traversal of the fix table.

First-passage times are not on this list: they were measured and withdrawn, in
Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator}, because a time to reach a radius from take-off carries
the launch geometry into the space domain.
\end{revblock}

\section{What this establishes, and what the segmentation receives}
\label{sec:transport-verdict}
\begin{revblock}
Over \SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, measured within a retained
segment by an estimator that annihilates a polynomial trend rather than estimating one,
cross-country soaring gives
$\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ for paragliders and
$\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo\pm\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErr$ for hang gliders, on
\num{\StatMsdParaFlights} and \num{\StatMsdHangFlights} flights. The wing classes that
depart from the pooled ensemble MSD by
\SI{\StatPrelimParaClassTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} (Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}) move this
exponent by \StatVarParaClassSpreadAlpha; native cadence, across a factor of ten in the
scale below which the trajectory has been smoothed, moves it by
\StatVarParaCadenceSpreadAlpha, season by \StatVarParaSeasonSpreadAlpha, and the declared
task by $\StatVarParaTaskGapOrderTwo$, the largest of the four. The two disciplines agree
within their uncertainties, which is not guaranteed: they differ in speed and in glide
ratio.

\paragraph{The class of process.}
Every moment scales with that one exponent. Over the fitted range and $q$ from
\StatShapeParaQMin\ to \StatShapeParaQMax, $\nu(q)$ varies by \StatShapeParaNuSpread\ for
paragliders and \StatShapeHangNuSpread\ for hang gliders, and the moment spectrum departs
from a line through the origin by \StatShapeParaLinearDeparture\ and
\StatShapeHangLinearDeparture\ in rms, an order of magnitude below the departure a Lévy
walk's knee would leave on this grid. The propagator is nevertheless not Gaussian:
$\alpha^{\mathrm{NG}}=\StatShapeParaNonGaussMedian$ and $\StatShapeHangNonGaussMedian$, flat
across the same window. The velocity autocorrelation is positive at every lag of the grid,
and its tail is a power law of $\gamma=\StatShapeParaVacfTailGamma$ and
\StatShapeHangVacfTailGamma, non-integrable in both, which is what sustains correlated
increments to \SI{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second} from a correlation already below $1/e$ by
\SI{\StatShapeParaVacfDecayS}{\second}. No leg duration is distinguished: the tail index of
the persistence-run durations moves from \StatShapeParaBetaStraight\ to
\StatShapeParaBetaLoose\ as the sinuosity threshold that defines a run is scanned, a spread
of \StatShapeParaBetaSpread. The quantiles of the increment distribution, which are neither
a moment nor a statistic about a common origin, put $H$ at \StatPropParaHurst\ and
\StatPropHangHurst, between the two moment routes. Over this window the archive is a
monofractal, non-Gaussian, directionally persistent motion with a non-integrable velocity
memory --- neither the Lévy walk this thesis was framed around nor a Gaussian one.

\paragraph{The limits.}
The window is \StatVarParaFitDecades\ decades wide, and neither end is set by the size of
the archive. Below it the trajectory has been smoothed over a window whose floor is
\PreprocSavgolMinWindow\ samples and which therefore scales with the logger's cadence
(Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}); nothing below that scale is read as motion. Above it the declared
task governs the displacement, the separation between closed and open courses in the local
slope growing from \num{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapLow} at
\SI{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapLowS}{\second} to \num{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapHigh} at
\SI{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapHighS}{\second}. Inside the window the scaling form is only
approximate: $H$ falls from \StatPropParaHurstBulk\ at the bulk of the increment
distribution to \StatPropParaHurstFlank\ at its flank, a spread across the quantiles of
\StatPropParaQuantileSpread\ and \StatPropHangQuantileSpread\ against \StatPropCalibSpread\
on an exactly self-similar process measured the same way. The exponent itself moves by
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrRange\ when the fitted range is halved, against
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrSampling\ from resampling at the day and site, and the
\num{\StatVarParaFitLags} lags of the fit carry no more than \StatVarParaDof\ effective
degrees of freedom, which is a budget for one exponent and not for a count of regimes. The
filtered variation has an effective exponent of \StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo\ over
\SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}; that the transport is
characterised by $\alpha=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ is a different statement, and the range
dependence and the quantile spread are each enough to withhold it. The window will not
widen with more data: a soaring day ends, so the upper cutoff follows from the system rather
than from the method.

\paragraph{What was withdrawn.}
Four measurements are withdrawn. The ensemble MSD about take-off is withdrawn as a source of
exponents and yields a timescale instead: displacement from the launch site crosses over
where the population leaves its launch area, at a median of
\SI{\StatAuditParaDepartureMedianS}{\second} for paragliders and
\SI{\StatAuditHangDepartureMedianS}{\second} for hang gliders on the first crossing of
\SI{\StatAuditParaDepartureRadiusKm}{\kilo\metre}. The first-passage time was brought in as
a route to $H$ in the space domain and withdrawn when its exponent moved across sub-ranges
of radius by more than the uncertainty it was meant to check; what it bought is the
sharpest evidence that the ensemble curve's failure belongs to the common origin rather
than to the time axis, since exchanging the time axis for a radius reproduces it
\impldetails{impl:global}. The sign-run count on the MSD residuals is kept as a description
of how those residuals are arranged and withdrawn as a test, every lag of that curve being
an average over the same flights; what carries the claim instead is the size of the
residual against the standard error on each lag. And the reading of a straight moment
spectrum as a Gaussian propagator is withdrawn, one exponent for every moment being a
statement about how the distribution scales and none about its shape; what it bought is the
non-Gaussian parameter, computed from moments the spectrum had already accumulated.

\paragraph{The hand-over.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} inherits a class of transport and six constraints on how it may be
decomposed.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Any statistic measured from take-off inherits the launch geometry. On the
        displacement statistic that contamination governs the curve out to the departure
        times above, \SI{\StatAuditParaDepartureMedianS}{\second} and
        \SI{\StatAuditHangDepartureMedianS}{\second}, and it is not confined to the
        observable that exposed it: any phase statistic binned by elapsed time since
        take-off carries it below those times. The remedy is the one used throughout this
        chapter, to measure within a segment and never about a common origin
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).
  \item The estimator is additive over any partition of the sample. $V_2(\Delta)$ is a mean
        over the placements of a second difference; labelling those placements by phase and
        recombining the per-phase means with the weights actually observed returns the
        pooled curve identically, and with it
        $\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo\ over
        \SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}. That is a property of
        the estimator rather than of the transport: it asserts no decomposition of an
        exponent into phase exponents, only that the arithmetic closes. A recombination
        that misses the pooled value is therefore evidence about the segmentation ---
        placements dropped at a phase boundary, counted twice, or straddling one and
        assigned to neither --- and is to be read that way before it is read as physics.
  \item A climb--glide threshold is declared in the same way as the sinuosity threshold of
        the persistence runs, and that threshold moved the run-length tail index by
        \StatShapeParaBetaSpread\ across its scan. Every phase statistic is to be reported
        against a scan of the threshold that produced it, with the movement quoted.
  \item The two horizontal components are analysed separately and never pooled. They are
        incompatible in amplitude (Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}) and they differ in exponent:
        $H=\StatPropParaHurstEast$ east against \StatPropParaHurstNorth\ north for
        paragliders, and \StatPropHangHurstEast\ against \StatPropHangHurstNorth\ for hang
        gliders. Pooling presupposes an isotropy this ensemble does not have.
  \item The non-Gaussian parameter is $\StatShapeParaNonGaussMedian$ for paragliders and
        $\StatShapeHangNonGaussMedian$ for hang gliders over the same window: whatever makes
        these increments heavier-tailed than Gaussian acts about fourfold more strongly on
        the smaller, slower wing. Any mechanism proposed for the phase structure has to
        produce that gap rather than average it away.
  \item The ergodicity question is handed on in the two forms that need no power law. The
        first is the drift of the exponent with the time already flown, obtained by running
        the same within-segment estimator on successive stretches of a record: an ageing
        process shows such a drift and a launch-geometry artefact does not
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:segmentation}). The second is the ergodicity-breaking parameter of
        Appendix~\ref{app:ctrw}, which compares the distribution of the per-flight time
        average across flights rather than two fitted slopes. The usual comparison of
        $\alpha_{\mathrm{EA}}$ with $\alpha_{\mathrm{TA}}$ requires both curves to be power
        laws, and this archive supplies one of the two.
\end{enumerate}

The class of transport is fixed over one window; which flight behaviour produces it is not.
Every number above mixes climbing, gliding and searching into one, and the persistent random
walk of Sec.~\ref{sec:modelling} takes its warrant from the memory measured here rather than
from any single one of them. Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} separates the phases, and is answerable
to the six constraints.
\end{revblock}
