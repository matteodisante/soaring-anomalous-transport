\chapter{Global transport}
\label{ch:global}

\begin{revblock}
This chapter measures the transport of the retained ensemble without applying any segmentation. 
What can be established that way is a \emph{class} of transport: how far a wing
gets in a given time, how that growth is distributed, how long the motion remembers its
direction, and how many distinct regimes the answer needs. What cannot be established is
which flight behaviour produces it, and that is Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}.

The measurements are made on the analysis ensemble assembled in Chapter~\ref{ch:dataset}:
\num{\StatMsdParaFlights} paraglider and \num{\StatMsdHangFlights} hang-glider flights,
holding \num{\StatMsdParaSegments} and \num{\StatMsdHangSegments} retained segments. Every
statistic below is computed within a retained segment.

One constraint from Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} holds throughout. The horizontal components are
anisotropic: the ratio of their mean squares sits at \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMedian\ for
paragliders and \StatPrelimHangIsoRatioMedian\ for hang gliders, so the two
marginals are measured separately. The discipline is also heterogeneous
(Sec.~\ref{sec:strata-compat}): read
against the pooled ensemble MSD, the typical wing class departs by
\SI{\StatPrelimParaClassTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} and the typical orographic group by
\SI{\StatPrelimParaGroupTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent}, against
\SI{\StatPrelimParaSeasonTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} across seasons. Wing class and
terrain are relevant strata and season is not, so every exponent below is reported by
stratum as well as pooled, and the two disciplines are never pooled into each other.

Over \SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, measured within a segment
and never about a common origin, the mean-squared increment of cross-country soaring grows
as $\Delta^{\alpha_1}$ with
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_1 = 2H =
  \begin{cases}
    \StatVarParaAlphaOrderOne\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOneErr
      & \text{paragliders}\quad(H=\StatVarParaHurstOrderOne),\\[2pt]
    \StatVarHangAlphaOrderOne\pm\StatVarHangAlphaOrderOneErr
      & \text{hang gliders}\quad(H=\StatVarHangHurstOrderOne),
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:headline-alpha}
\end{equation}
super-diffusive in both disciplines. The flown course is the pilot's own net heading, a
constant-velocity component distinct from the wandering around it; filtering it out of the
same trajectories moves the exponent to $\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ and
$\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo$, and the gap $\alpha_1-\alpha_2$ is how much of $\alpha_1$ was
that heading rather than the wandering (Sec.~\ref{sec:variations}).

It is not a
L\'evy walk: the spectrum of moments is straight, $\nu(q)$
moving by \StatShapeParaNuSpread\ and \StatShapeHangNuSpread\ across the range of $q$ read.
It is not Gaussian either: the non-Gaussian parameter is
$\StatShapeParaNonGaussMedian$ for paragliders and $\StatShapeHangNonGaussMedian$ for hang
gliders in the median, rising to an interior maximum near
\SI{\StatShapeParaNonGaussPeakS}{\second}.
\end{revblock}

\section{Displacement from take-off measures the launch geometry}
\label{sec:obs-global}
\begin{revblock}
Every flight starts at the origin, $\mathbf{r}(0)=\mathbf{0}$, so the position after an
elapsed time $t$ and the displacement from take-off are the same vector. Its mean square
admits two averages that answer different questions. The ensemble average
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MSD}(t)=\big\langle\,|\mathbf{r}(t)|^2\big\rangle ,
  \label{eq:ea-msd}
\end{equation}
taken over flights at a fixed elapsed time, fixes the transport regime through
$\mathrm{MSD}(t)\sim t^{\alpha}$ --- $\alpha=1$ Brownian, $\alpha>1$ super-diffusive,
$\alpha<1$ sub-diffusive, $H=\alpha/2$ --- and is the observable the anomalous-transport
question is normally posed with. The time average
\begin{equation}
  \overline{\delta^2(\tau)}
  =\big\langle\,|\mathbf{r}(t_0+\tau)-\mathbf{r}(t_0)|^2\big\rangle_{t_0} ,
  \label{eq:ta-msd}
\end{equation}
taken over starting times $t_0$ inside one record, answers how far a wing travels in a
window of duration $\tau$ placed anywhere in its flight. The ensemble average is
synchronised at take-off, so a feature it carries at a fixed elapsed time is a property of
that common origin; the time average slides its window with no privileged origin, so a
feature the first shows and the second does not belongs to the origin rather than the
motion.

Both are computed on the same analysis ensemble, with a flight answering a lag only when
it holds a fix within half its own native interval of it \impldetails{impl:global}.
Figure~\ref{fig:msd} shows both estimators.

\paragraph{Three controls rule out an artefact.}
A changing population, survivorship, and a shared heading could each produce the same arch
without it belonging to the motion.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt, topsep=5pt]
  \item \emph{A changing population.} Splitting by native cadence removes any drift from a
        growing population: across the \StatAuditParaCadenceCohorts\ paraglider classes the
        local slope spreads by only \StatAuditParaCadenceSpread\ against a swing of
        \StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing.
  \item \emph{Survivorship.} Fixed-duration cohorts remove it: the local slope spreads
        across cohorts by \StatAuditParaDurationSpread\ and \StatAuditHangDurationSpread,
        where a synthetic ensemble with only the \emph{speed} correlated to duration
        reproduces neither feature \impldetails{impl:global}.
  \item \emph{A shared heading.} It is bounded by the part of the mean square the mean
        vector carries, which never exceeds \SI{\StatAuditParaDriftPct}{\percent} for
        paragliders and \SI{\StatAuditHangDriftPct}{\percent} for hang gliders.
\end{itemize}
The flights do not share a heading; they share a starting point.

\paragraph{The origin of the crossover.}
A wing does not leave its launch site immediately: it climbs out, often circling close to
it, and only then commits to a course. Until it does, its distance from take-off is bounded
by the local soaring area however fast it is flying, which depresses the local slope at
intermediate lags; once the population has committed, the distance grows with the flown
speed, which returns the slope towards $2$. Because commitment happens at a spread of times
rather than at $t=0$, the transition overshoots.

Taking the first crossing of \SI{\StatAuditParaDepartureRadiusKm}{\kilo\metre} from take-off
as the departure onto the cross-country leg --- well inside the median flown path of
\SI{\StatPipeParaMedianPathKm}{\kilo\metre} --- the departure time and the slope peak land
together in both disciplines (Table~\ref{tab:departure}). The radius is chosen rather than
fitted, so the agreement corroborates the account rather than measuring it.

take-off is therefore a statement about launch geometry over the decade where the overshoot

and \StatMsdHangAlpha\ over \SIrange{\StatMsdParaFitMinS}{\StatMsdParaFitMaxS}{\second} ---
is a chord across a bend rather than a growth law the motion obeys.

\paragraph{The time average does not share the ensemble's crossover.}
Panel~(b) is Eq.~\eqref{eq:ta-msd} on the same ensemble. Over
\SIrange{\StatMsdTaParaFitMinS}{\StatMsdTaParaFitMaxS}{\second} and
\SIrange{\StatMsdTaHangFitMinS}{\StatMsdTaHangFitMaxS}{\second} it gives
$\alpha_{\mathrm{TA}}=\StatMsdTaParaAlpha$ ($H=\StatMsdTaParaHurst$) for paragliders and
$\StatMsdTaHangAlpha$ ($H=\StatMsdTaHangHurst$) for hang gliders, both above Brownian and
below ballistic, with a residual well under half the ensemble's. 
Its local slope is not flat --
it dips to a broad interior minimum of \StatAuditParaTaSlopeMin\ near
\SI{\StatAuditParaTaSlopeMinAt}{\second} for paragliders, and to \StatAuditHangTaSlopeMin\
for hang gliders, a discipline where the short-lag estimate itself carries the widest
error in the panel -- but its swing, \StatAuditParaTaSlopeSwing\ and
\StatAuditHangTaSlopeSwing, is under half the ensemble's, \StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing\ and
\StatAuditHangEaSlopeSwing. Neither curve shows the ensemble's crossover at the ensemble's
own timescale: what confirms that crossover is a property of the common origin is its
absence from panel~(b) near \SI{\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second}, not a claim that
panel~(b) is featureless everywhere.

\paragraph{The time average has an upturn of its own, at the far edge of its range.}
Past its interior minimum, panel~(b)'s slope climbs back above the ballistic value,
to \StatAuditParaTaSlopeMax\ at \SI{\StatAuditParaTaSlopeMaxAt}{\second} for paragliders and
\StatAuditHangTaSlopeMax\ at \SI{\StatAuditHangTaSlopeMaxAt}{\second} for hang gliders --
stronger on the smaller archive.

That is also where the segment population itself has
thinned to a small fraction of what answers the shorter lags, the same kind of thinning
noted below for the ensemble, so a rise there reads as a survivorship effect on the
segments long enough to reach it -- plausible rather than demonstrated, since panel~(b)
supplies no cadence or duration control of its own to test it against, the way the
ensemble's own crossover is tested in the paragraphs above. It is reported rather than
fitted around, because
$\alpha_{\mathrm{TA}}$ is already a chord across this bend and not a claim that the bend
is absent; both exponents are reported as \emph{effective} exponents over a stated range.

\paragraph{The gap between the two averages is not evidence of ageing.}
Fitted over their respective ranges the ensemble slope exceeds the time-averaged exponent by
\num{\StatMsdParaAlphaGap} for paragliders and \num{\StatMsdHangAlphaGap} for hang gliders, a
gap of the kind that signals a non-ergodic, ageing process~\cite{he2008}. Reading it that way
here presupposes both curves are exponents, and the ensemble figure is a chord across a curve
whose local slope covers \StatAuditParaEaSlopeSwing, so changing the fitted range moves it by
more than the gap. What the measurement supports is weaker: displacement from take-off grows
faster, over the upper part of its range, than displacement over a window of the same
duration placed anywhere in the flight. Separating ageing from launch geometry needs the
phase decomposition of Sec.~\ref{sec:segmentation} and the ergodicity-breaking parameter
below, neither of which needs a curve to be a power law.

\paragraph{Two direct checks: a duration split, and the parameter itself.}
Appendix~\ref{app:ctrw} derives why duration is the sharper question than the EA/TA gap:
for a continuous-time random walk with power-law waiting times, the time-averaged MSD
carries a prefactor that depends on the total duration $T$ of the record it is read from,
$\mathrm{TAMSD}(t,T)\sim T^{\alpha-1}t$, present for a non-ergodic process and collapsing
away for an ergodic one; and it defines the ergodicity-breaking parameter
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{EB}(t) \equiv
  \frac{\mathrm{Var}_f\!\big[\overline{\delta^2}_f(t)\big]}
       {\mathbb{E}_f\!\big[\overline{\delta^2}_f(t)\big]^2} ,
  \label{eq:eb-param}
\end{equation}
the scatter of the per-flight time average across flights, normalised by its square: a
finite positive constant for a non-ergodic process and zero for an ergodic one, however
long the flights run. Neither test needs a curve to be a power law, and both can be asked
of this archive directly, whatever process it turns out to be.

Figure~\ref{fig:msd-duration}a
puts the pooled time-averaged MSD of Figure~\ref{fig:msd}b beside its own fixed-duration
cohorts (\num{1}, \num{2} and \SI{4}{\hour} thresholds), each
cut at half its own threshold. Read at \SI{\StatMsdTaParaCohortAmpRefS}{\second}, a lag
common to all three, the paraglider cohorts sit at \StatMsdTaParaCohortAmpOneH,
\StatMsdTaParaCohortAmpTwoH\ and \StatMsdTaParaCohortAmpFourH\ times the pooled curve;
hang gliders move more, at \StatMsdTaHangCohortAmpOneH, \StatMsdTaHangCohortAmpTwoH\ and
\StatMsdTaHangCohortAmpFourH. The curves do not collapse, but the eye cannot see that they
do not: a \StatMsdTaParaCohortAmpFourH-fold separation is \num{0.13} decades, invisible on
a panel spanning the eight the record itself covers. That is what panel~(b) is for.

\paragraph{The parameter itself does not go away.}
App.~\ref{app:ctrw} defines $\mathrm{EB}(t,T)$ as a question about $T$, the duration of
the record, at a lag $t$ held fixed --- not about the lag --- so that is what is measured:
the lag is fixed at \SI{\StatErgoParaLagS}{\second}, well under a quarter of the shortest
duration threshold, and every flight is required to supply at least
\StatErgoParaMinWindows\ of its own windows at that lag before it answers for a given $T$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-gaussian} applies the same reach rule to its Gaussian null). Over
\SIrange{\StatErgoParaRangeMinH}{\StatErgoParaRangeMaxH}{\hour} of required duration,
$\mathrm{EB}(T)$ falls with $T$ in both disciplines --- from \StatErgoParaAtOneH\ on
\num{\StatErgoParaAtOneHFlights}\ paraglider flights of at least one hour to
\StatErgoParaAtFourH\ on \num{\StatErgoParaAtFourHFlights}\ of at least four; from
\StatErgoHangAtOneH\ to \StatErgoHangAtFourH\ for hang gliders --- but it does not reach
zero inside the range this archive can answer: the direct signature App.~\ref{app:ctrw}
defines, not the duration-cohort proxy above, and it needs neither curve to be a power law
nor the archive to be the sub-diffusive CTRW the appendix derives it for --- only that the
per-flight time average keeps a genuine spread across flights rather than concentrating
to zero as the record required to answer grows. A parameter that falls but does not
vanish over the range measured is weaker evidence than one that sits flat, and this
archive cannot rule out that it continues falling past four hours, where too few flights
remain to answer; what it does rule out is the read the lag-swept panel gave in an earlier
draft of this test, which fixed nothing and swept the wrong variable.

That the duration cohorts \emph{rise} with $T$ is not the direction Appendix~\ref{app:ctrw}'s
own toy derivation predicts: its $T^{\alpha-1}$ falls with $T$ for the sub-diffusive
$\alpha<1$ the model assumes, and this archive's transport is super-diffusive throughout.
Both that rise and $\mathrm{EB}(T)$'s own fall are compatible with a plainer account the
chapter has used before: duration and performance are confounded on this archive, since a
pilot who stays up longer is more likely flying a faster wing, better conditions, or both,
so raising the duration threshold does not lengthen the same flights, it selects a
different, more homogeneous population of them. That confound could by itself produce a
falling $\mathrm{EB}(T)$ -- a narrower population is a lower-scatter population -- so the
fall on its own is not evidence of ergodicity; what it does show is that the scatter across
flights is not the flat, always-positive quantity the lag-swept version of this test
reported, and it has not been shown to vanish either, which is the weaker and honest
reading. Separating the selection effect from a genuine dependence of the motion on how
long it has already run is the phase decomposition of
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}, on the EB parameter computed per phase rather than pooled; what
this section establishes is prior to that and does not need it: the time average is not
duration-independent, and its scatter across flights has not been shown to vanish, either
of which neither the ensemble MSD nor the filtered variation was built to test.

\paragraph{What the two estimators return.}
The time average gives an effective exponent on a curve close to a single power law. The
ensemble average gives a timescale, not an exponent: displacement from take-off crosses over
near \SI{\StatAuditParaEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second} for paragliders and
\SI{\StatAuditHangEaSlopeMaxAt}{\second} for hang gliders, where the population leaves its
launch area, and no single power law spans that crossover. Neither curve carries a
confidence interval here: a bootstrap over flights would treat the archive's records as
independent, which they are not, and a curve that is a systematic arch has no single exponent
for a sampling error to be an error \emph{of}. The resampling unit and the range dependence
are measured in Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-measure}, on the estimator that replaces this one.

Below the fitted range the curves carry two pipeline artefacts rather than flying ones: a
coverage rule meeting the integer lag grid, and the smoothing evaluated off-centre at a
segment's first samples, which moves the curve by up to \SI{\StatEdgeParaShortPct}{\percent}
below \SI{\StatEdgeParaShortLagS}{\second} on the \SI{\StatEdgeParaSamplePct}{\percent} of
fixes it touches \impldetails{impl:global}. Above it, the ensemble thins to a
survivorship-selected minority, holding only \num{\StatAuditParaFlightsWideLag} flights at
\SI{\StatAuditParaFlightsWideLagS}{\second}. Nothing outside the fitted range in
Figure~\ref{fig:msd} is read as motion.

A displacement statistic on this archive is contaminated three ways:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt, topsep=5pt]
  \item by the common origin just diagnosed;
  \item by each flight's own course, which does not simply inflate it, since
        \SI{\StatVarParaClosedPct}{\percent} of retained paraglider flights fly a closed
        triangle that saturates displacement at a scoring-rule timescale rather than a
        flying one;
  \item by the heterogeneity of Sec.~\ref{sec:strata-compat}, which lets a superposition of
        ordinary processes with different persistence times imitate a single anomalous one.
\end{itemize}
None of the three is repaired by subtracting an estimate of it; the next section meets the
specification by filtering instead.
\end{revblock}

\section{The filtered variation}
\label{sec:variations}
\begin{revblock}
The displacement statistic leaves a specification: a quantity measured from no common
origin, requiring no estimate of the flown course, and not turned into a single anomalous
law by a mixture of populations. The first two are met by construction, the third by
measurement below.

\paragraph{Filtering rather than subtracting.}
The course could be subtracted rather than filtered, but subtraction fails here: the
natural estimate $[\mathbf{r}(T)-\mathbf{r}(0)]/T$ is built from the record's own
endpoints, and subtracting it turns the trajectory into a bridge whose increments must sum
to zero by construction. On synthetic fractional Brownian motion of known exponent the
operation returns a value well below the truth at every lag, worst towards the record's own
length \impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{The estimator.}
A finite difference removes a trend without estimating it, because it annihilates the trend
identically. Writing $A_p$ for the difference of order $p$ taken at spacing $\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
  A_p\,\mathbf{r}(t;\Delta)
  =\sum_{k=0}^{p}(-1)^{k}\binom{p}{k}\,\mathbf{r}\bigl(t+(p-k)\Delta\bigr),
  \label{eq:filter}
\end{equation}
the operator vanishes identically on every polynomial trajectory of degree $p-1$, and the
\emph{filtered variation} is its mean square,
\begin{equation}
  V_p(\Delta)=\Bigl\langle\,\bigl|A_p\,\mathbf{r}(t;\Delta)\bigr|^{2}\Bigr\rangle
  \;\sim\;\Delta^{\alpha_p},
  \label{eq:variation}
\end{equation}
averaged over every admissible $t$ and flight and summed over the two horizontal
components, which is what makes it invariant under a rotation of the frame: an anisotropic
ensemble must not return a different exponent for having been measured with north somewhere
else. The propagator of Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator} does the opposite and keeps the
components apart, because the ensemble is anisotropic (Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}) and a
one-dimensional marginal is exactly what it reads; the two are different statistics of the
same trajectories rather than one of them pooled.

For a self-similar process with stationary increments $\alpha_p=2H$ at every
order, with only the prefactor depending on $p$: $p=1$ is the plain increment and is blind
to the origin, $p=2$ to a constant velocity, $p=3$ to a constant acceleration, so no drift
is estimated at any order and none can be estimated badly.

The three orders are not
successive refinements of one number, and are not expected to agree: $\alpha_1$ is the
exponent of displacement with the flown course still inside it, $\alpha_2$ is what that
displacement does once a mean velocity --- the course --- is set aside, and a course that is
no part of the wandering still adds to how far a wing gets, so the two answer different
questions even when both are read off the same trajectories. A difference of order $p$ spans
$p\Delta$ of record, so each order costs coverage at the long end of the grid and the scan
stops at $p=3$.

The scan over orders is itself a measurement. The difference $\alpha_1-\alpha_2$ is the part
of the apparent exponent that was the flown course, read off directly rather than modelled,
and the agreement between $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_3$ is the statement that nothing
polynomial is left for a higher difference to find. On synthetic data with a constant
course imposed on a process of known exponent, order~1 returns a displaced value and
orders~2 and~3 both recover the truth \impldetails{impl:global}. Everything below keeps to
the same segment rule as before, and never uses displacement from take-off.

\paragraph{The fitted range.}
\SIrange{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, \StatVarParaFitDecades\
decades, declared in advance for both disciplines and all three orders, with both ends set
by the system rather than the curve. Below, the Savitzky--Golay window has a floor of
\num{\PreprocSavgolMinWindow} samples (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}), and
\SI{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\second} sits above that window for the slowest logger retained.
Above, the declared task takes over: the CFD scoring rule rewards a closed course, and a
closed course comes home at a time set by the task rather than the air. The fit is ordinary
least squares on $\log V_p$ against $\log\Delta$, applied to the mean of the per-flight
curves rather than to per-flight exponents averaged afterwards.

\paragraph{Removing the course raises the exponent.}
$\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ differ by $\StatVarParaDriftShare$ for paragliders and
$\StatVarHangDriftShare$ for hang gliders, and the sign is the opposite of what a residual
drift would leave: a drift normally inflates a displacement statistic, but on this archive
the declared course \emph{suppresses} it, since \SI{\StatVarParaClosedPct}{\percent} of
retained paraglider flights and \SI{\StatVarHangClosedPct}{\percent} of hang-glider flights
fly a triangle that returns towards its start, saturating the plain increment at a
task-set lag which the second-order variation does not see at all.

Fitting closed and open
tasks separately confirms it directly: the gap between them (open minus closed) is
$\StatVarParaTaskGapOrderOne$ at order~1 for paragliders and $\StatVarHangTaskGapOrderOne$
for hang gliders --- closed tasks reading lower --- and it reverses to
$\StatVarParaTaskGapOrderTwo$ and $\StatVarHangTaskGapOrderTwo$ at order~2, with the order-1
gap growing substantially past the fitted range and the order-2 gap not moving. An exponent
read with no trend removed is therefore expected to sit below one measured with the course
filtered out, by the difference between the two orders.

\paragraph{Orders two and three agree, and order one checks against the time average.}
$\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_3$ agree to \StatVarParaOrderAgreement\ for paragliders and
\StatVarHangOrderAgreement\ for hang gliders, well inside either interval: nothing
polynomial survives the second difference for the third to remove. And $\alpha_1$, which
\emph{is} $\overline{\delta^2(\Delta)}$ under another name, computed by an independent
implementation over a different range, agrees with $\alpha_{\mathrm{TA}}$ of
Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global} more closely than either moves when its own range is halved ---
$\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOne$ against $\StatMsdTaParaAlpha$ for paragliders and
\StatVarHangAlphaOrderOne\ against \StatMsdTaHangAlpha\ for hang gliders.

\paragraph{The two orders answer to different ceilings.}
The reported exponent is bounded. A plain increment cannot report above the ballistic value
on any process with stationary increments: the displacement over $n$ steps is a sum of $n$
increments, and Minkowski's inequality bounds its norm by the sum of theirs,
\begin{equation}
  V_1(n\Delta)\le n^{2}\,V_1(\Delta),
  \label{eq:v1-bound}
\end{equation}
so $\alpha_1\le2$ whatever the motion does --- a bound the archive respects with room
to spare, at $\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOne$ and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderOne.

The control does not share that ceiling. On a differentiable trajectory
$A_2\,\mathbf{r}\simeq\ddot{\mathbf{r}}\,\Delta^2$, so $V_2\sim\Delta^{4}$ and the
second-order ceiling is $4$, twice the ballistic value; a straight line sits at the opposite
extreme, where the second difference vanishes identically. So
$\alpha_2=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ and $\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwo$ sitting just past $2$
says that the trajectory stays correlated and rough across the range; reading $\alpha_2$
against the bound that governs $\alpha_1$ is the only way it looks like a violation. How far the archive is from exact self-similarity is measured in
Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator}, on the distribution of the increments rather than on
any second moment.
\end{revblock}

\section{Compatibility across strata}
\label{sec:strata-compat}
\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{The test.}
The dataset spans more than four decades, two glider types and a range of performance
classes (Sec.~\ref{sec:glider}) whose kinematics differ substantially. Before treating the

are checked. The test is the horizontal MSD computed within each stratum and read against

\paragraph{What may be pooled.}
So the ensemble may be pooled over time and may not be pooled over wing class or over
terrain. Season is not a relevant stratum and the archive's two-decade span is not a
confound. Class and geography are relevant strata, and the difference is itself a result
rather than a nuisance: a competition wing covers ground faster than a recreational one,
and thermals over a mountain range are anchored to slopes and organised along ridges
whereas over flat terrain they drift with the wind, so the persistence of the glides and
the spacing of the climbs need not be the same. The exponent below is therefore reported
by wing class as well as pooled, alongside cuts by native cadence, season and declared
task (Table~\ref{tab:strata-alpha}); the orographic cut is made here, on the ensemble MSD,

\section{The uncertainty on the exponent}
\label{sec:transport-measure}
\begin{revblock}
The archive holds \num{\StatMsdParaFlights} paraglider and \num{\StatMsdHangFlights}
hang-glider flights, and \num{\StatVarParaClusters} and \num{\StatVarHangClusters} occasions
on which one site was flown on one day. An interval built on the first pair of counts is
not an interval built on the second, so which of them carries the independent measurements
has to be settled before either is quoted.

\paragraph{The resampling unit.}
The quantity clustered is the logarithm of each flight's own first-order variation at the
lag nearest the geometric centre of the fitted window
(\SI{\StatVarParaIccLagS}{\second}), the per-flight input every fit in this chapter
averages over. Its
\flow{intraclass correlation at a level $\ell$ is
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ICC}_\ell = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{between},\,\ell}}
    {\sigma^2_{\text{between},\,\ell}+\sigma^2_{\text{within},\,\ell}},
  \label{eq:icc}
\end{equation}
the share of the total variance sitting \emph{between} clusters at that level rather than
within them;} near zero the clustering is irrelevant, near one a cluster is effectively a
single measurement. The ladder (Table~\ref{tab:icc}) is zero at the flight by construction
and largest at the day and site in both disciplines --- the air over one site on one day
being what two flights actually have in common --- so every bootstrap in this chapter
resamples days and sites, of which the archive holds \num{\StatVarParaClusters} and
\num{\StatVarHangClusters}.

The correction this buys is small and in the expected
direction: resampling flights returns \StatVarParaAlphaOrderOneErrNaive\ on the paraglider
$\alpha_1$ against \StatVarParaAlphaOrderOneErrSampling\ from the clustered resample, both
sitting below the last digit of the exponent they qualify.

\paragraph{The sampling term and the range term.}
The two are reported separately. On $\alpha_2$ the day-and-site bootstrap returns
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrSampling\ for paragliders and
\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErrSampling\ for hang gliders; refitting each half of the fitted
window and halving the difference between the two exponents returns
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrRange\ and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErrRange. The sampling term
is negligible against the range term in both disciplines, and the quoted
$\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ and $\pm\StatVarHangAlphaOrderTwoErr$ are the two combined
in quadrature --- the range term with the sampling term invisible inside it. The same
construction gives \StatVarParaAlphaOrderOneErrRange\ at order one and
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderThreeErrRange\ at order three, growing with the order of the filter as
a higher difference of a noisier curve should: the second decimal of the exponent is not
determined, and a number carrying the bootstrap alone would claim four significant figures
on a curve that does not have two.

A third figure, the standard error least squares returns on the fitted slope, measures
something else: how well a \emph{straight line} describes the fitted points, treating each
residual as an independent draw from a common noise. The residuals of these curves are
neither independent (every lag averages the same flights) nor noise (the local slope drifts
across the fitted decades), so that figure is dominated by the curve's departure from a
power law rather than by sampling, and is not an interval on the exponent
\impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{What sets the two ends of the window.}
Below, the smoothing window is $\max(\tau_c,\,\PreprocSavgolMinWindow\,\Delta t)$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}): its floor is in \emph{samples}, so in seconds it widens with the
logger's step. \SI{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\second} therefore sits above it for
\SI{\StatVarParaSmoothedBelowFitPct}{\percent} of retained paraglider flights and
\SI{\StatVarHangSmoothedBelowFitPct}{\percent} of hang-glider ones; the slow remainder is
smoothed over more than the shortest fitted lag, and enters the average with the few
windows so sparse a record supplies.

Above, the declared task governs: closed and open courses separate in
local slope by \num{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapLow} at
\SI{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapLowS}{\second} and by \num{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapHigh} at
\SI{\StatVarParaTaskSlopeGapHighS}{\second} for paragliders, from
\num{\StatVarHangTaskSlopeGapLow} to \num{\StatVarHangTaskSlopeGapHigh} for hang gliders
(Figure~\ref{fig:transport}), growing smoothly with no lag at which the measurement becomes
clean; the ceiling \SI{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second} is set where that separation is still a
small fraction of what it reaches, leaving a window \StatVarParaFitDecades\ decades wide.

\paragraph{That ceiling belongs to the task, not to the air.}
Restricted to the open courses, where no course comes home by construction, the same
order-1 fit runs to \SI{\StatVarParaOpenFitMaxS}{\second} without the curve bending under
it. For paragliders $\alpha_1$ moves by \StatVarParaOpenAlphaOrderOneShift\ between the two
windows, from \StatVarParaOpenAlphaOrderOneNarrow\ to \StatVarParaOpenAlphaOrderOneWide,
and the rms departure from the fitted line grows only from
\StatVarParaOpenResidOrderOneNarrow\ to \StatVarParaOpenResidOrderOneWide\ dex. The closed
courses do the opposite over the same lags, moving
\StatVarParaClosedAlphaOrderOneShift\ and degrading to
\StatVarParaClosedResidOrderOneWide\ dex from \StatVarParaClosedResidOrderOneNarrow. Over
the stretch the wider window adds, the open local slope stays between
\StatVarParaOpenSlopeAddedMin\ and \StatVarParaOpenSlopeAddedMax, and
\num{\StatVarParaOpenFlightsWideLag} open flights still answer
\SI{\StatVarParaOpenWideLagS}{\second}, so the added decade is measured rather than
extrapolated: for paragliders the window is \StatVarParaOpenFitDecades\ decades wide once
the scoring rule is out of the sample, against \StatVarParaFitDecades\ pooled.

Hang gliders do not earn it. The shift is of the same size,
\StatVarHangOpenAlphaOrderOneShift, but the open local slope runs from
\StatVarHangOpenSlopeAddedMin\ to \StatVarHangOpenSlopeAddedMax\ across the added stretch
and only \num{\StatVarHangOpenFlightsWideLag} flights reach its far end, so the pooled
ceiling stands for that discipline and the wider window is quoted for paragliders alone.

The task gap at order two, $\StatVarParaTaskGapOrderTwo$, and the range dependence,
\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErrRange, are the same size and plausibly the same effect: both
measure how much of the fitted exponent is fixed by the long end of the window. The quoted
$\pm\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwoErr$ already carries this systematic through its range half, so
entering the task stratum as a further term would count the scoring rule twice. For hang

interval is left as the range term alone.

\paragraph{How many exponents the range supports.}
A piecewise-linear fit to $\log V_2$ against $\log\Delta$, with $k$ breaks scored by a
Bayesian information criterion of the standard form,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BIC}(k) = n_{\mathrm{eff}}\log\!\big(\mathrm{RSS}_k/n_{\mathrm{eff}}\big)
    + p_k\log n_{\mathrm{eff}},
  \label{eq:bic-breaks}
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{RSS}_k$ the fit's residual sum of squares and $p_k$ the number of free parameters
at $k$ breaks, but with $n_{\mathrm{eff}}=\StatVarParaDof$ the same effective degrees of
freedom used throughout this chapter substituted for the \num{\StatVarParaFitLags} nominal
lags, so a curve whose residuals are correlated cannot buy a break with points it does not
independently have.

This selects \StatVarParaBreaks\ break for paragliders at
\SI{\StatVarParaBreakOneS}{\second} ---
separating \StatVarParaSlopeLow\ from \StatVarParaSlopeHigh, bracketing the single-exponent
value by about the size of the range dependence --- and \StatVarHangBreaks\ for hang
gliders. Run on surrogates that are a single power law plus the measured sampling noise, the
same procedure invents a break \SI{\StatVarParaFalsePositivePct}{\percent} and
\SI{\StatVarHangFalsePositivePct}{\percent} of the time \impldetails{impl:global}, too often
for either count to be trusted: the correlation across lags puts \num{\StatVarParaFitLags}
lags at no more than \StatVarParaDof\ effective points, a budget for one exponent and not for
a count of regimes.
\end{revblock}

\section{The exponent from the increment quantiles}
\label{sec:transport-propagator}
\begin{revblock}
Every exponent quoted so far is a second moment of a displacement, and this archive has
already shown what a second moment can measure instead of the motion: averaged about a
common origin, it returned the geometry of the launch site. What is wanted is a route to
the exponent that uses neither a moment nor a common origin.


\paragraph{The propagator of the increments.}
Write $\Delta\mathbf{r}(t_0,\Delta)=\mathbf{r}(t_0+\Delta)-\mathbf{r}(t_0)$ for the
horizontal increment over a lag $\Delta$, inside a retained segment and never across the
gap that ends one (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). Pooled over starting times and flights, the
distribution of its modulus $r=|\Delta\mathbf{r}|$ is the propagator of the increments, and
for a self-similar process with stationary increments it takes the scaling form
\begin{equation}
  P(r,\Delta)=\Delta^{-H}\,F\!\left(\frac{r}{\Delta^{H}}\right),
  \label{eq:increment-scaling}
\end{equation}
with $F$ a fixed density independent of the lag. Every quantile of $r$ then grows as
$\Delta^{H}$: writing $q_p(\Delta)$ for the $p$th quantile, $\log q_p$ against
$\log\Delta$ is a straight line of slope $H$ at every $p$, fixed by the middle of the
distribution where every flight contributes and a heavy tail moves it not at all.

Neither
of the two defects of the earlier estimates enters here: no moment for a few large
increments to set, and no absolute position for the launch site to bias. The four levels
read are the lower quartile, the median, the upper quartile and the ninetieth percentile,
from one streaming pass keeping a histogram per lag and per native cadence
\impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{Why the modulus, and what the components are still for.}
The exponent has to be measured on the same quantity as the estimators it is being checked
against. The ensemble MSD averages $E^2+N^2$ and the filtered variation sums the squared
difference over both components to stay invariant under a rotation of the frame
(Sec.~\ref{sec:variations}); both are statements about $|\Delta\mathbf{r}|$, so only a
modulus exponent can be set beside them in Table~\ref{tab:threeroutes}. Reading $H$ off one
component and comparing it with those would be comparing two different quantities, and on
this ensemble they are numerically different as well.

That is not the same as pooling the two components into one histogram, which would be the
error Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} forbids: pooling presupposes that $E$ and $N$ share a
distribution, and they do not. The modulus presupposes nothing --- it is a scalar built
from both, and it is the scalar the other two estimators already use. The components are
therefore measured separately as well, and kept apart, because their disagreement is itself
a result: it is reported below and carried into Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} as a constraint. So
the pass histograms all three of $|\Delta x|$, $|\Delta y|$ and $|\Delta\mathbf{r}|$; the
modulus answers what the transport exponent is, and the components answer whether one
exponent can stand for both directions.

\paragraph{What it returns.}
$H=\StatPropParaHurst$ for paragliders and $\StatPropHangHurst$ for hang gliders, over
\SIrange{\StatPropParaFitMinS}{\StatPropParaFitMaxS}{\second}, from
\num{\StatPropParaIncrements} and \num{\StatPropHangIncrements} increments in
\num{\StatPropParaCadences} and \num{\StatPropHangCadences} native cadence classes ---
doubled into the units the filtered variation is quoted in, $\alpha=\StatPropParaAlpha$ and
$\StatPropHangAlpha$. Across the paraglider cadence classes, spanning a factor of ten in
smoothing scale, the estimate moves between \StatPropParaHurstMin\ and
\StatPropParaHurstMax, so the smoothing scale does not set it.

Table~\ref{tab:threeroutes} sets the quantile exponent beside the two second moments. They

measured by the order scan and read here from the other side, and it fixes which row a

taken in different frames and one statistic that is no moment at all, none of them measured
from take-off.

\paragraph{What the quantiles cost the scaling form.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:increment-scaling} claims the four quantiles share a slope. They do not:
fitted separately, $H$ falls from \StatPropParaHurstBulk\ at the lower quartile and the

and at every native cadence measured (Figure~\ref{fig:propagator}b draws the three
variables at \SI{\StatPropParaDrawnDtS}{\second}).

cadence, each with an error that resamples whole day-and-site clusters rather than trusting
the lags of the fit to be independent points. That error is small,
\StatPropParaHurstQuantileErrMax\ at worst for paragliders and
\StatPropHangHurstQuantileErrMax\ for hang gliders, because \num{\StatPropParaDrawnBlocks}
blocks of day-and-site clusters over an archive this size leave almost nothing for the
sampling to move, not because the estimator is forgiving. Against it the spread down the
column is \num{145} and \num{24} times larger: the quantiles disagreeing is not sampling
noise, and no error bar of this kind will make it one.

The naive alternative reads the other way. The least-squares error of the same fit is
\StatPropParaDrawnHurstErrOls\ and \StatPropHangDrawnHurstErrOls, larger than the resampled
one by up to a factor of twenty, but that is not the resampling being optimistic: an
ordinary least-squares error on a log--log fit measures how far the points sit off a
straight line, and here they sit off it because the relation is genuinely a little
curved --- the finding of this section, not an uncertainty about it --- and because it also
treats lags built from the same flights as independent observations, which they are not.
So neither number licenses three figures on the exponent.

What limits it is not the
sampling, negligible on an archive this size, but the systematic spread: across the
quantiles here, across native cadences (\StatPropParaHurstMin\ to \StatPropParaHurstMax\
for paragliders), and with the fitted range of Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-measure}. The
spread across the four quantiles is \StatPropParaQuantileSpread\ and
\StatPropHangQuantileSpread, against \StatPropCalibSpread\ on an exactly self-similar
process fitted the same way with less data \impldetails{impl:global}, and it is wider on
the modulus than on either component (\StatPropParaQuantileSpreadEast\ east and
\StatPropParaQuantileSpreadNorth\ north for paragliders, \StatPropHangQuantileSpreadEast\
and \StatPropHangQuantileSpreadNorth\ for hang gliders, Figure~\ref{fig:propagator}b) ---
expected rather than a surprise, since a small $|\Delta x|$ is not a small displacement
when the wing may be going due north: a component's lower quantiles mix barely moving with
moving across the axis being read and flatten the drift by averaging the two, while the
modulus keeps them apart.

Rescaling the histograms by $\Delta^{H}$ leaves \StatPropParaCollapseDex\ and
\StatPropHangCollapseDex\ dex of scatter through the bulk, a factor of
\StatPropParaCollapseFactor\ and \StatPropHangCollapseFactor\ in the rescaled density, close
enough that the curves read as one by eye, with visible separation only at the flanks
(Figure~\ref{fig:propagator}c). The scaling form is therefore only approximate: the centre
of the increment distribution spreads faster than its flanks, a shape that changes with
scale rather than staying self-similar. That qualifies the exponent rather than refuting
it --- bulk and flank sit closer to each other than either does to the Brownian value, and
no reading of these curves puts the transport near it --- but it is a second reason,
independent of the range dependence of $\alpha_2$, why a single exponent to three figures
would claim more than this archive supports.

\section{The archive is not a L\'evy walk}
\label{sec:transport-shape}
\begin{revblock}
An exponent fixes one moment of one distribution, and two processes can agree on it and be
different physics. A correlated Gaussian motion and a L\'evy walk both give
$\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^2\rangle\sim\Delta^{2H}$; what separates them is the rest of the
moment spectrum. It is measured on the same unsegmented trajectories, with no estimator in
common with the exponent itself, and it refuses the L\'evy walk.

\paragraph{The moment spectrum.}
Write
\begin{equation}
  \big\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}(\Delta)|^{q}\big\rangle\sim\Delta^{\,q\nu(q)}
  \label{eq:moment-spectrum}
\end{equation}
and read $q\nu(q)$ as a function of $q$. A self-similar monofractal process gives a straight
line through the origin with $\nu(q)$ constant; a L\'evy walk --- flight durations drawn
from a power-law tail, each traversed at finite speed --- gives a bilinear one, with a knee
at its tail index and a ballistic right-hand branch of slope one.

Over
\SIrange{\StatShapeParaFitMinS}{\StatShapeParaFitMaxS}{\second}, on \num{\StatShapeParaLags}
lags and $q$ from \StatShapeParaQMin\ to \StatShapeParaQMax, the spectrum is straight
(Figure~\ref{fig:shape}a): $\nu(q)$ varies by \StatShapeParaNuSpread\ for paragliders and
\StatShapeHangNuSpread\ for hang gliders about a single fitted $\nu=\StatShapeParaLinearSlope$
and $\StatShapeHangLinearSlope$, and the rms departure of $q\nu(q)$ from a line through the
origin, \StatShapeParaLinearDeparture\ and \StatShapeHangLinearDeparture, sits an order of
magnitude below what a L\'evy walk's knee leaves on this grid and range of $q$
\impldetails{impl:global}.

The moments are estimable rather than merely computed: the share
of each moment carried by the largest hundredth of its increments reaches
\SI{\StatShapeParaTailMaxPct}{\percent} at worst for paragliders and
\SI{\StatShapeHangTailMaxPct}{\percent} for hang gliders (Figure~\ref{fig:shape}b), well
short of the half that would make a moment a statement about a few flights rather than an
estimate.

On the whole trajectory, unsegmented, the archive does not support the L\'evy walk
this thesis was framed around, and what the spectrum leaves standing is a monofractal
process: a single exponent governing every moment over the range read.

The previous section's quantile fit found that $H$ falls from bulk to flank rather than
holding a single slope, which looks in tension with a straight spectrum. The two are
compatible: a moment of order $q$ integrates the whole increment distribution, so a local
exponent drifting between centre and flanks enters $\nu(q)$ weighted by where that moment
takes its mass, and the drift partly cancels, while a quantile fit reads the collapse at a
fixed probability level and averages nothing. The quantile spread
(\StatPropParaQuantileSpread, \StatPropHangQuantileSpread) is accordingly the smaller of the
two figures against the spread of $\nu(q)$ (\StatShapeParaNuSpread, \StatShapeHangNuSpread),
though only the ordering is established, not a derived bound; the refusal of the L\'evy walk
is untouched either way, since the departure a knee would leave exceeds both.

\paragraph{The drift runs the wrong way for a L\'evy walk.}
Its \emph{direction} points the same way as the spectrum. A L\'evy walk carries a ballistic
front, so its largest displacements grow with the lag faster than its bulk does and the
flank exponent would sit above the bulk one. The measured ordering is the reverse, $H$
falling from \StatPropParaHurstBulk\ at bulk to \StatPropParaHurstFlank\ at flank for
paragliders and from \StatPropHangHurstBulk\ to \StatPropHangHurstFlank\ for hang gliders.
The ninetieth percentile is not the front, whose weight decays with the lag, so this
corroborates the moment spectrum rather than testing the walk a second time.
\end{revblock}

\section{The propagator is not Gaussian}
\label{sec:transport-gaussian}
\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{The estimator: excess kurtosis, not an ad hoc ratio.}
A straight moment spectrum says that one exponent governs every moment of the increment
distribution. It fixes how that distribution \emph{scales} and says nothing about its
\emph{shape}, so calling the process a correlated Gaussian motion does not follow from it.
Gaussianity is a question about kurtosis, and the increment here is a two-dimensional
vector rather than a scalar, so the estimator is Mardia's multivariate kurtosis excess of
the joint $(\Delta x,\Delta y)$ distribution~\cite{mardia1970},
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_2^{\mathrm{NG}}(\Delta) = \mathbb{E}\!\big[Q(\Delta)^2\big] - d(d+2),
  \qquad Q=\Delta\mathbf{r}^{\!\top}\hat\Sigma^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{r},\quad d=2,
  \label{eq:nongauss}
\end{equation}
$Q$ the squared Mahalanobis distance under the pooled sample covariance $\hat\Sigma$ of the
increment. Every bivariate Gaussian gives $\alpha_2^{\mathrm{NG}}=0$, whatever its
covariance --- unlike a plain ratio of moments, which is only zero for an \emph{isotropic}
Gaussian and would need correcting on an archive Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} already measures as
anisotropic. The superscript keeps it apart from the filtered-variation exponent $\alpha_2$,
an unrelated quantity that happens to share the symbol at filter order $p=2$.

\paragraph{An interior maximum, and the one scale in the chapter.}
Over \SIrange{\StatShapeParaNonGaussMinS}{\StatShapeParaNonGaussMaxS}{\second} the median
value is \StatShapeParaNonGaussMedian\ for paragliders and \StatShapeHangNonGaussMedian\ for
hang gliders. Neither is flat: both rise to an interior maximum and fall back ---
\StatShapeParaNonGaussPeak\ at \SI{\StatShapeParaNonGaussPeakS}{\second} for paragliders and
\StatShapeHangNonGaussPeak\ at \SI{\StatShapeHangNonGaussPeakS}{\second} for hang gliders,
monotone over seven or more consecutive lags on each side, a shape and not scatter. A
maximum at two to four hundred seconds is a scale the chapter has otherwise found none of:
smaller below it (increments are averages over a smoothing window and a few heading
changes) and above it (a central-limit Gaussianisation begins to act). The two archives do
not overlap at any lag in the window, acting more strongly on the smaller, faster
discipline by the ratio of the two median ground speeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-kinematics}).

\paragraph{The two components, read separately.}
A single component is a scalar, so the classical univariate excess kurtosis applies to it
directly: $\langle\Delta x^4\rangle/\langle\Delta x^2\rangle^2-3$, the same construction as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nongauss} at $d=1$, reported per component the way
Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-axisroutes} reports every other estimator, since pooling
presupposes an isotropy this archive lacks. For paragliders the median is
\StatShapeParaKurtEastMedian\ east and \StatShapeParaKurtNorthMedian\ north: the north
component alone is \emph{sub}-Gaussian while the joint distribution is not, since a
component's kurtosis and the joint Mardia excess are different statistics of the same
increments and need not agree in sign. Hang gliders move the same way but do not cross
zero: \StatShapeHangKurtEastMedian\ east and \StatShapeHangKurtNorthMedian\ north. The
anisotropy Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} measures in amplitude and Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator}
measures in exponent reaches the shape of the propagator as well.

\paragraph{The pooled parameter is a width mixture, not a tail.}
The value is pooled by construction: the moments are accumulated over every flight before
any ratio is taken, so it is a property of the whole population of increments and not of
any flight's own propagator. Writing $S_f$ for a flight's own second moment (its trace,
$\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^2\rangle$) and $a_f$ for its own Mardia excess, pooling a
population of flights that are each exactly Gaussian but differ in amplitude gives
\begin{equation}
  d(d+2) + \alpha^{\mathrm{NG}}_{\text{pooled}}
    = d(d+2)\,\frac{\mathbb{E}_n\!\left[(1 + a_f/d(d+2))\,S_f^{2}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_n[S_f]^{2}} ,
  \label{eq:pooled-nongauss}
\end{equation}
an identity rather than an approximation, with the expectations weighted by the number of
windows each flight supplies. Every flight is exactly Gaussian when $a_f=0$: setting it so
leaves $d(d+2)\big(1+\mathrm{CV}^2(S_f)\big)$, the excess a population of Gaussian flights
of differing amplitude produces on its own, a scale-mixture null verified against synthetic
anisotropic, correlated Gaussian data before being trusted here \impldetails{impl:global}.
At $d=2$ that null is $8\,\mathrm{CV}^2(S_f)$, and it is \emph{above} the measurement at
every one of \num{\StatShapeParaNullLags} lags for paragliders and
\num{\StatShapeHangNullLags} for hang gliders. Subtracting it, the within-flight excess is
\emph{negative}: \StatShapeParaWithinFlight\ and \StatShapeHangWithinFlight\ in the median,
so a flight's own increment distribution is flatter than Gaussian, not heavy-tailed, which
is what a wing holding a characteristic airspeed should give.

This changes what the number means rather
than what it is: the pooled propagator genuinely is not Gaussian, because a mixture of
Gaussians of differing width is not one, but the excess is the width mixture and not a tail,
and what has to be reproduced is the between-flight amplitude spread,
$\mathrm{CV}(S_f)=\StatShapeParaAmplitudeCv$ against $\StatShapeHangAmplitudeCv$ at
\SI{\StatShapeParaNullFloorS}{\second}. The refusal of the L\'evy walk is untouched, resting
on the moment spectrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:shape}a), measured per lag and not pooled across
flights of different amplitude; a Mardia excess has no single value on a synthetic L\'evy
walk to compare against either, since it turns negative at short lag and swings by an order
of magnitude between seeds at long lag on the same protocol \impldetails{impl:global} ---
a heavy-tailed process the fourth moment itself struggles to certify, which is exactly why
that question is decided by the spectrum and not asked twice of this panel.

Beyond the fitted range $\alpha_2^{\mathrm{NG}}$ climbs, to \StatShapeParaNonGaussBeyond\ and
\StatShapeHangNonGaussBeyond\ at the longest lags of panel~(c): reported and not quoted,
since there the declared task governs the trajectory and the moment is carried by the fewest
flights. The process is monofractal and its propagator is not Gaussian: one exponent for
every moment fixes how the increment distribution scales, and leaves the shape it scales as
heavy as it is measured to be.
\end{revblock}

\section{The memory of the heading}
\label{sec:transport-memory}
\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{The estimator.}
Writing $\delta\mathbf{v}$ for the horizontal velocity fluctuation about a record's own
mean, the velocity autocorrelation is
\begin{equation}
  C(\tau)=\frac{\bigl\langle\,\delta\mathbf{v}(t_0)\cdot\delta\mathbf{v}(t_0+\tau)\,\bigr\rangle_{t_0}}
               {\bigl\langle\,|\delta\mathbf{v}(t_0)|^{2}\,\bigr\rangle_{t_0}},
  \label{eq:vacf}
\end{equation}
normalised so that $C(0)=1$, with the flown course, which would otherwise hold every lag
up, removed before the average is taken. It keeps to the same segment rule as before, is
computed by the Fourier identity rather than by a double loop, and is averaged across
records at each lag of the grid the moments use \impldetails{impl:global}.

\paragraph{Positive at every lag, and small at the first.}
$C$ is positive throughout Figure~\ref{fig:shape} and changes sign nowhere on the grid:
$C=\StatShapeParaVacfAtFloor$ for paragliders at the floor and $\StatShapeHangVacfAtFloor$
for hang gliders, decaying from there without a zero crossing. A single circling wing would
produce a sign change, its velocity pointing the other way half a turn later, but an ensemble
of them does not: radius and phase disperse across flights, so the average of many circles
cannot go negative even when every record in it turns.

What survives an average is the
shape, not the sign --- a minimum at half a period and a maximum at the period --- and both
are there, below the first lag Figure~\ref{fig:shape} plots. Measured at native cadence on the
\SI{1}{\hertz} segments, $C$ falls to \StatCirclingParaTrough\ at
\SI{\StatCirclingParaTroughS}{\second} and recovers to \StatCirclingParaCrest\ at
\SI{\StatCirclingParaCrestS}{\second} for paragliders, and to \StatCirclingHangTrough\ at
\SI{\StatCirclingHangTroughS}{\second} and \StatCirclingHangCrest\ at
\SI{\StatCirclingHangCrestS}{\second} for hang gliders \impldetails{impl:global}, the minimum
sitting at half the maximum's lag in both disciplines --- the circle, not a coincidence of the
estimator, and nowhere near zero. So the unsegmented ensemble already carries the circling,
with a period of \SIrange{\StatCirclingHangCrestS}{\StatCirclingParaCrestS}{\second}, which a
phase decomposition in Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} can be held to rather than asked to invent.

And $C$ is already small at the bottom of the fitted grid, falling below $1/e$ by
\SI{\StatShapeParaVacfDecayS}{\second} for paragliders and
\SI{\StatShapeHangVacfDecayS}{\second} for hang gliders, within its first lags --- a property
of the pipeline as much as of the flying, since the grid starts at the smoothing scale of the
slowest logger retained (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}).

\paragraph{Integrability, not size, sustains the exponent.}
A memory spent by \SI{\StatVarParaFitMinS}{\second} looks incompatible with increments still
correlated to \SI{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second}, but it is not the size of $C$ that sets the
growth law. For a stationary velocity the Green--Kubo relation
\begin{equation}
  \bigl\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}(\Delta)|^{2}\bigr\rangle
  =2\!\int_{0}^{\Delta}\!(\Delta-\tau)\,C(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau
  \label{eq:green-kubo}
\end{equation}
makes the growth law a statement about the convergence of an integral: a tail
$C(\tau)\sim\tau^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma<1$ has no finite integral and gives
$\langle|\Delta\mathbf{r}|^{2}\rangle\sim\Delta^{\,2-\gamma}$ across the window however small
$C$ has already become, while $\gamma>1$ gives diffusive motion beyond the correlation time
whatever $C$ is worth at the floor; multiplying $C$ by a constant moves every value on it and
leaves $\gamma$ where it was.

A single power law fitted to
\SIrange{\StatShapeParaVacfTailMinS}{\StatShapeParaVacfTailMaxS}{\second} gives
$\gamma=\StatShapeParaVacfTailGamma$ for paragliders and \StatShapeHangVacfTailGamma\ for
hang gliders, but that fit is itself an arch, the local slope running from
\StatShapeParaVacfGammaLocalMin\ to \StatShapeParaVacfGammaLocalMax\ across the same lags.
What the argument needs is not the fitted value: \emph{every} local slope is below unity, in
both disciplines and at all \num{\StatShapeParaVacfTailLags} lags, so non-integrability holds
pointwise rather than on a fitted average --- the stronger statement, and one that does not
depend on the tail being a power law at all \impldetails{impl:global}.

Two features of the
estimate push $\gamma$ up rather than down (a mean velocity estimated from the same record
pulls the tail towards zero, and the first decade of the integral in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:green-kubo} lies below the grid), so the exponent $2-\gamma$ implied for the
displacement is a floor rather than an estimate --- \StatShapeParaVacfImpliedAlphaFloor\ and
\StatShapeHangVacfImpliedAlphaFloor\ --- and the plain increment,
$\alpha_1=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderOne$ and \StatVarHangAlphaOrderOne, clears both floors: a
consistency between two channels, not a second determination, that excludes a velocity
memory integrable over this window.

\paragraph{The turning angles.}
The persistence has a microscopic form: the angle between successive horizontal displacement
vectors, taken at a stride of \num{\StatKinParaAngleStrideSamples} samples so the smoothing
window shares no sample between the two vectors \impldetails{impl:global}. Its median is
\SI{\StatKinParaAngleMedianDeg}{\degree} for paragliders, over \num{\StatKinParaAngles}
angles, and \SI{\StatKinHangAngleMedianDeg}{\degree} for hang gliders, with
\SI{\StatKinParaAngleForwardPct}{\percent} and \SI{\StatKinHangAngleForwardPct}{\percent}
falling within $\pi/6$ of the previous heading; restricted to matched \SI{1}{\hertz} segments
the medians are \SI{\StatKinParaAngleMedianOneHzDeg}{\degree} and
\SI{\StatKinHangAngleMedianOneHzDeg}{\degree}, so the gap between disciplines survives
matching, losing about a quarter of its size. Memoryless reorientation, a right-angle median,
is nowhere near.

A wing that changes heading by \SI{\StatKinParaAngleMedianDeg}{\degree} at a
time is a correlated walk, and a positive $C(\tau)$, a non-integrable tail, and increments
still correlated at \SI{\StatVarParaFitMaxS}{\second} are the ensemble consequences of the
same forward peak --- the warrant for the persistent random walk of Sec.~\ref{sec:modelling}.
The angles outside the forward cone are a minority of the same order as the climbing share of
Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim} (\SI{\StatKinParaClimbingPct}{\percent} for paragliders,
\SI{\StatKinHangClimbingPct}{\percent} for hang gliders): pooled, climbing and gliding give
one forward-peaked distribution and a $C(\tau)$ with no sign change, which is why the
circling is invisible to both statistics here and not absent from the flying. The
segmentation of Sec.~\ref{sec:segmentation} separates them, and the turning angles
conditioned on phase are where this result has to reappear.
\end{revblock}

\flow{Writing $f_\uparrow$ for the climbing
fraction of a flight and $\langle\dot z\rangle_\uparrow$, $\langle\dot z\rangle_\downarrow$
for the mean climb and sink rates, a flight that ends near the altitude it began at has
$\int\dot z\,\mathrm{d}t\approx0$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  f_\uparrow\,\langle\dot z\rangle_\uparrow
  + (1-f_\uparrow)\,\langle\dot z\rangle_\downarrow \approx 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \frac{f_\uparrow}{1-f_\uparrow} \approx
  \frac{|\langle\dot z\rangle_\downarrow|}{\langle\dot z\rangle_\uparrow},
  \label{eq:climb-balance}
\end{equation}
the climbing share required near a half whenever the two rates are comparable.}

Over the
median flight of \SI{\StatPrelimParaMedianDurH}{\hour} and \SI{\StatPrelimHangMedianDurH}{\hour}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}) both measured shares fall just short of a half --- the same fact as
the negative median, since the wing spends more of its time sinking and its mean rate of
climb exceeds its mean rate of sink. The sign of the vertical velocity is the coarsest
climb--glide rule available, so these two shares are what a segmentation in
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} must return as its threshold goes to zero.

\paragraph{The return probability.}
The return probability, the increment propagator at its centre
$P_0(\Delta)\equiv P(\mathbf{0},\Delta)$, decays as $\Delta^{-H}$ under the scaling form of
Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator} and so is another reading of $H$, at a fifth probability
level costing a fit and no further traversal of the fix table; it is defined here and not
measured. First-passage times are not on this list either: they were measured and withdrawn
in Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator}, since a time to reach a radius from take-off carries
the launch geometry into the space domain.
\end{revblock}

\paragraph{The limits.}
The window is \StatVarParaFitDecades\ decades wide, and neither end is set by the size of
the archive: below it the smoothing floor of Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol} governs, above it the
declared task (Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-measure}). Inside the window the scaling form is
only approximate (Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator}), and the fitted exponent moves with
the range by more than it moves under resampling (Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-measure}): each
of the two is on its own enough to withhold three-figure precision, so that transport is
characterised by $\alpha=\StatVarParaAlphaOrderTwo$ is a different, stronger statement than
the effective exponent quoted above. The window will not widen with more data: a soaring
day ends, so the upper cutoff follows from the system rather than the method.

\paragraph{What was withdrawn.}
Four measurements are withdrawn, each kept as a diagnostic rather than a result.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), itemsep=3pt, topsep=5pt]
  \item \emph{The ensemble MSD about take-off} yields a timescale instead of an exponent:
        displacement from the launch site crosses over where the population leaves its
        launch area (Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global}).
  \item \emph{The first-passage time}, brought in as a route to $H$ in the space domain,
        was withdrawn when its exponent moved across sub-ranges of radius by more than the
        uncertainty it was meant to check, but it did show that the ensemble curve's failure
        travels with the common origin rather than the time axis, since exchanging time for
        radius reproduces it (Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator}) \impldetails{impl:global}.
  \item \emph{The sign-run count on the MSD residuals} is kept as a description of how those
        residuals are arranged and withdrawn as a test, every lag of that curve being an
        average over the same flights; the size of the residual against the standard error
        on each lag carries the claim instead.
  \item \emph{A straight moment spectrum} is not read as a Gaussian propagator: it states
        how the distribution scales and nothing about its shape, which the non-Gaussian
        parameter measures instead, from moments the spectrum had already accumulated
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-gaussian}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The hand-over.}
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} inherits a class of transport and five constraints on how it may be
decomposed.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Any statistic measured from take-off inherits the launch geometry, out to the
        departure times above, and the contamination is not confined to the observable that
        exposed it: any phase statistic binned by elapsed time since take-off carries it
        below those times. The remedy is the one used throughout this chapter, to measure
        within a segment and never about a common origin (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).
  \item The estimator is additive over any partition of the sample: $V_2(\Delta)$ is a mean
        over the placements of a second difference, and labelling those placements by phase
        and recombining the per-phase means with the weights actually observed returns the
        pooled curve identically. That is a property of the estimator rather than of the
        transport, asserting no decomposition of an exponent into phase exponents, only that
        the arithmetic closes; a recombination that misses the pooled value is evidence
        about the segmentation and is to be read that way before it is read as physics.
  \item The two horizontal components are analysed separately and never pooled: they are
        incompatible in amplitude (Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}) and differ in exponent
        (Table~\ref{tab:fingerprint}), and pooling presupposes an isotropy this ensemble
        does not have. Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-axisroutes} puts the same split through the
        time average and both filtered-variation orders and finds the same ordering; the
        pooled quantile route is not the only estimator that sees it.
  \item The pooled non-Gaussian parameter (Table~\ref{tab:fingerprint}) sits, against a
        matched Gaussian null, \emph{below} it rather than above. What is handed on is
        therefore a between-flight amplitude spread and not an increment tail
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-gaussian}): a phase decomposition has to reproduce that
        spread across records, and must not produce a heavy tail within one.
  \item The ergodicity question is handed on already measured, in two forms that needed no
        power law: the drift of the exponent with the time already flown, obtained by
        running the same within-segment estimator on successive stretches of a record
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:segmentation}); and the ergodicity-breaking parameter of
        Appendix~\ref{app:ctrw}, measured at a lag held fixed
        (\SI{\StatErgoParaLagS}{\second}) and swept over the duration required of a flight,
        $\mathrm{EB}(T)$ falling from \StatErgoParaAtOneH\ to \StatErgoParaAtFourH\ for
        paragliders and from \StatErgoHangAtOneH\ to \StatErgoHangAtFourH\ for hang
        gliders as $T$ runs
        \SIrange{\StatErgoParaRangeMinH}{\StatErgoParaRangeMaxH}{\hour}
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global}), falling but not reaching zero over the range this
        archive can answer: pooled, the archive is not shown to be ergodic. What is handed
        on is not that finding but a narrower one, whether the scatter belongs to one phase
        or is spread across all of them, on the same parameter computed per phase rather
        than pooled. The usual
        comparison of $\alpha_{\mathrm{EA}}$ with $\alpha_{\mathrm{TA}}$ requires both
        curves to be power laws, and this archive supplies one of the two.
\end{enumerate}

The class of transport is fixed over one window; which flight behaviour produces it is not.
Every number above mixes climbing, gliding and searching into one, and the persistent random
walk of Sec.~\ref{sec:modelling} takes its warrant from the memory measured here rather than
from any single one of them. Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} separates the phases, and is answerable
to the five constraints.
\end{revblock}
