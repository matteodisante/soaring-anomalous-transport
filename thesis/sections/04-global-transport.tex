\chapter{Preliminary global transport}
\label{ch:global}

Chapter~\ref{ch:observables} defined displacement growth for the complete archive. This
chapter introduces the drift-removing second difference and turns to the distributional
structure of displacement: whether displacement quantiles rescale together, how
components and joint laws depend on direction, how flight duration and equipment
composition shape the result, and what these measurements constrain before flight phases
are assigned in Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}.
Table~\ref{tab:chapter3-populations} in Chapter~\ref{ch:observables} lists the flight
populations used throughout both chapters.

A first section removes constant velocity with a second difference and sets route
closure against it. Quantiles then test scalar rescaling, with a fixed-population
control, before the component and joint laws address vector scaling and dependence, and
time-averaged curves are grouped by region and initial altitude. Duration and equipment
composition then check how the observed growth depends on the selected flights. A final section states
the resulting constraints for the phase analysis in Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}.

\section{Drift removal and route closure}
\label{sec:drift-closure}

\subsection{Removing a constant velocity}
\label{sec:variations}

A constant velocity contributes a quadratic term to an uncentred MSD. If
$\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf v_d t+\mathbf Y(t)$ and the residual increment has zero mean, then
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E|\mathbf X(t,\tau)|^2
   =|\mathbf v_d|^2\tau^2+
      \mathbb E|\mathbf Y(t+\tau)-\mathbf Y(t)|^2.
  \label{eq:drift-msd}
\end{equation}
Without the zero-mean assumption there is also a cross term. The two contributions add
as functions of lag; their fitted exponents do not add.

A useful control is the second difference,
\begin{align}
  A_2\mathbf r(t;\tau)
    &=\mathbf r(t+2\tau)-2\mathbf r(t+\tau)+\mathbf r(t),
       \label{eq:filter}\\
  V_2(\tau)&=\left\langle|A_2\mathbf r(t;\tau)|^2\right\rangle.
       \label{eq:variation}
\end{align}
Since $A_2(\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_d t)=0$, this observable is exactly insensitive to a
constant velocity. It avoids estimating drift from the endpoints, which would impose a
return constraint on the residual trajectory. Its cost is a window of length $2\tau$.
We retain it for this specific purpose, alongside $V_1(\tau)=\langle R^2\rangle$.

To relate the second-difference slope to $H$, suppose the residual is a centred
finite-variance process with stationary increments and
$\mathbb E|\mathbf Y(t+\tau)-\mathbf Y(t)|^2=K\tau^{2H}$. Writing adjacent increments
as $\mathbf I_1$ and $\mathbf I_2$ gives
$2\mathbb E[\mathbf I_1\cdot\mathbf I_2]=K[(2\tau)^{2H}-2\tau^{2H}]$, hence
\begin{equation}
  V_2(\tau)=K\bigl(4-2^{2H}\bigr)\tau^{2H}.
  \label{eq:variation-hurst}
\end{equation}
For $K>0$ and $0<H<1$, half the slope of $V_2$ estimates $H$ under these
assumptions; fractional Brownian motion is one example~\cite{mandelbrot1968}.
At $H=1$, the prefactor vanishes and the logarithmic slope is undefined.

On a smooth curved trajectory,
$A_2\mathbf r=\ddot{\mathbf r}\,\tau^2+O(\tau^3)$, so $V_2\propto\tau^4$ at sufficiently
small lag. Half that slope is not the Hurst exponent of a stationary-increment position
process. Agreement between differences of order two and three is a consistency check;
it does not prove that all environmental trends have disappeared. In particular,
$b_1-b_2$ is not a measured fraction of transport due to wind or course geometry.

The measured $b_2$ values near or above two, together with the curvature in
Fig.~\ref{fig:transport}, prevent a direct interpretation as $2H$ for a
scale-free stationary-increment residual. The control removes constant velocity but does
not yield an intrinsic Hurst exponent here.

\paragraph{A plausible origin of the apparent exponent.}
The cancellation itself does not impose stationary increments or a power law
on the remaining motion. To see what $V_2$ measures, write the velocities averaged
over two consecutive intervals as $\overline{\mathbf v}_1=\mathbf I_1/\tau$
and $\overline{\mathbf v}_2=\mathbf I_2/\tau$. Then, exactly,
\[
 D_2(\tau)=
 \sqrt{\left\langle|\overline{\mathbf v}_2-
                         \overline{\mathbf v}_1|^2\right\rangle},
 \qquad V_2(\tau)=\tau^2D_2^2(\tau),\qquad
 b_{2,\mathrm{loc}}=2+2\frac{\mathrm d\log D_2}{\mathrm d\log\tau}.
\]
The averages use the same flights, origins and weights as $V_2$ at each lag.
In the saved curves over \SIrange{10}{10000}{\second}, $D_2$ varies
nonmonotonically between approximately \SIrange{4.9}{7.3}{\metre\per\second}
for paragliders and \SIrange{7.6}{11.5}{\metre\per\second} for hang gliders:
its magnitude changes much less than the three-decade lag range.
Thus $b_2$ near two can arise when the change between interval-mean velocity
vectors remains of comparable magnitude across the fitted lags. It does not
establish $H=1$: in Eq.~\eqref{eq:variation-hurst}, that exact boundary case
would give $V_2=0$. The small excesses above two are descriptive estimates,
not calibrated evidence for $H>1$.

Finite turning and velocity-persistence times provide a physical reason for
curvature. Constant-velocity cancellation retains circling, acceleration,
changes between glides and climbs, and changes in wind along a trajectory.
These motions introduce characteristic times rather than guaranteeing a
scale-free residual. At short lags, smoothing and cadence may also affect the
observed change; the smooth-trajectory $\tau^4$ limit above is a possible local
regime, not an assertion that the measured interval lies in that limit.

Nonstationarity is not required for this interpretation. In a single
stationary-increment population, the general identity is
$V_2(\tau)=4M_2(\tau)-M_2(2\tau)$; the power-law expression follows only if
the same law holds at both $\tau$ and $2\tau$. For the stationary exponentially
correlated velocity example of Eq.~\eqref{eq:persistent-msd}, with persistence
time $t_p$ and velocity variance $\sigma_v^2$, this gives
\[
 V_2(\tau)=2\sigma_v^2t_p^2
 \left[2\frac{\tau}{t_p}-3+4e^{-\tau/t_p}-e^{-2\tau/t_p}\right].
\]
Its short-lag growth is $4\sigma_v^2\tau^3/(3t_p)$ and its long-lag growth is
$4\sigma_v^2t_p\tau$. A slope near two can therefore occur in a crossover
even though the position increments are stationary and there is no globally
self-similar position process. This example establishes a mechanism, not a fit
to the flight curves; their bends need not follow one exponential persistence
time.

Population selection can compound these physical scale effects. Fixing the
long-flight cohort does not bring $b_2$ below two in either discipline
(Table~\ref{tab:orderscan}), so disappearing short flights alone do not explain
the result. That control still changes the admitted origins and their within-flight
weights with lag. Nor can the empirical $V_1$ and $V_2$ curves be inserted into
the stationary identity without matching their observations and weights.
The plausible working explanation is therefore that the fitted interval combines
velocity and route time scales with changing observation support, rather than
isolating one self-similar residual. Anisotropy alone would not invalidate
Eq.~\eqref{eq:variation-hurst}. To distinguish these explanations, the next
checks should hold flights and origins fixed through $2\tau$, examine the
increment law across flight time and phases, and seek a stable scaling interval
outside cadence and smoothing effects. The current evidence does not identify
one physical cause or prove that no useful restricted-regime $H$ exists.

\subsection{Open and closed routes}
\label{sec:strata-compat}

A declared closed task rewards return towards the starting region. An open task allows
continued progress away from it. This difference can change long-lag displacements even
if speed and short-time behaviour are similar. Task classes are compared separately in
Figure~\ref{fig:transport}; a declaration is not a measurement that a particular retained
segment closes exactly.

A circular trajectory makes the distinction precise. For
$\mathbf r(t)=a(\cos\omega t,\sin\omega t)$ with period $T_c=2\pi/\omega$,
\begin{equation}
  V_1(\tau)=4a^2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{T_c}\right),\qquad
  V_2(\tau)=16a^2\sin^4\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{T_c}\right).
  \label{eq:closed-circle}
\end{equation}
The displacement initially grows, then decreases as the lag approaches a complete return.
The second difference also retains this geometry. Removing constant drift cannot remove
closure, a turn, or a spatially varying forcing.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/closed_loop_schematic}
  \caption{Analytical straight and circular paths at the
same speed. The circle is continued periodically to evaluate second differences.
Both $V_1$ and $V_2$ vanish after a complete period: constant-velocity removal
preserves the effect of route closure.}
  \label{fig:closed-loop-schematic}
\end{figure}

The selected paraglider tasks include \num{\StatRevParaOpenFlights} open and
\num{\StatRevParaClosedFlights} closed flights; hang gliders contribute
\num{\StatRevHangOpenFlights} and \num{\StatRevHangClosedFlights}. Their effective
$b_1$ values are respectively \StatRevParaOpenAlphaOne\ versus
\StatRevParaClosedAlphaOne, and \StatRevHangOpenAlphaOne\ versus
\StatRevHangClosedAlphaOne. At order two the ordering reverses:
\StatRevParaOpenAlphaTwo\ versus \StatRevParaClosedAlphaTwo\ and
\StatRevHangOpenAlphaTwo\ versus \StatRevHangClosedAlphaTwo. This is consistent with
different route geometry affecting the two observables differently; it is not a
quantitative decomposition into drift and fluctuations.

A complementary descriptor compares net displacement with the path actually
observed on the common grid. Let $t_f^-$ and $t_f^+$ be the first and last retained
grid times, and let $L_f^{\rm obs}$ sum only the within-segment chords:
\begin{equation}
 L_f^{\rm obs}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S_f}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{fs}-2}
       |\mathbf r_{fs,j+1}-\mathbf r_{fs,j}|,\qquad
 \chi_f^{\rm obs}=\frac{|\mathbf r_f(t_f^+)-\mathbf r_f(t_f^-)|}
                         {L_f^{\rm obs}},\quad L_f^{\rm obs}>0.
 \label{eq:closure-ratio}
\end{equation}
For one uninterrupted observed path, the triangle inequality gives
$0\le\chi_f^{\rm obs}\le1$. Across separated segments, the denominator omits
unobserved travel while the numerator spans it, so this bound need not hold.
Reacquisition offsets can also affect the endpoint displacement. The full collector
contains \num{181} paraglider and two hang-glider values above one, all among
flights with multiple eligible segments. This is an observed-path descriptor,
not a reconstruction of the complete distance flown.

In the eligible archive, median $\chi_f^{\rm obs}$ is
\StatRevParaOpenClosure\ for open paraglider tasks and
\StatRevParaClosedClosure\ for closed ones; hang-glider medians are
\StatRevHangOpenClosure\ and \StatRevHangClosedClosure. These medians describe
the endpoint geometry of retained observations, with the gap limitation above.
Restricting to flights with one eligible observed segment gives closely similar
open/closed medians: 0.364/0.027 in paragliders and 0.177/0.026 in hang gliders.
This sensitivity check preserves the descriptive ordering; one eligible segment
need not cover the complete original flight. For an uninterrupted observed path,
small $\chi_f^{\rm obs}$ means little net progress relative to its length.
None of the increment statistics crosses a segment boundary.

Matching task groups by duration and region, and comparing both physical lag and
$\tau/T_f$, would help separate route geometry from population composition.

\section{Quantiles and self-similarity}
\label{sec:transport-propagator}

\subsection{The scaling prediction}

For an $H$-self-similar process starting at the origin,
$(\mathbf r(ct_1),\ldots,\mathbf r(ct_n))\overset d=
c^H(\mathbf r(t_1),\ldots,\mathbf r(t_n))$ for every $c>0$ and every finite choice of
times. Self-similarity therefore constrains joint distributions, beyond the MSD alone. With stationary increments, it implies the one-lag
prediction
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf X(t,\tau)\ \overset{d}{=}\
       (\tau/\tau_0)^H\mathbf X(t,\tau_0).
  \label{eq:increment-scaling}
\end{equation}
Writing $a_\tau=(\tau/\tau_0)^H$, and assuming the corresponding densities exist,
the two-dimensional and radial laws obey different Jacobians:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf x;\tau)=a_\tau^{-2}F(\mathbf x/a_\tau),
  \qquad p_R(r;\tau)=a_\tau^{-1}f(r/a_\tau).
  \label{eq:radial-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The radial law follows by integrating the joint density over angle, including the polar
Jacobian $r$. It does not require isotropy. In particular, a two-dimensional density at
the origin would scale as $a_\tau^{-2}$, not as $a_\tau^{-1}$.
These are consequences of the proposed vector scale family, not statements
that the measured laws already satisfy it. A shared scale factor does not
require the radial and component densities to have the same shape.

\paragraph{East and north scaling.}
Projecting Eq.~\eqref{eq:increment-scaling} onto either geographic axis gives
the signed component laws
\begin{equation}
 X_E(t,\tau)\overset d=a_\tau X_E(t,\tau_0),\qquad
 X_N(t,\tau)\overset d=a_\tau X_N(t,\tau_0).
 \label{eq:component-increment-scaling}
\end{equation}
Their one-dimensional densities therefore satisfy
\begin{equation}
 p_E(x;\tau)=a_\tau^{-1}f_E(x/a_\tau),\qquad
 p_N(y;\tau)=a_\tau^{-1}f_N(y/a_\tau),
 \label{eq:component-density-scaling}
\end{equation}
where $f_E(u)=\int F(u,v)\,\mathrm dv$ and
$f_N(v)=\int F(u,v)\,\mathrm du$ are marginals of the reference joint density
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:radial-jacobian}. Neither independence nor isotropy is required:
the same exponent $H$ does not require $f_E=f_N$.

The component quantile analysis below uses the magnitudes $Y_j=|X_j|$,
for $j\in\{E,N\}$. These obey
\begin{equation}
 |X_j(t,\tau)|\overset d=a_\tau|X_j(t,\tau_0)|,\qquad
 Q_{|X_j|,p}(\tau)=a_\tau Q_{|X_j|,p}(\tau_0).
 \label{eq:absolute-component-scaling}
\end{equation}
For $y>0$, their densities are
$p_{|X_j|}(y;\tau)=p_j(y;\tau)+p_j(-y;\tau)$ and have the same
one-dimensional factor $a_\tau^{-1}$. A log--log quantile slope is defined only
for positive finite quantiles; this qualification matters for absolute
components and prevents applying that fit directly to negative or zero signed
quantiles.

If scaling is assumed separately for the two marginals, one may instead write
$X_j(t,\tau)\overset d=(\tau/\tau_0)^{H_j}X_j(t,\tau_0)$ with distinct
$H_E$ and $H_N$. This is a weaker marginal hypothesis: even equal exponents
would not establish the vector law without its component dependence
(Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-vector-scaling}).

Define the radial quantile at probability $p$ by
\begin{equation}
  Q_p(\tau)=\inf\{r:\Pr[R(t,\tau)\le r]\ge p\},\qquad 0<p<1.
  \label{eq:quantile-definition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:increment-scaling} implies
\begin{equation}
  Q_p(\tau)=a_\tau Q_p(\tau_0),\qquad
  H_p=\frac{\mathrm d\log Q_p}{\mathrm d\log\tau}=H
  \quad\hbox{when }0<Q_p(\tau_0)<\infty.
  \label{eq:quantile-hurst}
\end{equation}
The four probabilities $p=.25,.50,.75,.90$ describe small, typical and larger
displacements. Quantiles use ranks, complementing moments without requiring a finite
fourth moment. Common quantile slopes are necessary for the proposed one-lag scaling
law; they do not establish self-similarity across several times.

\subsection{A fixed population across lags}
\label{sec:quantile-population}

At each lag, all percentiles belong to the same displacement distribution.
They do not label fixed groups of slow or fast flights. A separate sampling effect
arises when that distribution draws on different flights or weights at different lags.

If flight $f$ supplies $n_f(\tau)$ admissible increments, pooling
them defines
\begin{equation}
 \widehat F_\tau^{\rm pool}(x)
 =\sum_{f\in\mathcal F_\tau}
    \frac{n_f(\tau)}{\sum_g n_g(\tau)}\widehat F_{f,\tau}(x),\qquad
 \widehat F_{f,\tau}(x)=\frac1{n_f(\tau)}\sum_k
                   \mathbf1\{R_{fk}(\tau)\le x\}.
 \label{eq:quantile-mixture}
\end{equation}
As lag grows, short flights disappear and window counts change. Both membership
$\mathcal F_\tau$ and mixture weights therefore vary. Quantiles depend nonlinearly
on these weights: averaging flight-specific quantiles would give a different
quantity. Figure~\ref{fig:quantile-selection} illustrates the resulting bias.

The analysis therefore fixes the sampled population once and keeps the same flights,
weights and time origins at every lag. A flight joins the cohort $\mathcal F_*$ when
it has at least one retained segment of \SI{20000}{\second}. That threshold is an
operational choice; the minimum duration mathematically required to observe a
\SI{10000}{\second} increment is shorter. It supplies at least \num{1001} common
10-s origins in one qualifying segment. Other eligible segments of these same flights
also contribute when they support the required interval, so the cohort fixes flights
rather than only their longest segments. Its conclusions remain conditional on long
flights.

Let $\tau_{\max}=\SI{10000}{\second}$ and keep every starting
time $t_{fk}$ on the 10-s grid whose complete interval
$[t_{fk},t_{fk}+\tau_{\max}]$ lies within one retained segment. Then
\begin{equation}
 \widehat F_\tau^{\rm fixed}(x)
 =\frac1{|\mathcal F_*|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal F_*}\frac1{n_f^*}
     \sum_{k=1}^{n_f^*}\mathbf1\{R_f(t_{fk},\tau)\le x\},\qquad
 \widehat Q_p(\tau)=\inf\{x:\widehat F_\tau(x)\ge p\}.
 \label{eq:quantile-fixed-population}
\end{equation}
Flights, origins and weights are now independent of $\tau$, so quantiles measured at
different lags describe one sampled law. The convention inverts the empirical CDF
without interpolating between order statistics. The common origins overlap at most
lags; they preserve the sampled population without making its increments independent.

\StatRevHangQuantileFixedFlights\ hang-glider flights, retaining respectively
\num{\StatRevParaQuantileFixedOrigins} and \num{\StatRevHangQuantileFixedOrigins}
origins at every lag. These overlapping origins are not independent replicates.

\subsection{Relative spread}

\paragraph{What a self-similar process requires of these quantiles.}
Throughout this chapter $\tau$ is a \emph{lag}: the separation between two instants of
the same flight in the increment $\mathbf X_f(t,\tau)=\mathbf r_f(t+\tau)-\mathbf r_f(t)$
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:displacement-global}, with the origin $t$ taken inside a retained
segment. It is never the time elapsed since take-off, and every flight supplies many
origins $t$ at each $\tau$. Only Fig.~\ref{fig:msd}a uses elapsed time, and it is
labelled as such.

Let the process be $H$-self-similar with stationary increments. The law of
$R(t,\tau)=|\mathbf X_f(t,\tau)|$ is then independent of the origin $t$ and obeys
\begin{equation}
 R(t,\tau)\overset{\mathrm d}{=}(\tau/\tau_0)^{H}\,R(t,\tau_0),
 \label{eq:sssi-increment}
\end{equation}
where $\overset{\mathrm d}{=}$ denotes equality in distribution. Stationarity of the
increments is what allows the pooling over origins; self-similarity alone constrains
displacement from the start of the process rather than from an arbitrary $t$.
Quantiles are equivariant under multiplication by a positive constant, so
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sssi-increment} gives $Q_p(\tau)=(\tau/\tau_0)^{H}Q_p(\tau_0)$ at every
probability $p$, with the same exponent $H$. The common factor then cancels in any
ratio of two quantiles:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{Q_{0.90}(\tau)}{Q_{0.25}(\tau)}
  =\frac{Q_{0.90}(\tau_0)}{Q_{0.25}(\tau_0)}
 \qquad\text{for every }\tau.
 \label{eq:quantile-ratio-constant}
\end{equation}
A ratio that varies with lag therefore contradicts Eq.~\eqref{eq:sssi-increment} for
every choice of $H$, since the ratio is unchanged by any rescaling of $R$. This is a
necessary condition, and a collapse of rescaled distributions cannot restore it.

The test is blind to the exponent. For isotropic Gaussian increments the radius is
Rayleigh distributed and Eq.~\eqref{eq:quantile-ratio-constant} evaluates to
$\sqrt{\ln 0.10/\ln 0.75}\simeq2.83$ at every lag, for fractional Brownian motion of
any $H$ as much as for Brownian motion. A constant ratio constrains the shape of the
law; the growth exponent has to be measured separately.

\paragraph{Reading a changing ratio.}
The interpretation of an ordering such as $H_{0.25}>H_{0.90}$ follows directly from
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\log\tau}
       \log\!\left(\frac{Q_{0.90}(\tau)}{Q_{0.25}(\tau)}\right)
       =H_{0.90}(\tau)-H_{0.25}(\tau),
  \label{eq:quantile-ratio-growth}
\end{equation}
which vanishes identically under Eq.~\eqref{eq:quantile-ratio-constant}.
A negative right-hand side means that the ratio of large to small displacements decreases.
Both quantiles may increase in absolute distance. The lower one increases faster in
\emph{proportion}, so the distribution becomes less spread out by this relative measure.
For example, a slope difference of $-0.10$ maintained over a tenfold increase of lag
multiplies the ratio by $10^{-0.10}\simeq0.79$.

On a common lag grid, the fitted slope of $\log(Q_{.90}/Q_{.25})$ also equals
the difference of the fitted quantile slopes, by linearity of least squares.
Its sign need not imply monotonic change. In the controlled population, the
paraglider ratio rises from 1.97 at \SI{10}{\second} to a sampled maximum of
4.20 at \SI{90}{\second}; the hang-glider ratio rises from 2.27 to 5.52 at
\SI{80}{\second}. After these early maxima, the ratios fall to local minima of
2.23 at \SI{2410}{\second} and 2.32 at \SI{1970}{\second}, respectively.
They then rise to 3.01 and 2.60 at \SI{10000}{\second}. The negative fitted
contrast summarises this curved evolution; it does not describe a monotone
narrowing over the full interval.

This ratio measures relative quantile spread, leaving the outer 10\% and local
density shape unspecified. Observations can also change rank with lag.

A model must reproduce the quantile curves and their shape changes as well as their
average growth.

\section{Vector scaling and dependence}
\label{sec:transport-vector-scaling}

The radial quantiles describe displacement magnitude. We now ask whether the
same rescaling describes the signed east--north vector, including its dependence
structure. The population and origin controls of
Sec.~\ref{sec:quantile-population} remain fixed throughout this comparison.
An anisotropic distribution can obey a common scalar rescaling; isotropy is not
an assumption of the test.

\subsection{Testing east, north and radial scaling}
\label{sec:component-self-similarity}

The absolute east and north components and the radius change shape over
\SIrange{10}{10000}{\second}, even in the fixed population. To interpret their
comparison, first consider two ways marginal scaling can fail to determine vector
scaling.

Component exponents can differ. For centred independent Gaussian components with
variances $a\tau^{2H_E}$ and $b\tau^{2H_N}$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E R^2=a\tau^{2H_E}+b\tau^{2H_N},\qquad
  \frac12\frac{\mathrm d\log\mathbb E R^2}{\mathrm d\log\tau}
   =\frac{H_Ea\tau^{2H_E}+H_Nb\tau^{2H_N}}
          {a\tau^{2H_E}+b\tau^{2H_N}}.
  \label{eq:anisotropic-exponents}
\end{equation}
Each Gaussian marginal scales exactly, yet the radial effective exponent varies
with lag. No cross-correlation is needed.

Equal marginal exponents also leave component dependence undetermined. For example,
the equiprobable pairs
\begin{equation}
 \{(1,2),(-1,-2)\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \{(1,-2),(-1,2)\}
 \label{eq:joint-law-counterexample}
\end{equation}
have identical signed marginals and radius $\sqrt5$, but opposite cross-covariance.
Thus even matching both marginals and the radial law does not establish a common
joint law. The Cram\'er--Wold characterization uses all signed linear projections
\cite{fraiman2022}. Conversely, scalar scaling of the joint vector always implies
radial scaling, including for anisotropic laws. Operator self-similarity, with a
matrix exponent, is a broader hypothesis~\cite{didier2011}.

\begin{equation}

 \label{eq:positive-displacements}
\end{equation}
These are magnitudes of displacement over a lag. Absolute values discard direction;
$R$ measures the distance between the endpoints, rather than the path flown between
them.

\subsection{Moment spectrum}
\label{sec:moment-spectrum}

The quantile exponents above describe fixed ranks of the displacement law. Moments
weigh the whole law at once, with a weight set by the order, so the two families read
the same increments through different lenses and constrain a model jointly.

On the fixed population of Sec.~\ref{sec:quantile-population}, with $F$ fixed flights,
flight $f$ contributing the origin set $\mathcal O_f$ of size $n_f$, and every flight
carrying equal total weight, define
\begin{equation}
  M_q(\tau)=\frac1F\sum_{f=1}^{F}\frac{1}{n_f}
            \sum_{t\in\mathcal O_f}R_f(t,\tau)^{\,q},
  \qquad q>0,
  \label{eq:equal-flight-moment}
\end{equation}
and the exponents
\begin{equation}
  \zeta(q)=\frac{\mathrm d\log M_q(\tau)}{\mathrm d\log\tau},
  \qquad
  \nu(q)=\frac{\zeta(q)}{q},
  \label{eq:moment-exponents}
\end{equation}
both fitted by least squares in $\log\tau$ over the window fixed below.
Under Eq.~\eqref{eq:sssi-increment} the whole law rescales by $(\tau/\tau_0)^H$, so
$M_q(\tau)\propto\tau^{qH}$ and $\nu(q)=H$ at every order. Dividing by $q$ is what puts
the spectrum on the same footing as the quantile exponents $H_p$: both measure
$\mathrm d\log(\text{length})/\mathrm d\log\tau$.

An exact identity links the two families. Writing the moment as an integral over ranks and
substituting the fitted quantile laws $Q_p(\tau)=A_p\tau^{H_p}$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  M_q(\tau)&=\int_0^1 Q_p(\tau)^{\,q}\,\mathrm dp
           =\int_0^1 A_p^{\,q}\,\tau^{\,qH_p}\,\mathrm dp,\\
  \nu(q)&=\int_0^1 H_p\,w_p(q,\tau)\,\mathrm dp,
  \qquad
  w_p(q,\tau)=\frac{Q_p(\tau)^{\,q}}{\int_0^1 Q_{p'}(\tau)^{\,q}\,\mathrm dp'}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:nu-as-weighted-rank-exponent}
\end{equation}
So $\nu(q)$ is a weighted mean of the rank exponents whose weight grows with the size
of the displacement. As $q\to0$ the weight becomes uniform and $\nu$ tends to the plain
average of $H_p$; as $q$ grows the weight concentrates on the largest displacements and
$\nu$ tends to the exponent of the upper ranks. A spectrum flat in $q$ is what one
rescaling exponent predicts. A spectrum falling with $q$ says the upper ranks grow more
slowly than the lower ones, which is the same statement
Eq.~\eqref{eq:quantile-ratio-growth} makes without any fit.

Equation~\eqref{eq:nu-as-weighted-rank-exponent} also sets the sensitivity of the test.

\subsection{Distribution collapse}
To test a scale family, fit one exponent $H_{j,*}$ per quantity, shared by all
four ranks, with separate intercepts $b_{j,p}$, by minimising
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{p\in\{.25,.50,.75,.90\}}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal L}
 \left[\log\!\frac{Q_{j,p}(\tau)}{1\,\mathrm m}
       -b_{j,p}-H_{j,*}\log\!\frac{\tau}{\tau_0}\right]^2,
 \qquad \tau_0=\SI{1000}{\second}.
 \label{eq:common-quantile-fit}
\end{equation}
Ranks and lags have equal weight. On the common grid, $H_{j,*}$ is the mean
of the four separate slopes. The reference time fixes the intercept without
changing the exponent.

With $a_j(\tau)=(\tau/\tau_0)^{H_{j,*}}$, a density collapse plots
\begin{equation}
 u=y/a_j(\tau),\qquad a_j(\tau)\,p_{Y_j}(a_j(\tau)u;\tau).
 \label{eq:measured-density-collapse}
\end{equation}
The fitted exponent rescales both axes at every lag, without tuning the histogram
overlay. Densities use probability mass divided by bin width over the full positive
support; any exact zero is stored separately as an atom. Quantiles and empirical-CDF
distances use the observations directly, independently of binning.

The full positive support can approach numerical zero. Summing all bins that
intersect $[0,\SI{1}{\milli\metre})$, together with the zero atom, bounds the
probability in that interval below $3\times10^{-5}$ in every displayed law.
Very narrow bins can therefore have a high density while carrying little
probability. These values do not establish sub-millimetre physical resolution;
the median-normalised CDF panels below make the central probability structure
easier to compare than the full-range logarithmic densities.

\paragraph{Approximate collapse and its limits.}
Figures~\ref{fig:absolute-laws-para} and~\ref{fig:absolute-laws-hang} show a
clear visual improvement after power rescaling: the absolute east and north
densities approximately superpose over a broad part of their displayed range.
This is evidence of approximate marginal scaling on the measured interval,
with fitted exponents near 0.9. The radial curves also move into a broadly
common range, especially around their main peak and upper falloff, but retain
more visible differences on the rising side and around the peak.

The visual agreement is not exact distributional collapse. The maximum
power-rescaled ECDF separations are 0.177, 0.203 and 0.273 for east, north and
radius in paragliders, and 0.185, 0.269 and 0.331 in hang gliders
(Table~\ref{tab:component-collapse}). For example, 0.177 is a difference of
17.7 percentage points in cumulative probability for some pair of displayed
lags, not a relative density error. Logarithmic density axes can make such
differences appear small. Median normalisation reduces the radial distances
to 0.194 and 0.229, but leaves separation for all three quantities. The
rank-dependent slopes and changing quantile ratios provide further evidence
that one power scale is an incomplete description.

We therefore describe the east and north results as approximate collapse of
the absolute-component one-lag laws, rather than established self-similarity
of either signed component process. Taking absolute values loses sign
information, and these plots do not test joint scaling across multiple times.
Nor do different fitted $H_{E,*}$ and $H_{N,*}$ establish one common vector
exponent. The measured discrepancies have no calibrated sampling-error
threshold: they quantify the residual differences without constituting a
formal rejection test or excluding useful scaling on a more restricted range.

\paragraph{Why the radial density need not have a plateau.}
Scaling compares a given quantity across lags; it does not require the
component and radial scaling functions to coincide. At a fixed lag, polar
coordinates give the exact relation
\begin{equation}
 p_R(r;\tau)=r\int_0^{2\pi}
 p_{\mathbf X}(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta;\tau)\,\mathrm d\theta,
 \qquad r>0.
 \label{eq:radial-angular-density}
\end{equation}
If the joint density is continuous and finite at the origin, with a nonzero
value there, then $p_R(r;\tau)\sim2\pi p_{\mathbf X}(0,0;\tau)r$ as
$r\downarrow0$. In contrast, a continuous finite signed marginal density
gives $p_{|X_E|}(x;\tau)\to2p_E(0;\tau)$, and similarly for north.
A nonzero marginal value therefore gives a plateau, while the regular radial
density starts linearly from zero. This distinction does not require isotropy.
Geometrically, a small absolute east displacement selects a strip; a small
radius requires both components to lie in a small disk. Their probabilities
are respectively of order $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon^2$ under these regularity
conditions.

An exact scale-family example makes the distinction explicit. For independent
centred Gaussian components with common standard deviation
$\sigma_\tau=\sigma_0a_\tau$, the absolute-component and radial densities are
\begin{equation}
 p_{|X_E|}(x;\tau)=\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\sigma_\tau}
       e^{-x^2/(2\sigma_\tau^2)},\qquad
 p_R(r;\tau)=\frac{r}{\sigma_\tau^2}
       e^{-r^2/(2\sigma_\tau^2)},\quad x,r\ge0.
 \label{eq:gaussian-component-radial-scaling}
\end{equation}
Both collapse exactly after division of their argument by $a_\tau$ and
multiplication of their density by $a_\tau$, although only the component
density has a plateau. The extra $r$ becomes $a_\tau u$, leaving the same
overall factor $a_\tau^{-1}$ for the radial density in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:radial-jacobian}. This example explains why a rising radial
curve is compatible with scaling; it is not a Gaussian fit to the flights.
The observed small-radius histograms need not have reached the regular
asymptotic regime, particularly near numerical zeros. Their residual
separation across lags, rather than the absence of a plateau, is the
relevant limitation on collapse.

\paragraph{Squared displacements.}
For any of the nonnegative magnitudes $Y_j$ and $S_j=Y_j^2$, the inverse-CDF
quantiles satisfy exactly
\begin{equation}
 Q_{S_j,p}(\tau)=Q_{Y_j,p}(\tau)^2,\qquad
 \beta_{j,p}=2H_{j,p},\qquad
 p_{S_j}(s;\tau)=\frac{p_{Y_j}(\sqrt{s};\tau)}{2\sqrt{s}},\quad s>0.
 \label{eq:squared-displacement-scaling}
\end{equation}
The squared-density collapse therefore uses scale $a_j^2$ and exponent $2H_{j,*}$;
bootstrap slope intervals double too. Squared-law plots appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:squared-displacements}. Squaring is a monotone bijection for
nonnegative values, so corresponding ECDF distances are unchanged. It cannot
repair a failed collapse. Radial quantiles must still be computed from paired
components: a quantile of $X_E^2+X_N^2$ is generally not the sum of their quantiles.

\subsection{Signed joint distributions}
\paragraph{Spatial and temporal joint laws.}
Here \emph{joint} refers to the east and north components of the same displacement
$\mathbf X(t,\tau)=\mathbf r(t+\tau)-\mathbf r(t)$. At each lag, the
two-dimensional law $p_\tau(x_E,x_N)$ describes their simultaneous dependence,
including the shape and orientation of the displacement cloud. Comparing these
laws at several lags still compares separate two-dimensional distributions.

A temporal joint law also retains the association between displacements in
different intervals. For example, two consecutive intervals of duration $\tau$
give the four-dimensional random vector
\[
 \bigl(X_E(t,\tau),X_N(t,\tau),
       X_E(t+\tau,\tau),X_N(t+\tau,\tau)\bigr).
\]
Its law describes how the first displacement is related to the next, such as
whether an eastward displacement tends to be followed by another eastward
displacement or a return. The separate one-lag laws do not generally determine
this joint law. They can nevertheless constrain temporal dependence through
the distribution of accumulated displacements; they are not devoid of temporal
information.

Process self-similarity requires the joint scaling of every finite collection
of observation times, as defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-propagator}.
With stationary increments, the collapse of the one-lag vector laws is a
necessary consequence, but even exact collapse would not establish that full
property. The maps and distances below assess this one-lag consequence; they
do not estimate a four-dimensional temporal joint law. The subsequent held-out
comparison in Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-temporal-scaling} adds projections of two
consecutive intervals and quantifies approximate agreement on selected scales.

\paragraph{The measured spatial comparison.}
Figures~\ref{fig:joint-para} and~\ref{fig:joint-hang} retain the signs of
$(X_E,X_N)$ on the same flights and origins. Both coordinates are divided by
$s_0(\tau/\SI{1000}{\second})^{H_*}$, where $H_*$ is the mean of all twelve slopes
and $s_0=Q_{R,.50}(\tau_r)/(\tau_r/\SI{1000}{\second})^{H_*}$,
with $\tau_r=\SI{1070}{\second}$, the available lag nearest the reference time.
Thus the recorded median is adjusted to the common reference by the same power.
Geographic axes remain fixed,
without lag-specific centring, rotation or whitening.

The six lags share 64 bins per axis within $[-6,6]$, plus overflow cells.
With equal total weight per flight, let $p_{\tau,ij}$ be the joint cell mass.
The distance between two binned laws is
\begin{equation}
 D_{\mathrm{joint}}(\tau,\tau')=
 \frac12\sum_{ij}|p_{\tau,ij}-p_{\tau',ij}|,
 \label{eq:joint-distribution-distance}
\end{equation}
including overflow. This comparison resolves differences at the chosen bin size;
it is not a calibrated rejection test. Energy distance is an alternative for
full multivariate laws with finite first moments~\cite{szekely2013}, but inference
would still require calibration for paired flights and dependent origins.

\section{Flight duration and equipment composition}
\label{sec:duration-check}

Longer flights may select different equipment, pilots and conditions. A change in their
MSD can therefore reflect a change of population rather than an effect of observation
length. We examine both duration bands and their equipment composition, using the
identified segment curves from the full archive: \num{\StatDurationParaFlights} paraglider
and \num{\StatDurationHangFlights} hang-glider flights have segments of at least
eight fixes with native cadence at most \SI{10}{\second}. Here $T_f$ is the sum of the retained segment spans
of flight $f$; gaps and discarded time are not part of this observed duration.

First combine the available segment curves within each flight, weighting them by the
number of admissible time origins, then give every contributing flight equal weight
within a band of observed duration:
\begin{equation}
 m_f(\tau)=\frac{\sum_s n_{fs}(\tau)m_{fs}(\tau)}{\sum_s n_{fs}(\tau)},\qquad
 M_{[T_0,T_1)}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N_{[T_0,T_1)}(\tau)}
      \sum_{f:\,T_0\le T_f<T_1,\;n_f(\tau)>0}m_f(\tau).
 \label{eq:duration-flight-msd}
\end{equation}
For an eligible segment $fs$ of $N_{fs}$ fixes at interval $\Delta t_{fs}$,
$n_{fs}=N_{fs}-\operatorname{round}(\tau/\Delta t_{fs})$; it is zero outside that segment's
stored lag support, which ends at $\lfloor N_{fs}/2\rfloor\Delta t_{fs}$. No displacement
crosses a segment boundary. This equal-flight average differs from the equal-segment
archive average introduced earlier. Requested lags are rounded to each segment's native grid.

The four bands are disjoint, so no flight enters two of them. Nested cohorts
$T_f\ge T_0$ answer a different question, namely whether a pooled estimate survives
restriction to longer records. They cannot describe how transport depends on duration:
consecutive cohorts share most of their flights, since dropping every paraglider flight
under one hour removes \num{\StatDurationParaBandUnderOneHFlights} of
\num{\StatDurationParaFlights}, and each cohort is contained in the previous, so only a
monotone progression can appear. A band holds its membership fixed out to $\tau=T_0/2$,
which is where its curve ends in Fig.~\ref{fig:msd-duration}.

\section{Constraints for the phase analysis}
\label{sec:transport-verdict}

A model must account jointly for moment growth, changing quantile spread, the
regional anisotropy of Chapter~\ref{ch:observables}, velocity memory, and the contrasts
by route, duration and equipment. An effective exponent alone cannot describe this set of observations.

The independent terrain comparison strengthens the geographical interpretation
of the Pyrenean displacement axis, while the Alpine axis differs from the mean
highland orientation. A model of environment-dependent motion should preserve
this regional distinction; neither angle comparison supplies a causal separation
of terrain, wind and route selection. On the Channel Coast, ERA5 wind at the
mean altitude of the contributing flights gives an annual axis about 12--14
degrees from the flight axis, for both all hours and daytime. The warm-season
checks retain a broadly similar orientation at this altitude, whereas the
surface comparison is more sensitive to season. This supports a descriptive
regional resemblance, with a remaining offset; a mean wind direction alone
cannot explain centred displacement covariance.

Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} introduces phase labels. Connecting those phases to global
transport requires durations, displacements and dependencies within and between
episodes. If $c$ partitions all admissible windows into phase histories, including
windows crossing a phase boundary, then
\begin{equation}
  M_q(\tau)=\sum_c w_c(\tau)M_q(\tau\mid c),\qquad
  w_c=\frac{n_c}{\sum_{c'}n_{c'}},\qquad \sum_cw_c=1,
  \label{eq:phase-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
for window weighting. With flight weighting, apply the identity within each
flight first. Windows wholly inside individual phases omit transitions and
cannot generally reconstruct the global moment. Since both weights and conditional
amplitudes depend on lag, global exponents are not weighted phase exponents.

Any exhaustive partition satisfies this identity, including one with incorrect labels.
Reconstructing the global moments therefore checks the calculation; independent labels
are still needed to assess the phase assignment.
