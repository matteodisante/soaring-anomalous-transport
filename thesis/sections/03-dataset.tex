\section{The IGC tracklog format}
\label{sec:igcformat}

\begin{revblock}
A flight track is stored as an \emph{IGC file}, the plain-text format defined by the
FAI/IGC Technical Specification for IGC-approved GNSS flight recorders~\cite{fai_igc_spec},
decoded from fixed character positions; every downloaded track is checked at acquisition to
be IGC content --- an \texttt{A} record followed by at least one \texttt{B} record
(Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}).

Each line is a \emph{record} whose type is set by its first character: \texttt{A} the
flight-recorder identifier, \texttt{H} header fields (date, pilot, glider, datum, \dots),
\texttt{I} the declaration of optional extra fields, and \texttt{B} the GPS fixes that form
the body of the file. A \texttt{B} record decodes to a UTC time, latitude and longitude on
the WGS84 datum, a fix-validity flag, and \emph{two} altitudes in metres --- barometric
(pressure) then GNSS. The flag concerns the \emph{GNSS} solution only: \texttt{V} marks a
GNSS altitude drop-out and leaves the latitude, longitude and, on a logger with a working
pressure sensor, the barometric altitude usable --- the channel this analysis adopts
(Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}), a choice that matters because the vertical velocity derived
from it feeds the phase segmentation.

Decoding can succeed numerically on a corrupted byte that is not a legal encoding, so the
parser also checks format validity at decode time (a valid time, valid coordinate fields, a
valid hemisphere letter), dropping the record otherwise, on the same footing as a record of
the wrong length \impldetails{impl:igcformat}. This is distinct from the
\emph{plausibility} checks of fix-level cleaning (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}), which target
fixes that decode validly but are physically implausible given the rest of the trajectory.

The sequence of \texttt{B} records is a time-stamped three-dimensional trajectory at a
sampling period typically of a few seconds, not guaranteed uniform and varying across
recording devices --- a point the analysis must handle explicitly.
\end{revblock}

\section{Glider categories}
\label{sec:glider}

\begin{revblock}
Soaring kinematics depend strongly on the aircraft, so the glider must be identifiable
for any stratified analysis. Three categories exist, in increasing order of performance:
\textbf{paragliders} (flexible canopy; slowest, glide ratio $\sim\!8$--$11$, tight turns),
\textbf{hang gliders} (delta wing, flexible or rigid; faster, glide ratio $\sim\!10$--$15$),
and \textbf{sailplanes} (rigid aircraft; fastest, glide ratio $\gtrsim\!25$) --- different
enough in cruise speed, glide ratio and turn radius that the reference
study~\cite{vilpellet2026} analyses each separately rather than pooling them.

The category is set by the \emph{data source}: the FFVL competition is split by discipline
(Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}), so the present dataset spans both paragliders and hang
gliders, already enough for the cross-type comparison of~\cite{vilpellet2026}. Sailplanes
are collected by different portals and not yet in hand.
\end{revblock}

Within each discipline the flights can be further stratified by \emph{performance class},
recorded in \texttt{aile\_class}; \rev{the two disciplines use different class systems,
collected in Table~\ref{tab:aileclass}. Paragliders follow the EN/LTF certification
ladder, which does track performance (higher classes fly faster and flatter) and is
therefore the natural within-source stratification variable. The hang-glider scheme is
\emph{not} a ladder: the FAI classes are defined by the method of control, so they
stratify the fleet by design rather than by performance, and any ordering imposed on them
is an assumption to be checked rather than a property of the scheme.}

\flow{The two ladders are not documented on equal terms: the FAI classes come from a
freely published Sporting Code, the EN classes from a paid CEN standard with no public
authoritative text, so the EN entries of Table~\ref{tab:aileclass} are condensed from the
closest openly available source rather than the published standard itself
\impldetails{impl:glider}. Nothing in this thesis depends on the wording --- the class
field is a stratification label, and what the analysis needs is that the labels are
distinct and ordered, not what each one certifies.}

\section{Data quality and caveats}
\label{sec:dataquality}

A few limitations are already known and will shape the analysis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flights without a track.} About \SI{8}{\percent} of paraglider entries and
        about \SI{27}{\percent} of hang-glider entries have no IGC file (older or manually
        declared flights); they carry metadata only. These are the flights outside the
        \enquote{With GPS} column of Tables~\ref{tab:seasons-para}
        and~\ref{tab:seasons-hang}.
  \item \textbf{A small residue of unrecoverable files.} A few flights advertise a track
        whose download fails or returns something that is not valid IGC content
        (broken or placeholder links); these are recorded as failures and excluded.
  \item \textbf{Heterogeneous logging.} Sampling rate, availability of the two altitude
        channels, and per-fix positioning noise depend
        on the device; trajectories must be resampled and quality-filtered before any
        ensemble statistic is computed.
  \item \textbf{Altitude channel.} A sizeable minority of loggers record no
        pressure altitude -- almost a third of paraglider and roughly a sixth of hang-glider
        flights -- which fall back to the GNSS altitude, with the channel actually used
        recorded per flight (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}).
  \item \textbf{Glider metadata.} Flights are paragliders or hang gliders, tagged by source
        (Section~\ref{sec:glider}); the class field \texttt{aile\_class} must be normalised
        before it is used to stratify.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete dates.} \rev{Some historical entries carry the placeholder date
        \texttt{0000-00-00}, written as a sentinel at acquisition; such flights are
        discarded, since a date-less flight cannot be assigned to a season and no
        downstream step needs it. The cut is negligible and checked per season, not only
        archive-wide, since a small total share could still empty the smallest early
        seasons: \num{\StatCatParaPlaceholderDateCount} of
        \num{\StatCatParaFlights} paraglider entries carry the sentinel
        (\StatCatParaPlaceholderDatePct\,\%), \num{\StatCatHangPlaceholderDateCount} of
        \num{\StatCatHangFlights} hang-glider ones, and the worst-affected season loses
        only \StatCatParaPlaceholderWorstPct\,\% of its entries
        (\StatCatParaPlaceholderWorstSeason).}
\end{itemize}
These caveats motivate the pre-processing described in the remainder of this chapter.

\section{Trajectory pre-processing}
\label{sec:preproc}
Each IGC file is parsed into a sequence of fixes $(t_i,\varphi_i,\lambda_i,h_i)$ -- UTC
time, geodetic latitude and longitude, and altitude. The pipeline then proceeds in a fixed order:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item choose the altitude channel (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel});
  \item clean individual fixes (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel});
  \item trim the ground phases (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming});
  \item filter out unfit whole flights (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter});
  \item convert to a local Cartesian frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu});
  \item enforce a uniform sampling interval within each flight (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform});
  \item smooth and differentiate the trajectory (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}).
\end{enumerate}
The output is a tidy, analysis-ready representation written to three tables on the
data disk: one carrying a row per fix, one a row per segment, one a row per flight
\emph{attempted}. What each file holds and how the disk is organised is set out in
Sec.~\ref{impl:onDisk}.

Steps~(i)--(iv) act on the \emph{raw geographic} coordinates, since none of them
needs a direction. A horizontal distance comes straight from $(\varphi,\lambda)$ through
the great-circle (haversine) formula of Eq.~\eqref{eq:haversine}; a speed is one such
distance over an elapsed time; an altitude is read as logged. The conversion~(v) to the
metric East--North--Up frame
(Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}) then runs on the cleaned, trimmed track, with the origin at the first
fix that survives trimming, so the tangent-plane frame is built from good data. Steps
(vi)--(vii) come last because they produce \emph{vector} quantities: 3D position,
velocity and acceleration components, which need the Cartesian axes step~(v) provides.


Every step below is governed by a handful of numerical thresholds. Each is stated where it
is argued, and the whole set is gathered, with the symbols used here, in
Table~\ref{tab:workingparams} at the close of the chapter, beside the map of the pipeline.
All of them are \emph{working values}: fixed a priori, on physical scales rather than on
the transport observables, and audited a posteriori on the fraction of data each rule
removes (Table~\ref{tab:pipecensus}).


\subsection{Choice of altitude channel}
\label{sec:altchannel}
\begin{revblock}
The analysis adopts the \emph{barometric} altitude over the GNSS altitude for the vertical
dynamics: both are written to \SI{1}{\metre} resolution by the IGC format itself, but the
barometric channel carries far less high-frequency noise. Its errors are slow --- a
constant offset from the ICAO reference (tens of metres, Fig.~\ref{fig:altnoise}a) and a
drift already reaching tens of metres within the half-hour window of
Fig.~\ref{fig:altnoise}b --- both traceable to the gap between the day's actual sea-level
pressure and the ICAO reference of \SI{1013.25}{\hecto\pascal} \impldetails{impl:altchannel},
and annihilated by the differencing every dynamical quantity involves (a vertical velocity,
a displacement increment, an autocorrelation). The GNSS altitude is referenced to the
WGS84 ellipsoid, so it carries no atmospheric offset, but the vertical coordinate is the
worst-determined one geometrically, and multipath biases the range on timescales of
minutes~\cite{garciacrespillo2024} \impldetails{impl:altchannel}. This is why free-flight
variometers derive the climb rate from the pressure sensor rather than from GNSS.

\paragraph{Empirical confirmation.}
Figure~\ref{fig:altnoise} compares the two channels on \num{3000} flights per discipline
(Appendix~\ref{app:psd}) \impldetails{impl:altchannel}. At low frequency the two agree,
tracking the real climbs and glides; at high frequency the \emph{typical} flight is
comparable in both, the median spectra nearly coinciding at the plateau set by the metre
rounding common to both channels (panel~c). What separates the channels is the
\emph{spread} across flights: the barometric band stays tight, while the GNSS band fans out
by one to two orders of magnitude towards the Nyquist frequency, from receiver tracking
noise amplified by poor vertical dilution of precision on a substantial minority of
flights. Since the vertical velocity is a finite difference that amplifies exactly that
band, and which flights have poor GNSS reception cannot be known in advance, the uniformly
clean barometric channel is the safe choice --- a deliberate departure from the reference
study~\cite{vilpellet2026}, whose pipeline derives the vertical coordinate from GNSS.
\end{revblock}

\begin{revblock}
Four practical points follow. A trajectory uses a single channel end-to-end, never spliced
within a flight, since a mid-flight switch offset by tens of metres would inject a spurious
jump; which channel it uses is recorded as a per-flight attribute, so the two source groups
stay separable downstream \impldetails{impl:altchannel}. A flight is used with its
barometric channel when it has one; the few individual fixes with a missing value are
dropped rather than back-filled from GNSS --- \StatScanParaBaroMissFlightsPct\,\% of
barometric paraglider flights (\StatScanHangBaroMissFlightsPct\,\% hang) miss any value at
all, with a median of \StatScanParaBaroMissMedianFixes\ missing fixes among them against
$\sim\!10^4$ in a typical track \impldetails{impl:altchannel}, the hole handled downstream
like any other gap (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). A logger with no pressure sensor writes the
whole channel as zero, or a stuck sensor writes a constant non-zero value: a flight counts
as barometric only when the channel covers at least
\SI{\PreprocBaroPresentMinPct}{\percent} of its fixes
\emph{and} its total range exceeds \SI{\PreprocBaroMinRangeM}{\metre}, else it falls back to
GNSS, unfiltered --- a non-negligible minority (Figure~\ref{fig:altnoise}d), measured at
\StatScanParaNoBaroPct\,\% of paraglider and \StatScanHangNoBaroPct\,\% of hang-glider
flights scanned \impldetails{impl:altchannel}. That liveness test catches only a sensor
dead for the whole recording, not one that freezes partway through, and nothing downstream
closes that gap \impldetails{impl:altchannel}. Nor does the presence test check what it
falls back to: \StatScanParaBaroBorderlineCount\ paraglider flights sit just under its
cut rather than at zero, and for these the assumption that GNSS is the more complete
channel goes untested, since nothing in the pipeline measures GNSS completeness to
compare it against \impldetails{impl:altchannel}.

\paragraph{Mixed altitude sources.}
The mix is acceptable. The horizontal, two-dimensional transport analysis --- the main
subject of this work --- does not use the altitude at all, so every flight is usable there
regardless of channel. Altitude enters only the vertical velocity, hence the segmentation
and the phase-resolved observables, where the two source groups are kept distinct through
the per-flight record above and checked for compatibility on the fraction of airborne time
each phase accounts for, per discipline \impldetails{impl:altchannel}. Forcing GNSS on
every flight to avoid the mix would needlessly degrade the vertical signal of the
barometric majority to match the worst loggers.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Fix-level cleaning}
\label{sec:fixlevel}
\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Geometry on the raw coordinates.}
The cleaning runs on the raw geodetic coordinates $(\varphi_i,\lambda_i)$, before the
Cartesian conversion (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}), so distances between fixes are great-circle
(haversine) distances~\cite{sklearn_haversine} on a sphere of mean radius
$R_\oplus=\SI{6371}{\kilo\metre}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:haversine}
\Delta s_i = 2R_\oplus\arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\!\frac{\varphi_{i+1}-\varphi_i}{2}
  + \cos\varphi_i\cos\varphi_{i+1}\,\sin^2\!\frac{\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_i}{2}}\,,
\end{equation}
giving the horizontal speed $v_{xy,i}=\Delta s_i/(t_{i+1}-t_i)$ and, from the barometric
altitude, the vertical velocity $v_{z,i}=(h_{i+1}-h_i)/(t_{i+1}-t_i)$; the same distance also
yields the whole-flight \emph{path length} $L=\sum_i\Delta s_i$ and \emph{extent}
$D=\max_i\Delta s(\mathrm{fix}_0,\mathrm{fix}_i)$. The spherical approximation is ample:
replacing the WGS84 ellipsoid by a sphere perturbs an inter-fix distance by at most
$\lesssim\SI{0.3}{\percent}$ (the flattening scale) \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}, about
\SI{3}{\centi\metre} on a \SI{10}{\metre} step, far below the metre-scale noise of
consumer GNSS receivers.

\paragraph{Design principle.}
Corrupt or physically impossible fixes must be removed without discarding the flight
around them; an over-aggressive cleaning biases the result as much as dirty data would. A
spurious fix that survives lies at worst tens of metres from the true track, a
displacement the smoothing absorbs, whereas a genuine fix removed from a slow, localized
phase (a search, a climb) erases a real trapping event that nothing downstream can
restore. The cleaning therefore defaults to keeping the data: a fix is removed only on
positive evidence of a defect, and no detector deletes a fix that still carries a usable
horizontal position --- a corrupt altitude is marked missing and restored at resampling
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), a corrupt horizontal position is deleted outright.

\paragraph{Three classes of defect.}
Past the format-validity check made at decode time (Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}), a surviving
fix can still be wrong in one of three ways, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cleaning}. A fix
\emph{off the trend} --- a position spike, or a jump out and back within one step --- is
found by a robust local-outlier test, the \emph{Hampel identifier}~\cite{hampel1974,daviesgather1993}.
Let the window of fix $k$ be the fixes within $\pm w$ of $t_k$, and
$\tilde\varphi_k,\tilde\lambda_k$ the component-wise window medians. The residual is the
great-circle distance from that median point,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hampelresid}
  r_k=\Delta s\big((\varphi_k,\lambda_k),(\tilde\varphi_k,\tilde\lambda_k)\big),
\end{equation}
with local scale $\operatorname{MAD}_k=\operatorname{med}\{r_j:|t_j-t_k|\le w\}$, and the
fix is flagged when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hampel}
  r_k > \max\!\big(k\,\sigma_k,\ \varepsilon_{\min}\big),
  \qquad \sigma_k = 1.4826\operatorname{MAD}_k,
\end{equation}
$\sigma_k$ the normalized MAD~\cite{rousseeuw1993}: a departure of more than $k$ robust
standard deviations \emph{and} an absolute floor $\varepsilon_{\min}$ of a few times the
GPS noise. The working values are $k=\PreprocHampelK$, a half-window
$w=\SI{\PreprocHampelWindowS}{\second}$ and
$\varepsilon_{\min}=\SI{\PreprocHampelEpsM}{\metre}$, with the test evaluated only where
the window holds at least \PreprocHampelMinFixes\ fixes (rationale in
Sec.~\ref{impl:fixlevel}). A flag does not delete: what deletes is the absolute $v_{xy}$
bound, a fix removed when unreachable from the last accepted fix at that bound \emph{and}
the removal of its block lets the track rejoin within it. The identifier flags and
attributes, and supplies the per-flight anomaly count, but is not required for deletion
\impldetails{impl:fixlevel} --- a genuine tight thermalling circle produces the same
local-median departure as a spike, at a fraction of the speed a spike would imply, so the
protection lies in the speed bound that does the deleting rather than in the flag.
\end{revblock}

A fix \emph{on the trend} agrees with its neighbours yet is still wrong as a record -- a
duplicated or backward timestamp, or a run of frozen positions from a lost GNSS lock; its
residual is near zero, so it escapes the outlier test and needs a structural rule instead.

A value \emph{out of range} concerns the vertical channel: an impossible altitude or
climb rate is caught by an absolute bound that needs no context.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{An unreturned vertical step.}
The $|v_z|$ rule recognises two shapes: an \emph{out-and-back} spike is censored, a
coherent \emph{run} is a sustained manoeuvre and is kept. A single super-threshold step
that is never undone --- a barometric re-reference, or one corrupt record --- is neither,
and could carry a written vertical velocity of up to \SI{5117}{\metre\per\second} before
being caught, on \SI{8.3}{\percent} of paraglider and \SI{6.2}{\percent} of hang-glider
flights. The shape is detected and counted per flight, as \path{n_alt_level_shift}, so it
is visible rather than silently present \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}; what to \emph{do}
about it is left open, since the vertical channel feeds the phase segmentation of
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} rather than the horizontal transport measurement of
Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global}, so the choice belongs with that analysis.

\paragraph{Why the vertical channel is treated differently.}
The obvious symmetric design --- a Hampel test on $z$ too, corroborated by the climb-rate
bound --- is wrong here, for three reasons \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}. An absolute bound
is sufficient on $z$ and insufficient on $xy$: the vertical envelope is narrow and known,
while the same horizontal spike passes or fails the speed bound depending on the logger's
cadence, so only the horizontal case needs a local, scale-free test. A Hampel test on $z$
would flag the physics: the residual from a local median is largest exactly at thermal
entries and exits, the transitions the phase segmentation is built on, and the vertical
channel lacks the corroborating witness (speed) that lets a horizontal circle be told from
a spike. And the costs are asymmetric: a false vertical flag is cheap to fix by
interpolation except across a thermal entry, where interpolation erases the very
transition it should preserve --- so keeping the vertical rule to a bound that only fires
on the physically impossible avoids that failure mode altogether.

The absolute bounds are placed on data, where the per-fix distributions of
Fig.~\ref{fig:fixlevel} stop decaying; the Hampel and frozen-lock parameters are working
values fixed a priori (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}), checked a posteriori on the fraction
of fixes each rule removes (\emph{Completeness, the integrity gate, and validation} below).

\paragraph{Removal policy.}
A speed bound alone cannot attribute a defect, since the speed belongs to the segment
\emph{between} two fixes: attribution comes from the bounded-removal test, the block whose
removal restores the trend being the corrupt one, so an isolated spike is removed without
touching its neighbours. What happens next depends on \emph{which} value is corrupt, by a
single criterion --- keep every genuine horizontal sample, never hide a hole in the
horizontal record --- and in either case the corrupt value is simply discarded, with
reconstruction deferred to the single resampling step (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). A corrupt
\emph{altitude} leaves the fix kept and only that channel marked missing, since the
horizontal position and timestamp are intact and still sample the path; a corrupt
\emph{horizontal position or time} deletes the whole fix, because keeping it with the
position marked missing would hide the hole from the gap rule that decides whether to
bridge or split (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), and an invented straight line could then be laid
across a full thermalling circle \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}. The IGC validity flag needs
no rule of its own: a \texttt{V} flag certifies a degraded GNSS altitude
(Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}), so on a barometric flight the fix is untouched, and on a
GNSS-fallback flight it reduces to the corrupt-altitude case.

\paragraph{An impossible step is a boundary, never flown path.}
What must hold whatever the scan resolves is the invariant the rest of the pipeline leans
on: \emph{no step between two surviving fixes may break the $v_{xy}$ bound}. Where a block
is kept because nothing could rejoin it, the step by which the track returns from it is
itself impossible and, left alone, enters a segment as flown motion --- a jump of tens of
kilometres inside one second, in the archive. Such a step is a transition whose course is
unknown, so it is made a segment boundary on the same footing as a long gap
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), counted per flight as \path{n_boundaried}
\impldetails{impl:fixlevel}.
\end{revblock}

\rev{A corrupt first fix is a blind spot no bounded-removal test can close: the scan
carries the last accepted fix as an anchor and never questions its own first one, and that
fix becomes the origin of the local frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}), so the error does not
average out with record length --- five paraglider flights left the first full-archive run
some \SI{4500}{\kilo\metre} from their own origin, enough to raise the ensemble MSD by
seven orders of magnitude at the shortest lags. Two attribution rules were tried and both
failed, in opposite directions \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}, so the discontinuity is left as
a discontinuity and the guarantee is taken one stage later: a record out of reach of its
own first fix is refused whole (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter}). The invariant
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} rests on, $|\mathbf{r}(t)|\le v_{xy}^{\max}\,t$, holds either way,
checked directly on the written tables (Sec.~\ref{impl:verify}).}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Frozen-position runs (GNSS lock loss).}
When the receiver loses its GNSS lock, many loggers repeat the last position for tens of
seconds while the clock keeps running. Such a run passes every test above: horizontal
speed zero, altitude in range, valid timestamps. Kept, it contaminates the tail of the
waiting-time density $\psi(\tau)$ with a false long wait; cut too aggressively, it removes
real trapping events (tight thermalling climbs, also slow and horizontally localized) from
the same tail \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}. A run is therefore cut only when three
independent signatures agree:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Spatial diameter.} The run's bounding diameter --- the largest great-circle
        distance between any two of its fixes --- stays below $\delta_{xy}$
        \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}; a circling climb instead traces a disc of diameter
        $2R\approx\SIrange{30}{100}{\metre}$, at least twice $\delta_{xy}$, and clears the
        test on geometry alone.
  \item \emph{An independent witness --- the barometric altitude.} A GNSS lock-loss
        freezes only the GNSS position; a horizontally motionless run whose barometric
        altitude keeps changing at a plausible rate is a tight climb, and is kept. Only a
        byte-identical coordinate repeat may overrule this witness.
  \item \emph{Recorder declarations.} A byte-identical repeat of the coordinate fields is
        conclusive on its own --- no moving receiver rewrites the same bits. The
        \texttt{V} flag and a zeroed GNSS altitude are weaker, certifying a degraded GNSS
        altitude rather than a frozen fix, and decide only on a GNSS-fallback flight,
        where no barometric witness exists.
\end{enumerate}
A run is classified as a loss of signal only when spatially collapsed, witnessed, and
sufficiently long,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:frozenlock}
\begin{gathered}
  \underbrace{\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{run})<\delta_{xy}}_{\text{(i) collapsed}}
  \;\wedge\;
  \underbrace{W}_{\text{witness (ii)/(iii)}}
  \;\wedge\;
  \underbrace{\Delta t_{\mathrm{run}}\geq\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}}_{\text{duration}},
  \\[4pt]
  W=
  \begin{cases}
    |\Delta z_{\mathrm{baro}}|<\delta_z \,\vee\, \text{byte-identical} &
      \text{barometric flight,}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{V}/\text{zero-alt} \,\vee\, \text{byte-identical} &
      \text{GNSS-fallback flight.}
  \end{cases}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
and is then marked as a gap and the trajectory \emph{split} there, never interpolated
across, with segments keeping their parent's origin and clock (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).
The working values are $\delta_{xy}=\SI{\PreprocFrozenEpsM}{\metre}$,
$\delta_z=\SI{\PreprocFrozenDeltaZM}{\metre}$ and
$\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}=\SI{\PreprocFrozenTauS}{\second}$, the last of them argued below.

A real thermalling climb fails the cut on two independent instruments at once: a circling
wing turns at $R=v^2/(g\tan\phi)$ \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}, giving
$2R\approx\SI{30}{\metre}$ for the tightest paraglider circles and
$2R\gtrsim\SI{50}{\metre}$ for hang gliders, missing the diameter test~(i) by a factor of
at least $2$--$3$; and a climb of order \SI{1}{\metre\per\second} sustained over
$\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$ moves the barometer well past the
flatness tolerance $\delta_z$. The duration threshold is set on the motion's own timescale
--- about two thermalling periods --- so a run long enough to be cut spans at least two
full circles of any genuine climb: on a barometric flight the rule is therefore
conservative by construction, both margins having to fail at once, well outside their
designed range \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}. Below $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$ the rule
deliberately abstains --- a collapsed run and a genuine slow, localized stretch are not
distinguishable by these signatures at durations of a few tens of seconds --- at a bounded
cost, since a kept short freeze lengthens one waiting time by at most $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$,
swept in the $\psi(\tau)$ sensitivity checks (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming}).
\end{revblock}

\paragraph{Residual corner cases.}
\rev{The frozen-lock rule weakens where the barometric channel is absent, since the GNSS
altitude cannot witness a suspected GNSS lock loss --- it comes from the very receiver
whose lock is in doubt. On GNSS-fallback flights the witness slot of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozenlock} is therefore filled by the recorder's declarations alone, the
diameter and duration conditions unchanged \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}. Three error cases
follow: a tight climb within $\delta_{xy}$ whose fixes carry a \texttt{V} flag is
\emph{wrongly cut} on GNSS-fallback flights, with no barometer to defend it; an undeclared
\emph{jittering} freeze --- re-estimated each second rather than repeated byte-for-byte
--- is \emph{wrongly kept}, also GNSS-fallback only; and a jittered freeze under a
climbing barometer is \emph{kept at bounded cost} on barometric flights, since motion
during the run goes unrecorded (the path length under-counted, a spurious wait inserted)
while the displacement across the run stays correct, the first position after
re-acquisition being exact again. The first two are expected to be rare and cannot be
counted directly, since a rule that fails does so silently; what measures the error rate
instead is the injected-defect calibration below.}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Completeness, the integrity gate, and validation.}
The segmentation (Ch.~\ref{ch:phases}) needs a defined position and altitude at every time
within a \emph{segment} --- a maximal stretch between two splits --- not everywhere;
cleaning does not guarantee this fix by fix, and the guarantee is established one step
later, at resampling (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}) \impldetails{impl:fixlevel}. Cleaning also
counts how much it had to do over the airborne window (defects in a ground phase that
trimming removes anyway must not condemn the flight): a flight whose cleaning removed or
reconstructed more than \SI{\PreprocIntegrityMaxPct}{\percent} of its fixes is dropped
whole.

All detector parameters are validated by one procedure, run once the cleaning is built:
the thresholds are frozen a priori, never tuned on the transport observables; the removal
fraction of each rule is audited over the full archive, expected to touch only a small
share of fixes; each threshold is swept around its working value and judged well placed
when what it removes stays on a plateau rather than growing sharply
\impldetails{impl:fixlevel}; and, since an audit of removals says nothing about what a
rule misses, defects of known size are injected into clean tracks and scored on recovery
--- the only direct measurement of the error rates the firing counts cannot give
\impldetails{impl:fixlevel}.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Trimming of ground phases}
\label{sec:trimming}
\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Outer ground phases (take-off and landing).}
The logger starts recording before take-off and stops after landing; these ground phases
must be stripped before any kinematic analysis, using the horizontal speed $v_{xy}$ alone.
With $v_0=\SI{\PreprocTakeoffSpeedMps}{\metre\per\second}$ and
$T_0=\SI{\PreprocSustainedS}{\second}$ (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}), the retained
airborne segment is $[t_{\mathrm{on}},t_{\mathrm{off}}]$ with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trimming}
  t_{\mathrm{on}}=\min\bigl\{t:\ v_{xy}>v_0\ \text{on}\ [t,\,t+T_0]\bigr\},
  \qquad
  t_{\mathrm{off}}=\max\bigl\{t:\ v_{xy}>v_0\ \text{on}\ [t-T_0,\,t]\bigr\},
\end{equation}
the same sustained-speed rule read forward and time-reversed, so a wing soaring into wind
at near-zero ground speed is not mistaken for having landed. $T_0$ is set between two
scales: from below it must outlast a ground gust or GPS glitch (a few seconds), from above
it costs little when wrong, misfiring by at most $T_0$ at each end against the
\SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute} minimum duration a retained flight must reach
\impldetails{impl:trimming}. A flight with no sustained stretch at all is discarded. The
one structural caveat is a slow phase sitting right at the start or end of the flight (a
launch straight into ridge lift) being clipped along with the ground phase; the trimmed
fraction is recorded per flight so the exposure can be bounded and the tail inspected
directly \impldetails{impl:trimming}.

\paragraph{Interior ground stints (mid-flight landings).}
A mid-flight landing and relaunch recorded in one log would otherwise survive as an
interior near-zero-speed stint and read downstream as a false long wait in the tail of
$\psi(\tau)$. An interior-ground detector, built like the frozen-lock rule, cuts a stint
only when \emph{both} $v_{xy}<v_0$ continuously for at least
$T_{\mathrm{ground}}=\SI{\PreprocInteriorGroundMin}{\minute}$ --- far beyond any search
phase --- \emph{and} the adopted altitude channel is flat over the whole stint, its
detrended central span staying within
$\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}=\SI{\PreprocGroundFlatnessM}{\metre}$, the same independent-sensor
argument as Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozenlock}. The span is \emph{central}, not a full range,
because a full range grows without bound with the stint length on noise alone and would
fail a motionless pilot on a long stint while passing the same pilot on a short one
\impldetails{impl:trimming}; and on a GNSS-fallback flight, where the tolerance for the
noisier channel has not yet been calibrated, the guard abstains conservatively rather than
risk excising a genuine climb, at the cost of never flagging a mid-flight landing on those
flights \impldetails{impl:trimming}.

A cut stint is excised and the flight split there, exactly as at a long gap
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}); the flight-level cuts of Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter} are not
re-applied to the individual segments, but to the \emph{parent} flight --- its airborne
duration and path length summed over its segments --- since a segment is short because a
gap happened to fall there, which says nothing about whether the flight is genuinely
cross-country \impldetails{impl:uniform}. Trimming therefore runs before the flight-level
cuts in the pipeline order (step~(iii) before step~(iv)), so a duration or path length is
only meaningful once the ground phases are gone. Stints shorter than $T_{\mathrm{ground}}$
are not cut but \emph{flagged}: a wait with sustained $v_{xy}<v_0$ and a flat barometric
trace is marked suspect whatever its length, and the $\psi(\tau)$ fits are re-run with and
without the suspect waits as a sensitivity check, reported per discipline alongside the
fits.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Flight-level filtering}
\label{sec:flightfilter}
\begin{revblock}
After cleaning and trimming, some flights are still unfit for the ensemble analysis and
are dropped whole. Too aggressive a cut biases the transport statistics --- a high minimum
duration, for instance, preferentially keeps successful long flights and inflates the
apparent MSD exponent at long lags --- so filtering uses a \emph{minimal-threshold
strategy}: each threshold is set to the smallest value that still clears non-genuine
flights (sled runs, tows, aborted or truncated logs), read off the full-archive
distribution and retained-fraction curve of its own quantity (Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc}), on
the stretch where that curve is flat --- where moving the cut changes almost nothing, the
signature of removing junk rather than signal. Five track criteria gate a flight, plus a
metadata stratifier; the two that sum over the flight (duration, path) accumulate
\emph{within} a segment and never across a boundary, whether left by an earlier stage or an
inter-fix gap wider than the resampling's own $g_{\max}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), so a
flight is measured over the intervals it will actually be analysed over
\impldetails{impl:flightfilter}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \emph{Recorded duration} $T\geq\SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute}$, with an
        upper sanity bound $T\leq\SI{\PreprocMaxDurationH}{\hour}$ against loggers left
        running past the flight: \num{\StatScanParaOverlongCount} paraglider
        ``flights'' of \SIrange{\StatScanParaOverlongMinH}{\StatScanParaOverlongMaxH}{\hour}
        (none among hang gliders), which would poison the long-lag analyses.
  \item \emph{Flown path length} $L\geq\SI{\PreprocMinPathKm}{\kilo\metre}$, catching
        \num{\StatScanParaShortPathCount} of \num{\StatScanParaLongEnoughCount} paraglider
        and \num{\StatScanHangShortPathCount} of \num{\StatScanHangLongEnoughCount}
        hang-glider flights above the duration floor --- order $10^{-3}$ of either
        ensemble, duration already clearing tows and sled runs.
  \item \emph{Altitude activity.} The adopted channel's whole-flight range must reach
        \SI{\PreprocMinAltRangeM}{\metre}: a cross-country flight of at least
        \SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute} lives by climbing, so a record spanning less
        was not soaring. The cut is cheap: moving it from \SI{75}{\metre} to
        \SI{\PreprocMinAltRangeM}{\metre} changes the retained share by under two points
        \impldetails{impl:flightfilter}. The cached census holds only the barometric
        extremes, so on a GNSS-fallback flight this cut, and its diagnostic in
        Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc}c,f, is read on barometric flights alone
        \impldetails{impl:flightfilter}.
  \item \emph{Plausibility.} The mean ground speed of the retained track, $L/T$, must not
        exceed the discipline's own fix-level bound $v_{xy}^{\max}$ --- the weakest
        statement that catches a log corrupt beyond fix-level repair. On the present
        archive it removes no flight at all, a result rather than a redundancy: an
        impossible step is now made a segment boundary before it can enter $L$, so what
        such a record fails instead is the reach criterion below
        \impldetails{impl:flightfilter}.
  \item \emph{Reach.} No fix may lie further from the first than the bound could have
        carried it in the elapsed time, $\Delta s(\text{fix}_0,\text{fix}_i)\le
        v_{xy}^{\max}\,t_i$ for every $i$ --- stated per fix, not as the whole-flight
        extent against the whole duration, since that weaker form would license a fix
        \SI{100}{\kilo\metre} out ten seconds into the record on the grounds that the
        flight went on for three hours. This is what catches a corrupt first fix once its
        step is no longer summed into $L$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}): it removes
        \num{\StatPipeParaDropExtentOutOfReachOfTheFirstFix} paraglider and
        \num{\StatPipeHangDropExtentOutOfReachOfTheFirstFix} hang-glider flights, order
        $10^{-3}$ of either ensemble, and is checked again on the written tables as the
        invariant $|\mathbf{r}(t)|\le v_{xy}^{\max}t$ the whole of
        Chapter~\ref{ch:global} assumes (Sec.~\ref{impl:verify}).
  \item \emph{Glider class} (metadata). Flights whose \texttt{aile\_class} cannot be
        normalised (Sec.~\ref{sec:glider}) are dropped from class-resolved sub-analyses
        but kept in the pooled one; stratification by discipline is already possible.
\end{enumerate}
A further criterion, sampling regularity, is applied later, where a flight's time base is
examined (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}) \impldetails{impl:flightfilter}.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{From geographic to local Cartesian coordinates}
\label{sec:enu}
\rev{The cleaned, trimmed track is next mapped from geographic to local Cartesian
coordinates: resampling and smoothing operate on the coordinates \emph{componentwise}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:preproc}), and latitudes and longitudes cannot play that role, being angles
on a curved surface whose componentwise differences are not displacements. The mapping
proceeds in two steps.}

\paragraph{Step 1: geodetic to ECEF.} Each fix is converted to Earth-Centred,
Earth-Fixed (ECEF) coordinates on the WGS84 ellipsoid (semi-major axis
$a=\SI{6378137}{\metre}$, flattening $f=1/298.257223563$, $e^2=f(2-f)$)~\cite{nga_wgs84,esa_navipedia_ellipsoidal_cartesian},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ecef}
N(\varphi)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}},\qquad
\begin{aligned}
X &= (N+h)\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\\
Y &= (N+h)\cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\\
Z &= \big(N(1-e^2)+h\big)\sin\varphi,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$N(\varphi)$ the prime-vertical radius of curvature and $h$ the ellipsoidal height along
the normal, derived step by step in Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}.

\paragraph{Geodetic versus geocentric latitude.} \rev{The latitude $\varphi$ is
\emph{geodetic}: the angle between the equatorial plane and the ellipsoid \emph{normal}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ellipsoid}), not the geocentric latitude $\psi$ measured to the Earth's
centre $O$, since the normal does not pass through it. The gap
$\varphi-\psi\approx(e^2/2)\sin2\varphi$ (Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}) peaks at
$\varphi=45^\circ$ and reaches $\approx0.19^\circ$, which if confused would misplace a fix
by up to ${\sim}\SI{20}{\kilo\metre}$. No conversion is needed here: WGS84 --- hence the GPS
receiver and the IGC \texttt{B} records (Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}) --- already reports
geodetic latitude, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:ecef} with $N(\varphi)$ is precisely the transform
that consumes it; these are also the $(\varphi_0,\lambda_0)$ that orient the local frame of
Step~2 (Figure~\ref{fig:enu-rotation}).}

  \centering

\paragraph{Step 2: ECEF to local ENU.} \rev{Taking the first fix $(\varphi_0,\lambda_0,h_0)$
of the \emph{trimmed} track as the origin --- close to but not the take-off point, since
the ground phase has been removed (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming}) --- the ECEF displacement
$\Delta\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_0$ is rotated into the local East--North--Up (ENU)
frame tangent to the ellipsoid at that point,}
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}E\\N\\U\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\sin\lambda_0 & \cos\lambda_0 & 0\\
-\sin\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0 & -\sin\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0 & \cos\varphi_0\\
\cos\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0 & \cos\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0 & \sin\varphi_0
\end{pmatrix}
\Delta\mathbf{X},
\end{equation}
derived in Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}: an orthogonal matrix of unit determinant, so lengths
and angles are preserved and only the axes change. This ENU frame is the local Cartesian
frame the whole analysis is carried out in ($E$ east, $N$ north, $U$ along the local
vertical); the ECEF coordinates of Step~1 are only an intermediate representation, no
observable is ever expressed in them. Over the spatial extent of a single flight the
tangent-plane approximation is accurate to well below the GPS noise: it shortens a
distance $d$ along the surface by $\approx d^3/(24R_\oplus^2)$, about \SI{1}{\centi\metre}
at $d=\SI{20}{\kilo\metre}$ and \SI{13}{\centi\metre} at \SI{50}{\kilo\metre}
(Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}).

\subsection{Uniform sampling within a flight}
\label{sec:uniform}
\begin{revblock}
The smoothing and differentiation below assume a \emph{uniform} sampling interval, so each
surviving flight's time base is examined next. Loggers record at a fixed nominal rate ---
paragliders mostly at \SI{1}{\second} (\StatScanParaDtOneSecPct\,\% of flights), hang
gliders spread across several rates from \SIrange{1}{10}{\second} (Fig.~\ref{fig:sampling})
--- but the realised series can carry jitter and drop-outs. Each flight is kept at its
\emph{native} rate, since the derivatives are read off the local polynomial the smoothing
fits to each flight separately rather than by finite differences across flights
(Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}): what matters is uniformity \emph{within} a flight, not across
flights.

Per flight, with $\Delta t$ the median inter-fix interval, three cases arise. A
\textbf{uniform} series (all intervals equal to tolerance) is used as is. A
\textbf{mildly irregular} one --- isolated jitter or drop-outs, no gap beyond the split
bound $g_{\max}$ and less than \SI{\PreprocMaxMissingPct}{\percent} of the grid missing
--- is resampled onto the uniform grid at its native $\Delta t$, interpolating across the
small gaps only. One \textbf{broken by a long gap} --- native, or opened where cleaning
removed a frozen-lock run or a position discontinuity, both sides genuine and only the
transition unknown --- is not bridged, since interpolating across it would fabricate
dynamics the smoothing could not tell from real motion: the flight is \emph{split} at the
gap into independently analysed segments. The missing-fraction check runs \emph{per
segment}, after splitting rather than among the flight-level cuts, since it needs the time
base and the splits to exist first \impldetails{impl:uniform}: a segment exceeding
\SI{\PreprocMaxMissingPct}{\percent} residual missing fraction is dropped alone, and the
flight is lost only if no segment survives.

\paragraph{The split bound has two scales.}
The relative part, $\PreprocMaxGapFactor\,\Delta t$, tolerates isolated dropped fixes in
proportion to the cadence but alone would let a slow logger bridge holes approaching the
thermalling period, letting an interpolated straight line replace a full circle. The
absolute cap of \SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second} is pinned by three constraints: at least
twice the slowest common cadence, so a single missed fix never splits a flight; at most
about two thirds of a thermalling period, so a bridged gap can never hide a full circle;
and well below $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$, for consistency with the frozen-lock rule. A floor
at $2\,\Delta t$ keeps the first constraint true at every cadence, giving
$g_{\max}=\min\big(\PreprocMaxGapFactor\,\Delta t,\,\max(\SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second},\,2\,\Delta
t)\big)$: the relative bound sets it at the dominant \SI{1}{\second} rate, the absolute cap
from \SIrange{2}{10}{\second}, the floor above that \impldetails{impl:uniform}. On the
census this leaves \StatScanParaGapSplitCombPct\,\% of paraglider and
\StatScanHangGapSplitCombPct\,\% of hang-glider flights with at least one split, against
\StatScanParaGapSplitRelPct\,\% and \StatScanHangGapSplitRelPct\,\% had the relative part
acted alone; since a split censors the phase in progress, preferentially removing long
phases from the duration distributions, the cap is treated as a working value and swept in
the validation procedure of Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel} \impldetails{impl:uniform}. The bound
is the same for every channel, altitude included: the interpolation error is set by the
signal's curvature, not its speed, and a wide altitude gap would erase the climb--glide
transitions that $v_z$, the segmentation's discriminant, exists to resolve
\impldetails{impl:uniform}.

\paragraph{Consequences of a split.}
A split is bookkeeping rather than a new trajectory: the ENU conversion (step~v) runs
before this step, so every fix already carries coordinates and elapsed time referred to
the parent origin, and \textbf{a segment keeps its parent's origin and clock}. Ensemble
quantities (the MSD, the propagator) need the position at a given elapsed time, not the
path in between, so a split costs them nothing; quantities that \emph{accumulate along the
path} (path length, phase duration, any time-averaged window) are always summed inside one
segment, never across a gap. Three consequences follow: a phase truncated at a boundary is
censored and excluded from the duration fits and $\psi(\tau)$ rather than counted as
complete \impldetails{impl:uniform}; a segment starting at parent time $t_0>0$ is used as
an observation window onto a process already $t_0$ old, not re-zeroed into a fresh flight,
since re-zeroing would mix statistics of young and aged trajectories; and the flight-level
cuts of Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter} are not re-applied to segments, which would bias
against gaps concentrated in particular conditions, so a segment need only be long enough
to carry the smoothing window, $T_{\mathrm{seg}}=\SI{\PreprocMinSegmentS}{\second}$
(Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}).

\paragraph{Interpolation across short gaps.}
What is filled is only the short holes, up to $g_{\max}$, inside a segment, per channel.
The time base and a channel come apart in one place: $g_{\max}$ bounds the interval
between two \emph{fixes}, not an interval over which a fix exists but carries no value ---
the vertical channel can be absent while the horizontal record is perfect, so no time gap
opens and no split is declared, and the linear fill spans the hole whatever its length.
Such a hole does not split the flight, since the horizontal trajectory Chapter~\ref{ch:global}
measures is intact across it; but the reconstruction must be visible, tracked by its own
per-fix flag \texttt{z\_reconstructed} and per-segment share, distinct from
\texttt{interpolated} (a statement about the time base alone) \impldetails{impl:uniform}.

The horizontal coordinates are interpolated by a monotone piecewise cubic (a
shape-preserving Hermite interpolant, chosen so it cannot swing outside the range of the
points it passes through, which an unconstrained spline could) and the adopted altitude
linearly, an asymmetry that follows from what each channel is used for: the altitude is
differentiated once more before it is read, so an interpolation artefact in $z$ propagates
into a phase assignment where the same artefact in $E$ or $N$ is absorbed by the smoothing
\impldetails{impl:uniform}. The linear error over a hole of width $g$ is bounded by
$\max|\ddot z|\,g^2/8$ \impldetails{impl:uniform}; holes are bridged inside a phase far
more often than across a transition, where the barometric signal has a nearly constant
slope, so the error stays at the metre scale even at the \SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second} cap.

The result is the completeness invariant promised at cleaning: within a retained segment,
every grid time carries a defined $(E,N,z)$. Every filled point carries a flag ---
\texttt{interpolated} where the time base had no fix, \texttt{z\_reconstructed} where a fix
was there but its altitude was not --- so downstream statistics can distinguish measured
from reconstructed samples, and a phase boundary the segmentation places inside a bridged
stretch is censored exactly as at a split. The gap bound $g_{\max}$ is the
sampling-regularity criterion of the flight-level filtering (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter});
Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} makes the fraction of flights it leaves whole auditable. One caveat
carries downstream: a few observables, the turning angle above all, are intrinsically
$\Delta t$-dependent by definition, so where such observables are compared across flights,
the ensemble is stratified by rate or restricted to the dominant one.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Smoothing and differentiation}
\label{sec:savgol}
\begin{revblock}
Velocity and acceleration drive the kinematics and the segmentation, but differencing raw
GPS coordinate series amplifies the high-frequency noise of Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}. A
\textbf{Savitzky--Golay} filter~\cite{savitzky1964} solves both problems at once: it fits a
low-order polynomial to a sliding window by least squares and reads off that polynomial, or
its derivatives, at the window centre, returning a smoothed value, velocity and
acceleration in one pass per component.\footnote{In the window of $w$ samples centred on
$t_i$, the coefficients of $P(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_k\,(t-t_i)^k$ minimize
$\sum_j\big(x_j-P(t_j)\big)^2$; the smoothed value is $a_0$, the velocity $a_1$, the
acceleration $2a_2$, and since the grid is uniform the whole filter is a convolution with
precomputed coefficients~\cite{savitzky1964}.} The fit is done in units of samples, so
velocity and acceleration in physical units are $a_1/\Delta t$ and $2a_2/\Delta t^2$ with
the flight's own $\Delta t$ --- the only place the cadence enters, and why flights of
different cadences need no common grid. The window never crosses a segment boundary; near
one it is fitted on the nearest full window inside the segment and evaluated off-centre,
carrying higher variance, flagged the same way as an interpolated point
\impldetails{impl:savgol}.

The two hyperparameters, window length $w$ (odd, in samples) and polynomial order $p$, are
fixed from the measured noise spectrum rather than by eye. From the ensemble Welch spectrum
of the raw ENU components, the frequency $f_c$ of the knee where the dynamical signal meets
the flat GPS noise floor gives a smoothing timescale $\tau_c=1/f_c$; $w$ is $\tau_c/\Delta
t$ rounded up to the nearest odd integer, floored at $w=5$ (the smallest window a cubic fit
meaningfully determines). At the dominant \SI{1}{\second} cadence the two rules coincide;
at every slower cadence the floor binds and the window spans more than $\tau_c$, a cost
accepted since those cadences carry little noise to remove at the noise scale in the first
place and are excluded from $\Delta t$-sensitive observables by the rate stratification
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). The order $p=\PreprocSavgolOrder$ is the lowest that works: an
acceleration needs curvature ($p\geq2$), and one order more is needed for the velocity,
since on a symmetric window an even-order term leaves the odd-order coefficients untouched
--- the velocity of a $p=2$ fit is identical to a straight-line fit's, and $p=3$ is the
first order that responds to the acceleration varying across the window
\impldetails{impl:savgol}. The vertical is treated on the same footing as the horizontal:
the three channels' knees coincide at $f_c\approx\SI{0.2}{\hertz}$
($\tau_c=\SI{\PreprocSavgolTauCS}{\second}$), since the flat high-frequency floor of every
channel comes from the IGC format's own rounding rather than from receiver noise, so what
distinguishes the noisy GNSS minority is how high its floor sits, not where the knee is
\impldetails{impl:savgol}.

The choice is checked on a held-out sample against three criteria: the residual spectrum
(raw minus smoothed) flat above $f_c$; the smoothed kinematics within the physical bounds
of Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}; and stability when $w$ is changed by one step
\impldetails{impl:savgol}.

\paragraph{A fourth criterion, on the observable itself.}
A fourth criterion is a property of what the filter is for: the smoothing must not remove
the displacement the transport analysis is about to measure. The window is floored at
\PreprocSavgolMinWindow\ samples, binding for every logger slower than \SI{1}{\hertz}, so
in seconds the window is $\max(\tau_c,\,\PreprocSavgolMinWindow\,\Delta t)$: \SI{5}{\second}
at a \SI{1}{\second} cadence, \SI{50}{\second} at \SI{10}{\second}, reaching the lower end
of the range Chapter~\ref{ch:global} fits its exponent over. Measured on synthetic
trajectories of known exponent ($\alpha=1.8$, the regime Chapter~\ref{ch:global} finds) and
no measurement noise, the filtered MSD sits within \SI{2.3}{\percent} of the true one at a
lag of two samples and within \SI{1}{\percent} from three samples upward: a cubic filter
reproduces any locally cubic trajectory exactly, and a strongly correlated trajectory is
locally close to cubic. What does move the short-lag MSD is measurement noise, in the
opposite direction: independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ adds
${\approx}2\sigma^2$ at every lag, and with $\sigma=\SI{3}{\metre}$ the measured-to-true
ratio is $8.1$ at one sample, falling to $1.05$ at twelve and $1.003$ at sixty-four ---
smoothing de-biases rather than erases it, and the floor it leaves,
$2\sigma^2\approx\SI{18}{\metre\squared}$, sits orders of magnitude below the measured MSD
everywhere in the fitted range \impldetails{impl:savgol}. The consequence is that the
first decade of Fig.~\ref{fig:msd} is instrument, not transport, and the fit does not begin
there; a second, related effect is that a flight answers a lag with its own nearest fix,
so a logger slower than \SI{1}{\hertz} shows a short-lag staircase --- both effects die out
well below the fit range.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Notation}
\label{sec:notation}
Throughout:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Horizontal position.} $\mathbf{r}(t)=\big(x(t),y(t)\big)=\big(E(t),N(t)\big)$,
        in this local frame. By construction each trajectory starts at the horizontal
        origin, $\mathbf{r}(0)=\mathbf{0}$.
  \item \textbf{Vertical position.} $z(t)$, the adopted altitude channel
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}). Unlike $\mathbf{r}$, it is \emph{not} re-zeroed: it
        is kept at its measured value, so the height at the start of free flight is
        retained (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}).
  \item \textbf{Time.} $t$, the elapsed flight time, with the clock reset to $t=0$ at the
        start of free flight (the first fix of the trimmed track, Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming}).
        A segment created by a split at a long gap keeps its parent flight's origin and
        clock (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).
  \item \textbf{Velocity.} $\mathbf{v}(t)=\dot{\mathbf{r}}(t)$, the horizontal velocity,
        with magnitude the horizontal speed $v_{xy}=|\mathbf{v}|$; $v_z=\dot z$, the
        vertical velocity.
  \item \textbf{Displacement.} $\Delta\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}(t_0+t)-\mathbf{r}(t_0)$, the
        horizontal displacement over a lag $t$.
\end{itemize}
A terminological rule holds throughout the document: a bare \enquote{position} means
$\mathbf{r}$, the horizontal one; a statement about the full three-dimensional point says
\emph{3D position} explicitly.

\paragraph{Isotropy}
The transport analysis would like to study the one-dimensional marginal of the propagator
in place of the full two-dimensional density (Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global}), a substantial
simplification. The reduction is legitimate only if the process is isotropic, and isotropy
cannot be assumed a priori here. It's important to highlight that a single flight could be
strongly directed: a chosen route, the orography, the valley winds. Isotropy can therefore
hold at best at the level of the \emph{ensemble}, and the check is run there.

The primary test is the per-component variance ratio: for an isotropic process
$\langle E(t)^2\rangle=\langle N(t)^2\rangle=\mathrm{MSD}(t)/2$ at every $t$, so the ratio
$\langle E(t)^2\rangle/\langle N(t)^2\rangle$ should be identically $1$. The check is
necessary, not sufficient: isotropy proper asks whether the conditional distributions
$P(E(t)=x\mid\mathbf{r}_0,t_0)$ and $P(N(t)=x\mid\mathbf{r}_0,t_0)$ (here
$\mathbf{r}_0=\mathbf{0}$ and $t_0=0$, take-off) coincide, of which equal variances are
only one necessary consequence. Operationally the ratio screens whether that stronger
comparison is worth pursuing at all: if the 10--90\% band intersects unity over a zone of
lags, the two distributions could still coincide there; where it does not, the weaker
condition already fails and isotropy is ruled out without needing the fuller comparison.
Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-isotropy} shows it is not. The curve plotted is the median of a
cluster bootstrap resampling (200 times) whole take-off-site-and-day groups, 
with a 10--90\% band. Across all evaluated lags, the transport analysis shows that the paraglider 
band remains bounded between \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMin\ and \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMax, 
maintaining an approximately constant offset from unity without ever intersecting the unity line. 
The hang gliders oscillate mildly instead: unlike the
paragliders the ratio is not steady, swinging between values below unity and above it,
from \StatPrelimHangIsoRatioMin\ to \StatPrelimHangIsoRatioMax.

The consequence is operative. The process cannot be treated as isotropic, so the two
marginals $P(E(t)=x\mid\mathbf{r}_0,t_0)$ and $P(N(t)=x\mid\mathbf{r}_0,t_0)$ are analysed \emph{separately} rather than merged
into a single one-dimensional sample and read as a stand-in for the 2-D propagator. Both
directions are studied on their own, alongside the modulus
$|\mathbf{r}(t)|=\sqrt{E(t)^2+N(t)^2}$.

Two decisions organise the chain.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The order.} Every check on the scalar record as logged (times, geodetic
        coordinates, altitude) comes first (stages~1--4), and the vector geometry is
        built only from what survives (stages~5--7). Converting coordinates does not
        clean a record: it recomputes each fix in a new frame, corrupt or not. Running
        it before the cleaning stages would therefore buy nothing, and would cost
        rework whenever trimming moved the fix the frame is anchored to, or filtering
        dropped the record outright.
  \item \textbf{The level.} Each stage acts per fix, per flight or per segment, and
        every parameter it adapts is read off the record it is acting on rather than off
        the archive around it. No flight's cleaning therefore depends on
        which other flights happen to be in the dataset. What is shared across flights
        is only the thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}, fixed a priori.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the chain retains.}
Run over the whole archive, the cascade retains
\num{\StatPipeParaKept} of \num{\StatPipeParaAttempted} paraglider flights
(\StatPipeParaKeptPct\,\%) and \num{\StatPipeHangKept} of \num{\StatPipeHangAttempted}

rest went. The numbers are a \emph{cascade}, not a set of marginal effects: a flight
counted against one criterion was still standing when that criterion tested it, so the
table reads in pipeline order and the counts do not add up to what each cut would remove
on its own (those are the \texttt{\textbackslash StatScan*} numbers quoted in
Secs.~\ref{sec:flightfilter} and~\ref{sec:uniform}, measured on the raw population).

Inside the retained flights the cleaning is light, which is what the design asked for:
it deletes \StatPipeParaRemovedPct\,\% of paraglider fixes and
\StatPipeHangRemovedPct\,\% of hang-glider ones, and invalidates an altitude on a further
\StatPipeParaAltInvalidPct\,\% and \StatPipeHangAltInvalidPct\,\%. \rev{The local outlier test
flags far more than is deleted, and by design: it never gates, so
\StatPipeParaFlaggedKeptPct\,\% and \StatPipeHangFlaggedKeptPct\,\% of fixes are flagged
and \emph{kept}. The flag is a per-fix record and a per-flight anomaly count, and what
condemns a fix is the impossibility gate (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}).}
Resampling reconstructs \StatPipeParaInterpPct\,\% and \StatPipeHangInterpPct\,\% of grid
points. A split is common but not the rule: \StatPipeParaSplitPct\,\% of retained
paraglider flights and \StatPipeHangSplitPct\,\% of hang-glider ones carry at least one,
the difference following the cadences of Fig.~\ref{fig:sampling} as
Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform} anticipated.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{The analysis ensemble, defined.}
\label{par:ensemble}
Everything downstream reads one object, and this is it. The \emph{analysis ensemble} is
the set of trajectories written to \path{fixes.parquet}: one row per grid point of every
\emph{retained} segment of every \emph{retained} flight, carrying the elapsed time $t$ in
the parent clock and the local Cartesian coordinates $(E,N,z)$ with their first two
derivatives. A flight is retained when it passes stages 1--4 of
Figure~\ref{fig:pipelinemap} and at least one of its segments survives stage 6; a segment
is retained when it is long enough and dense enough on its own uniform grid. That leaves
\num{\StatPipeParaKept} paraglider flights of \num{\StatPipeParaAttempted}
(\SI{\StatPipeParaKeptPct}{\percent}) in \num{\StatMsdParaSegments} segments, and
\num{\StatPipeHangKept} hang-glider flights of \num{\StatPipeHangAttempted}
(\SI{\StatPipeHangKeptPct}{\percent}) in \num{\StatMsdHangSegments}. No consumer re-applies
a retention rule: a flight or segment that should not be analysed has no rows to read.
Every segment keeps its parent's origin and clock, so a position is always measured from
that flight's take-off and a time is always elapsed since it, whether or not the record is
continuous up to that point. Section~\ref{sec:prelim} characterises this ensemble,
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} analyses it, and \path{docs/guide/data-on-disk.md} states which
columns each estimator reads and what a segment boundary means to it.
\end{revblock}
