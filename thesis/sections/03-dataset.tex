\section{The IGC tracklog format}
\label{sec:igcformat}

A flight track is stored as an \emph{IGC file}, the plain-text format defined by the
FAI/IGC Technical Specification for IGC-approved GNSS flight recorders~\cite{fai_igc_spec}.
Its layout is fixed by that standard, so a fix can be decoded from fixed character
positions; every downloaded track is checked at acquisition to be
IGC content --- an \texttt{A} record followed by at least one \texttt{B} record
(Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}).

Each line is a \emph{record} whose type is set by its first character -- a code assigned by
the standard, not chosen by the logger. The records relevant here are:
\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{A}] the flight-recorder/manufacturer identifier (first line of the file);
  \item[\texttt{H}] header records (flight date, pilot, glider, geodetic datum, \dots);
  \item[\texttt{I}] the declaration of the optional extra fields appended to every fix;
  \item[\texttt{B}] the GPS fixes -- the body of the file.
\end{description}
A \texttt{B} record stores its fields at fixed character positions. Decoded against the
standard, the example
\begin{center}
\texttt{B\,094329\,4933044N\,00005193E\,A\,00095\,00149}
\end{center}
reads as: UTC time \texttt{09:43:29}; latitude $49^\circ33.044'$\,N and longitude
$000^\circ05.193'$\,E on the WGS84 datum; a fix-validity flag; and \emph{two} altitudes in
metres -- the barometric (pressure) altitude (\SI{95}{\metre}) followed by the GNSS
altitude (\SI{149}{\metre}).

The validity flag takes two values and concerns the \emph{GNSS} solution only.
\texttt{A} marks a fix whose GNSS altitude is usable; \texttt{V} marks a GNSS drop-out, or a
fix for which the receiver could not resolve altitude, in which case the standard requires
the GNSS-altitude field to be written as zero. A \texttt{V} flag therefore does not make the
fix two-dimensional: the latitude and longitude stay usable and, on a logger with a working
pressure sensor, so does the barometric altitude, which is the channel this analysis adopts
(Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}). What a \texttt{V} flag signals lost is the GNSS altitude, not
the horizontal position.

Decoding a \texttt{B} record from fixed character positions can succeed numerically while
the result is not a valid encoding at all -- a corrupted byte can still parse as a digit.
The parser therefore checks, at decode time and independently of any flight-dynamics
judgement, that the record is actually a legal encoding -- a valid time, valid
latitude/longitude fields, a valid hemisphere letter -- dropping it otherwise, on the same
footing as a record with the wrong length or a non-numeric field \impldetails{impl:igcformat}.
This is a check of \emph{format} validity, distinct from
the \emph{plausibility} checks of fix-level cleaning (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}, e.g.\ the
inter-fix speed bound), which target fixes that decode validly but would be physically
implausible given the rest of the trajectory.

The two altitude channels differ in reference and in noise; the analysis adopts the
\emph{barometric} one for the vertical dynamics, for reasons quantified in
Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}---a choice that matters because the vertical velocity derived
from it feeds the phase segmentation. On older
loggers one channel may be absent (recorded as zero).

The sequence of \texttt{B} records is thus a time-stamped three-dimensional trajectory. The
sampling period is typically a few seconds, is not guaranteed to be uniform, and varies
across recording devices -- a point the analysis must handle explicitly.

\section{Glider categories}
\label{sec:glider}

Soaring kinematics depend strongly on the aircraft, so the glider must be identifiable for
any stratified analysis. Three broad categories exist, in increasing order of performance:
\textbf{paragliders} (flexible canopy; slowest, glide ratio $\sim\!8$--$11$, tight turns),
\textbf{hang gliders} (delta wing, spanning flexible framed wings and rigid wings;
faster, glide ratio $\sim\!10$--$15$), and
\textbf{sailplanes} (rigid aircraft; fastest, glide ratio $\gtrsim\!25$). They are, in
effect, different aircraft---differing several-fold in cruise speed, glide ratio and turn
radius---which is why the reference study~\cite{vilpellet2026} analyses each separately
rather than pooling them.

In practice the category is set by the \emph{data source}, since the public databases are
organised by discipline. Because the FFVL competition is split by discipline
(Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}), the present
dataset spans \emph{both} glider types: the glider-type stratum has two levels, and the
cross-type comparison of the reference study~\cite{vilpellet2026} is therefore already
possible between paragliders and hang gliders. The third category, \emph{sailplanes}, is
collected by different portals and is not yet in hand (Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}); until
it is added the cross-type analysis is limited to the two FFVL disciplines.

Within each discipline the flights can be further stratified by \emph{performance class},
recorded in \texttt{aile\_class}; \rev{the two disciplines use different class systems,
collected in Table~\ref{tab:aileclass}. Paragliders follow the EN/LTF certification
ladder, which does track performance (higher classes fly faster and flatter) and is
therefore the natural within-source stratification variable. The hang-glider scheme is
\emph{not} a ladder: the FAI classes are defined by the method of control, so they
stratify the fleet by design rather than by performance, and any ordering imposed on them
is an assumption to be checked rather than a property of the scheme.}

\begin{revblock}
The two ladders are not documented on equal terms, and it is worth saying why, since it
governs how far each entry of Table~\ref{tab:aileclass} can be trusted. The FAI classes
come from the Sporting Code, which the FAI publishes for free: anyone can download the
current edition and check a definition against it. The EN classes come from a
\emph{European standard}, which is a different kind of document. Standards are drafted by
the European Committee for Standardization (CEN) and then published and \emph{sold} by the
national standards bodies---AFNOR in France, DIN in Germany, BSI in the United
Kingdom---as copyrighted documents, priced per copy at a few hundred euros. There is no
public version: unlike a law or a sporting code, the authoritative text of EN~926-2 cannot
be consulted without buying it, and it cannot be quoted here.

The EN entries of Table~\ref{tab:aileclass} are therefore condensed from Table~1 of the
\emph{final draft} of the standard, \mbox{FprEN~926-2:2012}, the version submitted to
formal vote, which the CEN working group circulated
publicly~\cite{fpren926_2_2012}. That draft is the closest thing to the source text that
is openly available, and the class descriptions in it are unlikely to have moved in the
published \mbox{EN~926-2:2013}, though that has not been checked against the published text. Nothing in this thesis depends on the wording:
the class field is used as a stratification label, and what the analysis needs from it is
that the labels are distinct and ordered, not what each one certifies.
\end{revblock}

Two caveats bear on any use of \texttt{aile\_class}. First, the field must be normalised
before it can serve as a stratum: normalisation here means merging the label variants
of the same class onto one canonical value per discipline and, where possible, recovering a
blank or placeholder entry from the wing model name. Second, the class certifies the wing,
not the pilot. The normalisation is done at the pre-processing stage
(Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter}) \impldetails{impl:glider}; the second point no processing can
remove, and it bounds what a class-stratified result can be taken to mean.

\section{Data quality and caveats}
\label{sec:dataquality}

A few limitations are already known and will shape the analysis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flights without a track.} About \SI{8}{\percent} of paraglider entries and
        about \SI{27}{\percent} of hang-glider entries have no IGC file (older or manually
        declared flights); they carry metadata only. These are the flights outside the
        \enquote{With GPS} column of Tables~\ref{tab:seasons-para}
        and~\ref{tab:seasons-hang}.
  \item \textbf{A small residue of unrecoverable files.} A few flights advertise a track
        whose download fails or returns something that is not valid IGC content
        (broken or placeholder links); these are recorded as failures and excluded.
  \item \textbf{Heterogeneous logging.} Sampling rate, availability of the two altitude
        channels, and per-fix positioning noise depend
        on the device; trajectories must be resampled and quality-filtered before any
        ensemble statistic is computed.
  \item \textbf{Altitude channel.} A sizeable minority of loggers record no
        pressure altitude -- almost a third of paraglider and roughly a sixth of hang-glider
        flights -- which fall back to the GNSS altitude, with the channel actually used
        recorded per flight (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}).
  \item \textbf{Glider metadata.} Flights are paragliders or hang gliders, tagged by source
        (Section~\ref{sec:glider}); the class field \texttt{aile\_class} must be normalised
        before it is used to stratify.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete dates.} Some historical entries have placeholder dates,
        \rev{written as the sentinel value \texttt{0000-00-00} at acquisition. The sentinel
        makes them recognizable, and such flights are discarded rather than carried
        along: a flight whose date is unknown cannot be assigned to a season, and no
        downstream step needs it. The cut is negligible, and measured rather than assumed
        to be: \num{\StatCatParaPlaceholderDateCount} of the
        \num{\StatCatParaFlights} paraglider entries carry the sentinel
        (\StatCatParaPlaceholderDatePct\,\%) and
        \num{\StatCatHangPlaceholderDateCount} of the \num{\StatCatHangFlights}
        hang-glider ones. The per-season breakdown is checked too, and not only the
        total, because a cut that is negligible archive-wide could still empty a single
        early season, which is where the smallest flight counts sit: the worst-affected
        season loses \StatCatParaPlaceholderWorstPct\,\% of its entries
        (\StatCatParaPlaceholderWorstSeason), so no season is materially thinned.}
\end{itemize}
These caveats motivate the pre-processing described in the remainder of this chapter.

\section{Trajectory pre-processing}
\label{sec:preproc}
Each IGC file is parsed into a sequence of fixes $(t_i,\varphi_i,\lambda_i,h_i)$ -- UTC
time, geodetic latitude and longitude, and altitude -- with both altitude channels
retained \impldetails{impl:preproc}. The pipeline then proceeds in a fixed order:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item choose the altitude channel (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel});
  \item clean individual fixes (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel});
  \item trim the ground phases (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming});
  \item filter out unfit whole flights (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter});
  \item convert to a local Cartesian frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu});
  \item enforce a uniform sampling interval within each flight (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform});
  \item smooth and differentiate the trajectory (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}).
\end{enumerate}
The output is a tidy, analysis-ready representation in a growing
\texttt{soaring.analysis} sub-package\rev{, written to three tables on the data disk:
one row per fix, one per segment, one per flight \emph{attempted}. What each file holds
and how the disk is organised is set out in Sec.~\ref{impl:onDisk}}.

Steps~(i)--(iv) act on the \emph{raw geographic} coordinates, since the only geometric
quantities they need (inter-fix speed and spatial extent) \rev{are scalar distances, which
the great-circle (haversine) formula of Eq.~\eqref{eq:haversine} gives directly from
latitude and longitude}, with no coordinate conversion. The conversion~(v) to the
metric East--North--Up frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}) is then done on the cleaned, trimmed track,
with the origin at the first fix that survives the trimming, so the tangent-plane
frame is built from good data.

Steps~(vi)--(vii) come last for a different
reason: unlike the scalar checks above, they produce \emph{vector} quantities---the
components of the 3D position, velocity and acceleration---which require Cartesian axes to
be expressed in, and
therefore cannot run before the frame of step~(v) exists.

\begin{revblock}
Every step below is governed by a handful of numerical thresholds. They are collected once,
here, in Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}, so that the value in force at any point of the
chapter can be read off a single page instead of being hunted through the text; each
subsection then argues for its own entries rather than restating them. All of them are
\emph{working values}: fixed a priori, on physical scales rather than on the transport
observables, and audited a posteriori on the fraction of data each rule removes
(Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}). The table is typeset from the configuration file itself, so a
value changed there changes here.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Choice of altitude channel}
\label{sec:altchannel}
The analysis adopts the \emph{barometric} altitude rather than the GNSS altitude for
the vertical dynamics. The
reason is one of signal quality in the frequency band that matters. \rev{The IGC format
writes both altitude channels as whole metres, so the resolution of either is fixed at
\SI{1}{\metre} by the file itself; what distinguishes the barometric channel is that} it
carries very little high-frequency noise.
Its errors are slow: a constant offset from the ICAO reference, tens of metres up to order
\SI{100}{\metre} (visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:altnoise}a as the gap between the two
channels; most of that gap is the barometric offset, though the GNSS altitude contributes a
smaller bias of its own), and a drift that already reaches tens of metres within the
half-hour window of Fig.~\ref{fig:altnoise}b, and can grow further over a multi-hour
flight. \rev{Both have the same origin: the barometric altitude is a pressure reading
converted through the ICAO standard atmosphere, so it is offset by the difference between
the day's actual sea-level pressure and the ICAO reference of \SI{1013.25}{\hecto\pascal},
and it drifts as that pressure changes over the hours of the flight and as the real
temperature profile departs from the standard one. Drift of the sensor itself is a third
and much smaller contribution.}

Slow errors are annihilated by the
differencing that every dynamical quantity involves (a vertical velocity, a displacement
increment, \rev{the autocorrelation of a velocity}). The GNSS altitude, by contrast, is \rev{referenced to a fixed geometric
surface (the WGS84 ellipsoid), so it carries no atmospheric offset, but it is vertically
noisier: the satellite geometry resolves the vertical coordinate less well than the
horizontal one\footnote{\rev{The asymmetry is geometric. A receiver sees satellites only
above the horizon, so the ranging directions bracket it in the horizontal plane but all
arrive from the same side vertically; the vertical coordinate is correspondingly the
worst-determined one. This is what the \emph{vertical dilution of precision} measures.}}
(the \emph{vertical dilution of precision}): the GPS performance standard commits to
\SI{8}{\metre} horizontal against \SI{13}{\metre} vertical error at \SI{95}{\percent}
globally, and \SI{15}{\metre} against \SI{33}{\metre} at the worst
site~\cite{gps_sps_ps_2020},} and \rev{\emph{multipath}---the direct signal arriving
together with replicas delayed by reflection off terrain or the pilot's own
equipment---biases the range on timescales of minutes rather than adding independent noise
from fix to fix~\cite{garciacrespillo2024}}.

This is why free-flight variometers derive the
climb rate from the pressure sensor rather than from GNSS.

\paragraph{Empirical confirmation.}
The data confirm this picture, with one qualification. Figure~%
\ref{fig:altnoise} compares the two channels over a subsample sized for a robust spectral
estimate (Appendix~\ref{app:psd}): \rev{\num{3000} flights per discipline, a size chosen on
the stability of the median spectrum itself and justified in Sec.~\ref{impl:altchannel}}
\impldetails{impl:altchannel}. At low frequency the two
agree, tracking the real climbs and glides. The high-frequency behaviour is where they differ.
For the \emph{typical} flight the two are comparable: both approach
the same flat high-frequency plateau---the level at which the spectra in panel~c
flatten towards the Nyquist frequency---set by the metre resolution to which the IGC format
rounds \emph{both} channels, so the median spectra (panel~c, solid lines) nearly coincide.

What separates the channels is the \emph{spread} across flights, the shaded bands. The barometric band stays
tight (that channel is uniformly clean), whereas the GNSS band fans out by one to two
orders of magnitude towards the Nyquist frequency: a substantial minority of flights carry
large GNSS high-frequency noise, \rev{from receiver tracking noise amplified by a poor
vertical dilution of precision---not from multipath, whose error is correlated over
minutes and so raises the low-frequency end of the spectrum rather than the
floor---while} most do not. Since the vertical velocity is a finite difference, which amplifies exactly that
band (differencing scales a spectral component of frequency $f$ by
$2\sin(\pi f\,\Delta t)/\Delta t$, an amplification that grows with $f$: it acts as a
high-pass filter), and since one cannot know in advance which flights have poor GNSS reception, the
uniformly clean barometric channel is the safe choice. This
\emph{departs from the reference study}~\cite{vilpellet2026}, whose pipeline derives the
vertical coordinate and velocity from the GNSS altitude; the divergence is deliberate, and one
of the points re-examined here.

Several practical points follow.

\paragraph{One channel per flight.}
A trajectory uses a single altitude channel end-to-end;
the barometric and GNSS values are never spliced within a flight, since a
mid-flight switch between two channels offset by tens of metres would inject a
spurious jump.

\paragraph{Source recorded.}
Which channel a flight uses is recorded as a per-flight
attribute of the processed dataset, so the two source groups remain separable in
every downstream analysis; the raw IGC files are never modified
\impldetails{impl:altchannel}.

\paragraph{Missing fixes.}
A flight is used with its barometric channel when it has one, and
the few individual fixes with a missing barometric value are dropped rather than
back-filled from GNSS. \rev{Only
\StatScanParaBaroMissFlightsPct\,\% of barometric paraglider flights
(\StatScanHangBaroMissFlightsPct\,\% of hang gliders) miss any barometric value at
all, with a median of \StatScanParaBaroMissMedianFixes\
(\StatScanHangBaroMissMedianFixes) missing fixes among them---against $\sim\!10^4$
fixes in a typical track---and a worst case of \num{\StatScanParaBaroMissMaxFixes}
(\num{\StatScanHangBaroMissMaxFixes}), bounded by the presence threshold itself: a flight
whose channel covers less than \SI{95}{\percent} of its fixes is not treated as barometric
at all (Sec.~\ref{impl:altchannel}).} Whether the dropped fixes
are scattered or consecutive, the hole they leave is handled downstream like any
other gap (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).

\paragraph{\flow{Absent or stuck sensor: the GNSS fallback.}}
A logger with \emph{no} pressure sensor writes the whole
barometric channel as zero (the presence is essentially all-or-nothing, not a
scattering of gaps)\rev{, so a flight counts as barometric only when the channel covers
at least \SI{95}{\percent} of its fixes---high on purpose, since a partially filled
channel is worse for the vertical analysis than a uniformly sourced GNSS one
(Sec.~\ref{impl:altchannel})}; such a flight falls back to the GNSS altitude, unfiltered --
a non-negligible minority of flights (Figure~\ref{fig:altnoise}d). \rev{The mere presence
of a non-zero barometric channel} is not enough: a stuck sensor writes a constant non-zero
value and would feed the
segmentation a vertical velocity of identically zero, so a barometric channel
whose total range over the flight stays below \SI{\PreprocBaroMinRangeM}{\metre} is treated as
absent, and the flight falls back to GNSS.
\rev{The fallback rate is measured on the full archive:
\StatScanParaNoBaroPct\,\% of the \num{\StatScanParaTracks} paraglider and
\StatScanHangNoBaroPct\,\% of the \num{\StatScanHangTracks} hang-glider flights scanned
carry no barometric channel \impldetails{impl:altchannel}.}

\begin{revblock}
The liveness test as written catches only a sensor that is dead for the \emph{whole}
recording, and it should be read as that and no more. The statistic is a single
whole-flight $\max-\min$, so a barometer that records normally for the first ten minutes
and then freezes keeps a range of hundreds of metres, is judged alive, is adopted, and
delivers a vertical velocity of identically zero over most of the flight---which is
precisely the outcome the test is here to prevent. Nothing downstream closes the gap: the
frozen-lock rule of Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel} needs the \emph{position} to be frozen, and a
cruising wing never trips it; the flight-level altitude-activity cut consumes the same
extremum difference and inherits the same blindness. A test that did close it would be a
\emph{local} one---a moving-window range, or a longest-constant-run bound---and that is a
new threshold with its own failure mode against a wing genuinely holding altitude, so it
is not adopted here on the strength of an argument alone. What the present rule guarantees
is stated exactly: a channel that is constant throughout is refused, and one that dies
partway is not.
\end{revblock}

\paragraph{Mixed altitude sources.}
The mix is acceptable, for two reasons. \rev{The \emph{horizontal}, two-dimensional
transport analysis---the main subject of this work, of which the MSD and its scaling are one
observable among several---does not use the altitude at
all}, so every flight is usable there regardless of channel. Altitude enters only the vertical
velocity, hence the segmentation and the phase-resolved observables; there the two source
groups are kept distinct through the per-flight altitude-source record introduced
above. \rev{The compatibility check therefore has to be run on the segmentation itself
rather than on the altitude statistics: both groups are segmented with the same rules and
compared, per discipline, on the fraction of airborne time each phase accounts for. The two
estimates of each fraction are compatible when they agree within their uncertainties; a
disagreement in any phase is the signal that the noisier channel is biasing the phase
assignment. One number per phase per group is a deliberately simple check, and it is the
right one, because the fraction of time per phase is exactly what the vertical channel
feeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}).} If the noisier GNSS-only flights were to bias a
vertical statistic, that analysis is restricted to the barometric flights -- still the large
majority -- while the horizontal analyses keep the full sample. Forcing GNSS on \emph{all}
flights, to avoid the mix, would be the wrong trade: it would needlessly degrade the vertical
signal of the barometric majority to match the worst loggers.

\subsection{Fix-level cleaning}
\label{sec:fixlevel}
\paragraph{Geometry on the raw coordinates.}
The cleaning runs on the raw geodetic coordinates $(\varphi_i,\lambda_i)$, before
the Cartesian conversion (\rev{Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}}), so distances between fixes cannot be
Euclidean; they are
great-circle (haversine) distances\rev{~\cite{sklearn_haversine}} on a
sphere of mean radius $R_\oplus=\SI{6371}{\kilo\metre}$. For two consecutive fixes,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:haversine}
\Delta s_i = 2R_\oplus\arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\!\frac{\varphi_{i+1}-\varphi_i}{2}
  + \cos\varphi_i\cos\varphi_{i+1}\,\sin^2\!\frac{\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_i}{2}}\,,
\end{equation}
from which the horizontal speed between the fixes is $v_{xy,i}=\Delta s_i/(t_{i+1}-t_i)$ and,
from the barometric altitude, the vertical speed is $v_{z,i}=(h_{i+1}-h_i)/(t_{i+1}-t_i)$.
The same distance also yields two whole-flight quantities used below: the \emph{path
length} $L=\sum_i\Delta s_i$, the total distance flown along the track, and the
\emph{extent} $D=\max_i\Delta s(\mathrm{fix}_0,\mathrm{fix}_i)$, the farthest any fix lies
from the first. The spherical approximation is ample here. Replacing the WGS84
ellipsoid by a sphere perturbs an inter-fix distance by at most a fraction of the order of
the ellipsoid's flattening, $\lesssim\SI{0.3}{\percent}$\rev{\footnote{An arc length on a
curved surface is proportional to the radius of curvature used to compute it, so the
relative error made by using the mean radius $R_\oplus$ in place of the ellipsoid's local
radius is of the order by which the two differ. On WGS84 the radii of curvature range from
the polar $b^2/a=\SI{6335.4}{\kilo\metre}$ to the equatorial $a^2/b=\SI{6399.6}{\kilo\metre}$,
a spread of the order of the flattening $f=(a-b)/a=1/298.257\approx3.4\times10^{-3}$, hence
the \SI{0.3}{\percent} quoted here~\cite{nga_wgs84}.}}---about \SI{3}{\centi\metre} on a
\SI{10}{\metre} step---whereas the horizontal position error of consumer GNSS receivers is
of the order of metres \rev{(the GPS performance standard commits to \SI{8}{\metre} at
\SI{95}{\percent} globally~\cite{gps_sps_ps_2020})}; the correction is therefore far below the noise on the very same
quantity. Working on the raw coordinates also avoids converting them before the trajectory
has been cleaned.

\paragraph{Design principle.}
Corrupt or physically impossible fixes must be removed without discarding the flight around
them. At the same time, every removed or altered fix modifies the trajectory whose
statistics are about to be measured, and an over-aggressive cleaning biases the result as
much as dirty data would. The cleaning therefore assumes as little as possible about the
motion: only smoothness at the sampling scale, never a model of the transport itself.

The two possible errors have different costs. A spurious fix that survives the tests below
lies \rev{at worst tens of metres from the true track---at most the paraglider speed bound of
Table~\ref{tab:cleaning} times the native step, $\approx\SI{45}{\metre}$ at
\SI{1}{\hertz}---and typically far closer, within the absolute floor $\varepsilon_{\min}$ of
the outlier test defined below in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hampel}: a
displacement the smoothing absorbs.} A genuine fix removed from a slow, horizontally
localized phase (a search, a climb) erases a real trapping event, and nothing downstream
can restore it. The cleaning therefore defaults to keeping the data: a fix is removed only
on positive evidence of a defect, and no detector deletes a fix that still carries a usable
horizontal position \rev{(at most, a corrupt \emph{altitude} channel is marked missing and
restored at resampling, Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}; a corrupt horizontal position is never
reconstructed---the fix carrying it is deleted outright)}.

\paragraph{Three classes of defect.}
Past the format-validity check made at decode time (Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}), which
rejects any record that is not a legal encoding and is already implemented and
unit-tested, a surviving fix can still be wrong in one
of three ways, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cleaning}.

A fix \emph{off the trend} -- a GPS position spike, or a jump out and back within one
step -- is found by a robust\rev{\footnote{\emph{Robust} has its technical sense here: the
median and the median absolute deviation have a breakdown point of \SI{50}{\percent}, so up
to half the fixes in the window may be arbitrarily wrong without displacing the estimate. A
mean and a standard deviation have breakdown point zero---a single spike drags both, inflating
the scale by just enough to hide itself.}} local-outlier test, the \emph{Hampel
identifier}~\cite{hampel1974,daviesgather1993}.
Let the window of fix $k$ be the set of
fixes within $\pm w$ of $t_k$, and let
$\tilde\varphi_k=\operatorname{med}\{\varphi_j:|t_j-t_k|\le w\}$ and
$\tilde\lambda_k=\operatorname{med}\{\lambda_j:|t_j-t_k|\le w\}$ be the component-wise
window medians. The residual of fix $k$ is its great-circle distance from that median
point,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hampelresid}
  r_k=\Delta s\big((\varphi_k,\lambda_k),(\tilde\varphi_k,\tilde\lambda_k)\big),
\end{equation}
with $\Delta s$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:haversine}, and the robust local scale of the residuals
is $\operatorname{MAD}_k=\operatorname{med}\{r_j:|t_j-t_k|\le w\}$.
The fix is flagged when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hampel}
  r_k > \max\!\big(k\,\sigma_k,\ \varepsilon_{\min}\big),
  \qquad \sigma_k = 1.4826\operatorname{MAD}_k,
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma_k$ is the normalized MAD, the median absolute deviation rescaled to estimate
a Gaussian standard deviation~\cite{rousseeuw1993}: the flag requires a departure of more
than $k$ robust standard deviations \emph{and} more than an absolute floor
$\varepsilon_{\min}$ of a few times the GPS noise (working values in
\rev{Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}}; step-by-step definition and
rationale in Sec.~\ref{impl:fixlevel}). \rev{A flag does not delete. What deletes is the
absolute $v_{xy}$ bound: a fix is removed when it is unreachable from the last accepted
fix at that bound \emph{and} the removal of the block it belongs to lets the track rejoin
within it. The identifier's role is to flag and to attribute---to say which fix of a
neighbourhood departs from it---and to supply the per-flight anomaly count; the reason it
cannot also be required is argued below.}

A genuine but under-sampled tight turn is kept either way, and it is worth seeing why,
because it is what a naive speed-only rule would get wrong and what the identifier was
originally there to protect. The Hampel test measures a departure from the local median
track, and a tight thermalling circle produces exactly such a departure: at a
\SI{10}{\second} cadence a circle of a few tens of metres in diameter puts consecutive
fixes on opposite sides of their own window median, and the residual clears
$\varepsilon_{\min}$ on geometry alone. Those fixes are the trapping events the transport
analysis exists to measure. \rev{What separates them from a spike is not the flag but the
speed: a circling wing flies at an ordinary one, whereas a position spike of the same size
implies an inter-fix speed no aircraft in this dataset can sustain. The protection is
therefore already in the bound that does the deleting, which is where it came from.}

\begin{revblock}
The rule was originally a conjunction---flagged \emph{and} impossible---and running it
over the archive exposed a limit in the \emph{flag} that is the reason it no longer is.
The Hampel identifier has a breakdown point of
\SI{50}{\percent}: up to half a window may be arbitrarily wrong without displacing the
median, which is what makes it robust---and past that half the median moves onto the
corruption instead. The archive contains the case. Some loggers write the null position
$(0,0)$ for scattered runs of a few seconds each; where several such runs fall inside one
\SI{\PreprocHampelWindowS}{\second} window they exceed half of it, the median follows
them, the corrupt fixes score a residual of \emph{zero} and go unflagged, and it is the
good fixes around them that are flagged instead. Requiring the flag there does not make
the rule conservative, it makes it blind: on one such flight the identifier caught 17 of
99 null fixes, and every survivor left a step of some \SI{5000}{\kilo\metre} in the
record.

The rule adopted is therefore the \emph{impossibility gate on its own}. A fix is deleted
when it is unreachable from the last accepted fix at the $v_{xy}$ bound \emph{and} the
removal of the block it belongs to lets the track rejoin within that bound. Both halves of
the argument above survive the change. A genuine tight circle is still never a candidate,
because it flies at an ordinary speed---so the protection the flag was there to give is
given by the bound itself, which is where it came from in the first place. And the
attribution the flag supplied---which endpoint of an impossible step is the wrong
one---is supplied instead by the rejoining test, which names the block whose removal
restores the trend. What the flag stops being is a \emph{necessary} condition for
deletion; what it remains is the per-fix record and the per-flight anomaly count. The
change bites only where the identifier is outside its own stated range of validity, and
that is precisely where the previous rule failed.
\end{revblock}

A fix \emph{on the trend} agrees with its neighbours yet is still wrong as a record -- a
duplicated or backward timestamp, or a run of frozen positions from a lost GNSS lock; its
residual is near zero, so it escapes the outlier test and needs a structural rule instead.

A value \emph{out of range} concerns the vertical channel: an impossible altitude or
climb rate is caught by an absolute bound that needs no context.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{An unreturned vertical step.}
The $|v_z|$ rule recognises two shapes and there is a third. An \emph{out-and-back} spike
is censored; a coherent \emph{run} is a sustained manoeuvre and is counted and left
(Sec.~\ref{impl:fixlevel}). A single super-threshold step that is never undone---a
barometric re-reference, or one corrupt record---is neither. It was therefore censored by
nothing \emph{and counted by nothing}, and since it is a discontinuity rather than an
outlier the smoothing stage differentiated it: the written table carried vertical
velocities of up to \SI{5117}{\metre\per\second}. It is not a curiosity. On a sample of
the archive, \SI{8.3}{\percent} of paraglider flights and \SI{6.2}{\percent} of
hang-glider flights carry at least one.

What is done here is the part that is not a judgement: the shape is detected and counted,
per flight, as \path{n_alt_level_shift}, so that it is visible and auditable rather than
silently present. What to \emph{do} about it is left open, deliberately. The three
candidate treatments---split the flight at the step, on the same footing as an impossible
horizontal step; re-reference the altitude after it, restoring continuity; or invalidate a
window around it and let the resampling bridge it---each change a rule argued elsewhere in
this chapter, and none of them follows from the specification. The vertical channel feeds
the phase segmentation of Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} and not the transport measurement of
Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global}, which is horizontal, so the choice belongs with that analysis
rather than ahead of it.
\end{revblock}

\begin{revblock}
The vertical channel is treated differently from the horizontal one on purpose, and the
asymmetry is worth stating, because the obvious symmetric design---a Hampel test on $z$ too,
corroborated by the climb-rate bound---is the wrong one here. Three things differ.

First, an absolute bound is \emph{sufficient} on $z$ and insufficient on $xy$. The vertical
envelope is narrow and known: no wing in this dataset sustains
$|v_z|>\SI{\PreprocMaxVSpeedMps}{\metre\per\second}$, and no altitude outside
$[\PreprocMinAltM,\PreprocMaxAltM]\,\si{\metre}$ is sensor-plausible, so a spike in altitude
is almost always out of range on its own. Horizontally there is no such luck: a
\SI{50}{\metre} spike is an impossible \SI{50}{\metre\per\second} at a \SI{1}{\second}
cadence but an unremarkable \SI{5}{\metre\per\second} at \SI{10}{\second}, so the same defect
passes or fails the bound depending on the logger. The horizontal case needs a local,
scale-free test precisely because its absolute bound is not decisive; the vertical case does
not.

Second, a Hampel test on $z$ would flag the physics. The residual from a local median is
largest where the signal turns fastest, and on the altitude channel that means thermal
entries and exits---where $v_z$ swings by several \si{\metre\per\second} within seconds
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). Those transitions are what the phase segmentation is built on. A
horizontal circle, by contrast, can be told from a horizontal spike by its speed, so the
local test there has a corroborating witness that the vertical one lacks: the quantity that
would have to arbitrate a suspected altitude spike \emph{is} the climb rate, which is the
quantity under suspicion.

Third, the costs are not symmetric. A false vertical flag marks one altitude missing and it
is interpolated back at resampling; a false horizontal flag would delete a genuine sample of
the path. But an interpolation laid across a thermal entry erases the transition rather than
restoring it, so a cheap-looking vertical false positive is expensive exactly where it is
most likely. Keeping the vertical rule to a bound that only fires on the physically
impossible avoids that failure mode altogether.
\end{revblock}

The thresholds are set in two ways. \rev{The absolute bounds are placed on data: the
$v_{xy}$ and $|v_z|$ bounds sit where the per-fix distributions of
Fig.~\ref{fig:fixlevel} stop decaying, and the altitude window is a sensor-plausibility
band audited on the same distributions} \flow{(lower bound negative for the
reference-offset reason in the caption of Table~\ref{tab:cleaning})}\rev{. The remaining
parameters---those of the Hampel test and of the frozen-lock rule---are working values
fixed a priori. All of them, with their values, are in
Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}}; their reasonableness
is then checked a posteriori on the fraction of fixes each rule removes (paragraph
\emph{Validating the cleaning} below).

(Status: the routine
that walks a trajectory applying the three detectors is still to be built.)

\paragraph{Removal policy.}
A speed bound alone cannot attribute a defect: the speed belongs to the segment
\emph{between} two fixes and does not identify which endpoint is wrong. \rev{The
attribution comes from the bounded-removal test---the block whose removal restores the
trend is the corrupt one---so an isolated spike is removed without touching its
neighbours, and a short burst is removed as one block, the smallest that works. The
local-outlier residual, being a property of a single fix, says the same thing where it is
valid, and is recorded for that reason; it is not what decides.}

What happens to a defective fix depends on \emph{which} of its values is corrupt.
\rev{Two cases arise---a corrupt altitude, and a corrupt horizontal position or
timestamp---and they are handled asymmetrically. The asymmetry} follows from a single
criterion: keep every genuine horizontal sample, and
never hide a hole in the horizontal record. In \rev{either} case the corrupt value itself is
simply discarded---nothing is estimated in its place at this stage; whatever needs
reconstructing is reconstructed once, at the resampling step (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).

If the corrupt value is the \emph{altitude}, the fix is kept and only its altitude is
marked missing. \rev{In practice the fix keeps its row in the trajectory table and its
altitude entry is set to a missing value, with a per-fix boolean recording that the value
was removed by the cleaning rather than never written by the logger; the two are
indistinguishable downstream otherwise, and only the first is a statement about data
quality.} The horizontal position and the timestamp are intact, so the fix still
samples the path---exactly the information the transport analysis measures---and
discarding it would throw that genuine sample away. The missing altitude is restored by
interpolation at resampling\rev{, where the filled point is flagged as interpolated in turn};
the barometric channel is smooth enough that this costs less
than the sensor noise (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}).

If the corrupt value is the \emph{horizontal position or the time}, the whole fix is
deleted. One could imagine the symmetric treatment---keep the fix and mark the horizontal
position missing---but that would be dangerous: the resampling step decides whether to bridge a
hole or to split the flight by looking at the \emph{time gap} between surviving fixes.
Ten consecutive kept-but-positionless fixes would look like ten samples of a continuously
recorded stretch; the gap rule would never fire, and the resampler would lay an invented
straight line across an interval that may hold a full thermalling circle. Deleting the
fixes leaves the hole visible, and the gap rule then does its job: a short hole is
bridged, a long one splits the flight (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). The altitude sample lost
with the deleted fix is the cheap part, for the same smoothness reason as above.

The IGC validity flag needs no rule of its own here: a \texttt{V} flag certifies a
degraded GNSS altitude (Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}), so on a barometric flight the fix is
untouched, and on a GNSS-fallback flight it reduces to the corrupt-altitude case above.

\paragraph{\rev{An impossible step is a boundary, never flown path.}}
\rev{The two actions above---delete the block, or split at the step---cover between them
what the scan can resolve. What must hold whatever it resolves is a weaker statement, and
it is the one the rest of the pipeline leans on: \emph{no step between two surviving
fixes may break the $v_{xy}$ bound}. Where a block is kept because nothing could rejoin
it, the step by which the track returns from that block is itself impossible, and left
alone it enters a segment as flown motion---in the archive, a jump of tens of kilometres
inside one second, carried thereafter into every average taken over that segment. Such a
step is a transition whose course is unknown, which is what a long gap is, so it is made
a segment boundary on the same footing (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). It is stated as a
property of the output rather than as one more case of the scan, so that it holds whatever
the scan enumerated. How often it acts is counted per flight, as \path{n_boundaried}: a
high count would locate the fault in the scan rather than in the data.}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{A corrupt block that opens the record.}
The blind spot is real and it matters more than its rarity suggests. A scan that carries
the last accepted fix as an anchor never questions its own first anchor; and the first fix
of the trimmed track becomes the origin of the local frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}), so a
corrupt one displaces every coordinate of the flight rather than one of them, and the
error does not average out with length of record. Five paraglider flights left the first
full-archive run sitting some \SI{4500}{\kilo\metre} from their own origin. Since the
ensemble MSD is an average of $|\mathbf{r}(t)|^2$ over flights, five records out of
\num{156017} raised it by seven orders of magnitude at the shortest lags.

No fix-level rule attributes it, and that absence is a decision rather than an omission.
An impossible step is a statement about a \emph{pair} of fixes and says nothing about
which of the two is the wrong one. Away from the ends of a record the bounded-removal test
settles the question by evidence---it asks which removal restores the trend---and at the
very first fix there is no trend behind the step to ask. Two rules were nevertheless
written, and both were wrong, in opposite directions. One fired on the first impossible
step, which accuses the earlier end always: applied to an ordinary position spike three
seconds into a record it deleted the good fixes and promoted the spike to origin, the same
failure with the sign reversed. The other fired on a discontinuity the scan itself
declares near the start, which sounds like evidence---a discontinuity survives only where
no bounded removal after the step restored the trend---but the split is placed at the
first fix of the block that could not be rejoined, and that block lies \emph{after} the
step whenever the corruption simply outlasts $w$. A \SI{50}{\second} corrupt run five
seconds in therefore deleted five good fixes, kept fifty bad ones, and left the origin
\SI{5039}{\kilo\metre} out.

So the discontinuity is left as a discontinuity, and the guarantee is taken one stage
later, where it can be stated without attributing blame to either end: a record out of
reach of its own first fix is refused whole (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter}). That bound
never depended on a fix-level rule. What such a rule would have bought is the recovery of
two flights in \num{156017}, against a failure mode now demonstrated twice---and the
invariant the whole of Chapter~\ref{ch:global} rests on,
$|\mathbf{r}(t)|\le v_{xy}^{\max}\,t$, holds either way, checked as such on the written
tables (Sec.~\ref{impl:verify}).
\end{revblock}

\paragraph{Frozen-position runs (GNSS lock loss).}
When the receiver loses its GNSS lock, many loggers repeat the last position for tens of
seconds while the clock keeps running. Such a run passes every test above: horizontal speed
zero, altitude in range, valid timestamps. If kept, it enters the analysis as a long
\emph{waiting time} and contaminates the tail of the waiting-time density $\psi(\tau)$,
the distribution of the durations of the slow, localized phases. If
the detector is too aggressive, however, it splits tight thermalling climbs, which are
also slow and \emph{horizontally} localized, and destroys real trapping events. The two
errors enter the same tail with the same weight: a kept freeze inserts a false long wait,
a cut climb removes a real one. They differ in what can be repaired downstream: a kept
run with a flat barometric trace is still flagged suspect and swept in the $\psi(\tau)$
sensitivity checks (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming}), while an erased trapping event is
unrecoverable. A run is therefore cut only when three independent signatures agree,
combined as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozenlock} below:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Spatial diameter.} A lost lock collapses the whole run into the
        logger's quantisation: the run's \emph{bounding diameter} -- the largest
        great-circle distance between any two of its fixes -- stays below $\delta_{xy}$,
        \rev{a tolerance of this rule set a few times above the GPS noise. It is written
        $\delta_{xy}$, and not $\varepsilon$, to keep it apart from the Hampel floor
        $\varepsilon_{\min}$: the two carry the same value at present but answer different
        questions---one is the smallest departure from a local median worth calling an
        outlier, the other the largest spread a run may show and still count as motionless
        (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}). The run is not a fixed-length window: a candidate
        run is the maximal stretch of consecutive fixes whose bounding diameter stays
        below $\delta_{xy}$, and the rule then asks how long that stretch lasts.} A
        circling climb instead traces a disc of diameter
        $2R\approx\SIrange{30}{100}{\metre}$, larger than $\delta_{xy}$ by at least a
        factor of two (quantified below), and clears the test on the diameter alone.
  \item \emph{An independent witness -- the barometric altitude.} A GNSS lock-loss freezes only
        the GNSS position; the pressure sensor is a \emph{separate} instrument. A wing that is
        really thermalling keeps gaining height on the barometric channel, whereas a true freeze
        shows a flat barometric trace. A horizontally motionless run whose barometric altitude
        is still changing at a plausible rate is a (tight) climb, and is \emph{kept}. One
        signature, and only one, may overrule this witness: coordinates repeated
        \emph{byte-identically}, defined in~(iii) \rev{(the structure of
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozenlock} makes this explicit: on a barometric flight the witness
        is a flat barometer \emph{or} a byte-identical repeat, so nothing else can cut a
        run whose barometer climbs)}.
  \item \emph{Recorder declarations.} Two signatures declare a defect directly, and they
        carry different weight. A \emph{byte-identical} repeat of the coordinates -- the
        latitude and longitude fields of consecutive \texttt{B} records written character
        for character the same -- is conclusive on its own: no moving receiver rewrites
        the very same bits, so the positions are invented whatever the barometer says. The
        \texttt{V} flag and the zeroed GNSS altitude are weaker: they certify a degraded
        \emph{GNSS altitude}, not a frozen horizontal fix \flow{(Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat})},
        so they decide
        only where no barometric witness exists, i.e.\ on a GNSS-fallback flight.
\end{enumerate}
A run is classified as a loss of signal only when it is spatially collapsed,
\emph{witnessed}, and sufficiently long,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:frozenlock}
\begin{gathered}
  \underbrace{\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{run})<\delta_{xy}}_{\text{(i) collapsed}}
  \;\wedge\;
  \underbrace{W}_{\text{witness (ii)/(iii)}}
  \;\wedge\;
  \underbrace{\Delta t_{\mathrm{run}}\geq\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}}_{\text{duration}},
  \\[4pt]
  W=
  \begin{cases}
    |\Delta z_{\mathrm{baro}}|<\delta_z \,\vee\, \text{byte-identical} &
      \text{barometric flight,}\\[2pt]
    \texttt{V}/\text{zero-alt} \,\vee\, \text{byte-identical} &
      \text{GNSS-fallback flight.}
  \end{cases}
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The run is then marked as a gap and the trajectory is \emph{split} there rather than
interpolated across, so that no motion is invented over an interval whose true path is
unknown; the segments keep their parent's origin and
clock (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).

The witness $W$ is hierarchical. On a barometric
flight \rev{none of the recorder's own declarations---the \texttt{V} flag, a zeroed GNSS
altitude---can} overrule a climbing barometer; the one
exception is the byte-identical repeat, which is conclusive on its own. Coordinates
repeated bit for bit are invented positions even when the height gain is real, so such a
run is cut even while the barometric altitude climbs.

Quantitatively, a real thermalling climb fails the cut on two independent instruments at
once. Geometrically, a circling wing turns at
$R=v^2/(g\tan\phi)$\footnote{\rev{The coordinated-turn relation: in a steady banked
turn the lift $L$ balances gravity vertically, $L\cos\phi=mg$, while its horizontal
component provides the centripetal force, $L\sin\phi=mv^2/R$; dividing the two gives
$\tan\phi=v^2/(gR)$, i.e.\ $R=v^2/(g\tan\phi)$.}}: the tightest paraglider
circles ($v\approx\SI{10}{\metre\per\second}$ at a bank of $\phi\approx35^\circ$\rev{,
ordinary values for tight thermalling}) give
$2R\approx\SI{30}{\metre}$, and hang gliders, which fly faster, turn wider
(\rev{$v\approx\SI{13}{\metre\per\second}$ at the same bank gives}
$2R\gtrsim\SI{50}{\metre}$). Against the working value of $\delta_{xy}$
(\rev{Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}}), test~(i)
therefore misses by a factor of at least $2$ for the tightest paraglider circles and
$\gtrsim 3$ for hang gliders, more for anything wider---ratios to be rescaled if
$\delta_{xy}$ moves. Barometrically, a climb of order \SI{1}{\metre\per\second} sustained
over $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}=\SI{\PreprocFrozenTauS}{\second}$ moves the barometer by
$\gtrsim\SI{60}{\metre}$ against the flatness tolerance $\delta_z$, which is set well
below that scale (\rev{Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}}).

The duration threshold itself is set on the motion's timescale: $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$ is
about two thermalling periods and three times the \SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second} gap cap of
Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}, so a run long enough to be cut spans at least two full circles of
any genuine climb, whose diameter test~(i) then sees in full. Cutting a genuine climb on a barometric
flight therefore requires two failures at once: the geometric margin of test~(i) and the
barometric witness of test~(ii). The only alternative is a byte-identical repeat, which no
\rev{genuinely} moving receiver writes.

\begin{revblock}
The margins make that conjunction unlikely rather than merely improbable in principle: the
diameter test is missed by a factor of two or more, and the barometric one by an order of
magnitude, so both would have to fail well outside their designed range at the same moment
and on the same run. On a barometric flight the rule is, in this sense, conservative by
construction---it errs towards keeping.

The duration condition is the one place where the rule deliberately abstains. A run that is
spatially collapsed and witnessed but lasts less than $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$ is
\emph{kept}, and that is the intended behaviour, not an oversight: at durations of a few
tens of seconds a collapsed run and a genuine slow, horizontally localized stretch---a
search, the core of a tight climb---are not distinguishable by these signatures, and
cutting on them would remove the very events the analysis measures. The cost of the
abstention is bounded by the threshold itself. A kept short freeze lengthens one waiting
time by at most $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$, which is small against the tail of $\psi(\tau)$
where the anomalous exponent is read, whereas a wrongly cut climb removes a waiting time
outright. Short freezes that do get through are still visible downstream: they leave a run
of interpolated-looking, near-identical positions, and the $\psi(\tau)$ sensitivity checks
sweep $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$ precisely to bound how much of the tail depends on this
choice.
\end{revblock}

\paragraph{Residual corner cases.}
The rule assumes a functioning barometric channel, and weakens where that channel is
absent. This is not in tension with adopting the GNSS altitude for the \emph{dynamics} of
fallback flights (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}): there, GNSS is used because it is the only
altitude available; here, the question is different---whether an instrument can
\emph{witness} a suspected GNSS lock loss---and the GNSS altitude cannot, because it comes
from the very receiver whose lock is in doubt and freezes together with the position it
would be checking. On GNSS-fallback flights the witness slot of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozenlock} is therefore filled by the recorder's declarations alone (the
\texttt{V} flag or zeroed GNSS altitude, or a byte-identical repeat); the diameter and
duration conditions still apply unchanged, so \enquote{alone} refers only to the witness,
not to the whole rule. The residual error cases are then:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Wrongly cut} (GNSS-fallback only): a circling climb tight enough to
        stay within $\delta_{xy}$ whose fixes carry a \texttt{V} flag---with no barometer
        to defend it, the declarations cut it.
  \item \emph{Wrongly kept} (GNSS-fallback only): an undeclared \emph{jittering}
        freeze. Some receivers, having lost the lock, do not repeat the last position
        byte-for-byte but re-estimate it each second, so consecutive positions differ by a
        metre or two of receiver noise: the run is genuinely frozen, yet neither
        byte-identical nor declared, and it is kept.
  \item \emph{Kept, at bounded cost} (barometric flights): a jittered freeze under a
        \emph{climbing} barometer is kept by construction. \rev{The cost is bounded, but it
        is not nil, and it is worth naming exactly. Every position recorded during the run
        sits inside the $\delta_{xy}$-ball of the last true fix, so the run enters the
        analysis as a wait. If the wing did move during it---wind drift, or a genuine glide
        under a climbing barometer---that motion goes unrecorded: the path length is
        under-counted by the distance actually covered, and a waiting time of up to the
        run's duration is inserted where there was none. What is \emph{not} lost is the
        displacement across the run, because the first position after re-acquisition is
        correct again; the quantities that read a position at a given elapsed time (the
        MSD, the propagator) therefore see the right endpoint, and it is the
        path-accumulating quantities---path length, phase durations---that pay.}
\end{itemize}
\rev{The first two cases---\emph{wrongly cut} and \emph{wrongly kept}---are expected to be
rare: each needs a lock loss, a fallback flight, and a run whose bounding diameter happens
to sit just on the wrong side of $\delta_{xy}$, all at once. Their frequency, however, is
not something the pipeline can report, and it would be wrong to claim otherwise: a rule
that fails does so silently, and counting how often a detector \emph{fires} says nothing
about how often it was right to. What the audit below measures is the firing rate; what
measures the error rate is the injected-defect calibration of check~(d), where defects of
known size are planted in clean tracks and the recovered fraction is counted directly. The
default to keep is what makes the two asymmetric in the meantime: an unfired rule leaves
real dynamics intact.}

\paragraph{Completeness of the resampled trajectory.}
The segmentation (Ch.~\ref{ch:phases}) needs a defined horizontal position \emph{and}
altitude at every time, since the vertical velocity is its discriminant. Cleaning does not
guarantee this fix by fix -- it may delete a fix (a corrupt horizontal position) or mark a channel
missing (a barometric spike, a \texttt{V} fix on a GNSS-fallback flight) -- because the
guarantee is established one step later, at resampling (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). A
\emph{segment} is a maximal stretch of the flight between two splits---equivalently,
between long gaps. \rev{It is the unit of analysis in the sense that every quantity
accumulated along the path---a path length, a phase duration, a smoothing window---is
computed inside one segment and never across the gap that ends it, since the trajectory
between two segments is unknown (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). The guarantee that the
segmentation needs is therefore not \enquote{complete $(x,y,z)$ everywhere} but
\enquote{complete $(x,y,z)$ \emph{within} a segment}}, not across a split, established by
the single audited interpolation of Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}.

\paragraph{Flight-level integrity gate.}
Cleaning acts fix by fix, but it also keeps count of how much it had to do. The counters
are taken over the airborne window: defects in a ground phase that trimming removes
anyway must not condemn the flight. If cleaning has removed or reconstructed more than a
small fraction of a flight's airborne fixes, the flight is dropped whole.

\paragraph{Validating the cleaning.}
All the detector parameters are validated by one procedure, run once the cleaning
routine is built (Ch.~\ref{ch:phases}):
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item \emph{Freeze the working values.} The thresholds are fixed a priori at the
        values of \rev{Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}}; nothing is tuned
        on the transport observables themselves.
  \item \emph{Audit the removal fractions.} The cleaning is run over the full
        archive with per-rule counters (the same machinery as the integrity gate): for
        each rule and each discipline, the fraction of fixes flagged and the fraction
        removed. The expectation is that each rule touches only a small fraction of
        fixes; this is the criterion to be verified, not a fact assumed in advance.
  \item \emph{Sweep each threshold.} Each parameter is varied around its working
        value and the removal fractions are recomputed. \rev{\enquote{Well placed} is made
        quantitative rather than left to the eye. Write $\rho(\theta)$ for the fraction of
        fixes a rule removes at threshold $\theta$, and read the local logarithmic
        sensitivity
        \[
          S(\theta)=\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\log\rho}{\mathrm{d}\log\theta}\right| .
        \]
        A threshold sits on a plateau when $S\lesssim1$ over a decade centred on the
        working value: halving the threshold then at most doubles what it removes, which is
        the behaviour of a rule biting into a sparse tail of defects. $S\gg1$ means
        $\rho$ is growing like a power of the threshold with a large exponent---the rule has
        reached the bulk of the distribution and is removing dynamics, not defects---and the
        working value is reconsidered. Reporting $\rho(\theta)$ and $S$ at the working value,
        per rule and per discipline, is what the sweep produces.} The same sweep covers the
        split cap of Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}, monitored on its split fractions rather than on
        removed fixes.
  \item \emph{False negatives.} Steps (b)--(c) measure what the cleaning
        removes, not what it misses---the corrupted fixes that pass undetected.
        \rev{Nothing in an audit of removals can supply that number, so this check is run
        as a matter of course and not only on suspicion: defects of known size (spikes,
        frozen runs, time glitches) are injected into clean tracks in known quantity, the
        cleaning is re-run, and each rule is scored against each defect type on the four
        counts---defects recovered and missed, clean fixes wrongly removed and correctly
        kept. The result is a confusion table per rule per defect type, with the recovered
        fraction as a function of defect size, and it is the only direct measurement of
        the error rates that the firing counts of step~(b) cannot give.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Trimming of ground phases}
\label{sec:trimming}
\paragraph{Outer ground phases (take-off and landing).}
The logger starts recording before take-off and stops after landing; these ground phases must
be stripped before any kinematic analysis. Detection uses the \emph{horizontal} speed
$v_{xy}$ alone (from~\eqref{eq:haversine}) -- the vertical speed plays no role here. With
$v_0=\SI{\PreprocTakeoffSpeedMps}{\metre\per\second}$ and
$T_0=\SI{\PreprocSustainedS}{\second}$ (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}), the retained airborne segment
is $[t_{\mathrm{on}},t_{\mathrm{off}}]$ with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trimming}
  t_{\mathrm{on}}=\min\bigl\{t:\ v_{xy}>v_0\ \text{on}\ [t,\,t+T_0]\bigr\},
  \qquad
  t_{\mathrm{off}}=\max\bigl\{t:\ v_{xy}>v_0\ \text{on}\ [t-T_0,\,t]\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Take-off is thus the \emph{first} time the speed stays above $v_0$ continuously for
$T_0$, and landing the \emph{last} time it has done so: the same sustained-speed rule read
forward and time-reversed. Reading the landing rule backward from the end matters. A rule
of the form \enquote{cut at the first sustained drop below $v_0$} would fire in the middle
of the flight, because a wing soaring into wind can hold a ground speed near zero
while genuinely flying. The persistence $T_0$ prevents a gust or a
GPS glitch on the ground from being mistaken for take-off. A flight with no sustained
stretch at all has no airborne segment and is discarded, with the reason recorded.

\begin{revblock}
$T_0$ is set between two scales rather than tuned. From below, it must outlast the
artefacts it exists to reject: a ground gust that briefly pushes a laid-out wing, or a
position glitch that fakes a fast step, lasts a few seconds at most, and
$T_0=\SI{\PreprocSustainedS}{\second}$ is several times that. From above, it must cost
little when it is wrong, and it can misfire only by at most $T_0$ at each end of the
flight---negligible against the \SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute} minimum duration a
retained flight has to reach anyway (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter}). Between those bounds the
result is insensitive to the exact value, which is the reason it can be fixed a priori.
\end{revblock}

One caveat is structural. The criterion is a \emph{ground} speed, so a slow phase
that happens to sit right at the start or end of the flight---a launch straight into
ridge lift, say---can be clipped along with the ground phase. \rev{The fraction of each
flight that trimming removes is recorded per flight and its distribution over the archive
reported as a histogram once the pipeline runs in full. That histogram bounds the
\emph{exposure} to this failure---how much of a flight the rule can have taken---but it
does not detect the failure itself, since a correctly trimmed ground phase and a wrongly
clipped ridge-lift launch produce the same trimmed fraction. What separates them is
inspection at the tail: the flights in the upper percentiles of the trimmed-fraction
distribution are the candidates, and they are few enough to be examined one by one against
their altitude trace, which rises during a ridge-lift launch and does not on the ground.}
Both $v_0$ and $T_0$ affect that fraction, so they are made explicit
\impldetails{impl:trimming}.

\paragraph{Interior ground stints (mid-flight landings).}
The rule above trims only the
\emph{outer} ground phases: a mid-flight landing and relaunch recorded in one log (a
top-landing, say) would survive as an interior near-zero-speed stint and read downstream
as a false long wait in the tail of $\psi(\tau)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}). An
interior-ground detector is therefore
specified, built like the frozen-lock rule: it cuts only on corroborated evidence,
because its dangerous error is the opposite one -- the soaring-into-wind failure
mode above. \rev{A stint is cut only when \emph{both} of the following hold:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \rev{$v_{xy}<v_0$ continuously for at least
        $T_{\mathrm{ground}}=\SI{\PreprocInteriorGroundMin}{\minute}$---far beyond any
        search phase, so no genuine slow phase reaches this length;}
  \item \rev{the adopted altitude channel flat over the whole stint, its range staying
        within $\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}=\SI{\PreprocGroundFlatnessM}{\metre}$ once the slow
        barometric drift has been removed---a wing at zero ground speed in real air still
        moves vertically, the same independent-sensor argument as
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozenlock}.}
\end{itemize}
\rev{The detrending is not a detail. The barometric reference itself wanders by tens of
metres over an hour (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}), which over a stint of
$T_{\mathrm{ground}}$ is already of the order of $\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}$; testing the raw
range would let a perfectly stationary pilot fail the flatness condition on a pressure
change alone. What the condition has to measure is the \emph{residual} vertical motion
after that drift is taken out, and $\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}$ is a tolerance on that
residual.}

\begin{revblock}
That the spread is the central one is not cosmetic, and the alternative was in force
until it was measured. A full range is a maximum statistic: over zero-mean noise it grows without bound with the
number of samples, as $2\sigma\sqrt{2\ln n}$. At the metre-scale barometric noise of this
archive it reads \SI{4.6}{\metre} over a 60-sample stint and \SI{7.1}{\metre} over
\num{3000}, so against a \SI{5}{\metre} tolerance the same motionless pilot passed the
test on a one-minute stint and failed it on a ten-minute one---on noise alone. Since an
interior stint must last $T_{\mathrm{ground}}$ to be considered at all, it failed on every
stint the rule was ever offered, and the guard could not fire. The central span carries
the same units and the same reading and sits at $3.3\sigma$ whatever the stint's length,
so $\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}$ means the same thing on a stint of any duration; a genuine
climb of hundreds of metres exceeds it either way.

One consequence is stated rather than hidden. \emph{On a GNSS-fallback flight the guard
abstains.} The intended rule is that the vertical witness there uses the GNSS channel with
a tolerance widened to match its noise, and in doubt the stint is kept; the widening is
not implemented, because the factor it needs is a measured ratio between the two channels'
short-timescale noise and a first measurement on the archive did not reproduce the
ordering Fig.~\ref{fig:altnoise} reports. Until that is settled, the single barometric
tolerance is applied to both channels, which on a noisier one simply means the flatness
condition is not met and the stint is kept---the conservative half of the intended rule,
and the safe direction, since the error this guard exists to avoid is excising a genuine
climb. What it costs is that a mid-flight landing on a GNSS-fallback flight is neither
excised nor flagged.
\end{revblock}

A stint that is cut is excised, and the flight is split at the excision: the parts
before and after are treated from then on as separate segments of the same parent flight,
exactly as at a long gap (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). The segment rules apply unchanged---a
phase interrupted by the cut is censored rather than counted as complete, and the
flight-level cuts of Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter} are not re-applied to the individual
segments.

\begin{revblock}
The order of the two steps is worth stating explicitly, because it is the reverse of the
order in which they are described. Trimming runs first (step~(iii) of the pipeline) and the
flight-level cuts second (step~(iv)): a duration or a path length is only meaningful once
the ground phases are gone, so the cuts have to see the trimmed track. When trimming has
split a flight, those cuts are still evaluated on the \emph{parent}---its airborne duration
is the sum of its segments' durations, its path length the sum of their path lengths---and
not on each segment separately. The reason is stated in Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}: a segment is
short because a gap happened to fall there, which says nothing about whether the flight is a
genuine cross-country one, and re-applying a whole-flight criterion to a fragment would
throw away exactly the data recorded around gaps. Segments face only the one requirement
that is physically theirs---being long enough to carry the smoothing window
(\SI{\PreprocMinSegmentS}{\second}, Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}).
\end{revblock}

Stints shorter than $T_{\mathrm{ground}}$ are not cut but
\emph{flagged}: a wait with sustained $v_{xy}<v_0$ and a flat barometric trace is marked
suspect whatever its length, and the $\psi(\tau)$ fits are re-run with and without the
suspect waits as a sensitivity check -- the same flag-without-deleting pattern as the
outlier identifier. \rev{Flagged waits are reported, not merely used internally: their
count, and the share of the total waiting-time mass they carry, are given per discipline
alongside the two $\psi(\tau)$ fits, so the reader can see how much of the tail rests on
them.} $T_{\mathrm{ground}}$ and $\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}$ are parameters
of this guard; they will join the \texttt{trimming} block of the configuration file when
the guard is implemented, with working values \rev{as in Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}}.

\subsection{Flight-level filtering}
\label{sec:flightfilter}
After cleaning and trimming, some flights are still unfit for the ensemble analysis and are
dropped whole. The step is delicate: too aggressive a cut biases the transport statistics -- a
high minimum duration, for instance, preferentially keeps successful long flights and
\rev{inflates the apparent MSD exponent at long lags, since the flights that survive such a
cut are the ones that kept going}. The flight-level filtering therefore uses a
\emph{minimal-threshold strategy}: for each criterion, the threshold is set to the smallest
value that still clears non-genuine flights (sled runs, tows, aborted or truncated logs), so
the ensemble is disturbed as little as possible. In practice \enquote{minimal} is read
off the data, not guessed: for each criterion the full-archive distribution of its quantity
is drawn (Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc}, top row), together with the fraction of flights that the
cut alone would retain as the threshold is moved (bottom row).

\rev{The non-genuine
population does not announce itself the same way in both criteria. In the path length it
forms a distinct cluster at the low end, separated from the genuine flights by a sparse
tail, and the threshold can be placed just beyond it. In the duration histogram no such
cluster is visible; what locates the cut there is the retained-fraction curve alone, which
is flat over a wide stretch before the bulk begins.} The threshold is placed
\rev{in either case} on the
stretch where the retained-fraction curve is flat---there, moving the cut changes almost
nothing, which is precisely the statement that it removes junk and not signal. \rev{Five}
track criteria gate a flight, plus a metadata stratifier:
\rev{Two of the four are sums over the flight, and what they are summed over is part of
the rule rather than an implementation detail: the duration and the path accumulate
\emph{within} a block and never across the boundary that ends it, since the trajectory
there is unknown and a straight line drawn over it was never flown.}

\rev{A boundary is either
a marker an earlier stage left---an excised frozen-lock run, a re-acquisition offset, a
mid-flight landing---or an inter-fix gap wider than the $g_{\max}$ the resampling will
split at (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). The second kind is easy to forget, because nothing
marks it: a marker records what a \emph{stage} decided, and a hole in the log is nobody's
decision. Forgetting it counted ten minutes of missing record as ten minutes of flight,
and the six kilometres of straight line across it as six kilometres flown. Using the same
$g_{\max}$ here as there is what makes a flight measured over the intervals it will
actually be analysed over.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \emph{Recorded duration} $T\geq\SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute}$. A cross-country flight spans many
        climb--glide cycles; shorter logs are sled runs or aborted attempts. An upper
        sanity bound $T\leq\SI{\PreprocMaxDurationH}{\hour}$ removes loggers left running past the flight:
        the census holds \num{\StatScanParaOverlongCount} paraglider ``flights'' of
        \SIrange{\StatScanParaOverlongMinH}{\StatScanParaOverlongMaxH}{\hour}, with paths
        up to \num{\StatScanParaOverlongMaxPathKm}\,km (and none among the hang gliders),
        which would poison \rev{the long-lag analyses generally, the MSD among them}.
  \item \emph{Flown path length} $L\geq\SI{\PreprocMinPathKm}{\kilo\metre}$, with $L=\sum_i\Delta s_i$
        from the great-circle steps of~\eqref{eq:haversine}, a floor against degenerate
        logs. On the census, above \SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute} the cut catches
        \num{\StatScanParaShortPathCount} of \num{\StatScanParaLongEnoughCount} paraglider
        flights (median extent \StatScanParaShortPathMedianExtentKm\,km) and
        \num{\StatScanHangShortPathCount} of \num{\StatScanHangLongEnoughCount} hang-glider
        flights: a fraction of order $10^{-3}$ of either ensemble. Duration already clears
        tows and sled runs.
  \item \emph{Altitude activity.} \rev{The total range of the adopted altitude channel over
        the airborne track---the difference between its maximum and its minimum---must
        reach \SI{\PreprocMinAltRangeM}{\metre}. The threshold is a positive statement
        about what the flight did, not merely a guard against a dead sensor: a
        cross-country flight of at least \SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute} lives by
        climbing, and several climbs of several hundred metres each are what keep it
        airborne, so a record spanning less than
        \SI{\PreprocMinAltRangeM}{\metre} over that time was not soaring---a vehicle log, a
        tow, a stuck channel. The cut is nonetheless cheap, which is what makes it safe:
        moving it from \SI{75}{\metre} to \SI{\PreprocMinAltRangeM}{\metre} changes the
        retained share of barometric paraglider flights by under two points, so the two
        values sit on the same plateau and the population between them is thin. Like the
        other two, it is a \emph{whole-flight} criterion, evaluated on the parent before any
        splitting; an individual segment may well span less, and that is accepted for the
        same reason the duration cut is not re-applied per segment---a segment is short
        because a gap fell there, not because the flight was not a flight.
        One limitation belongs with it: the cached census records only the
        \emph{barometric} extremes, so on a GNSS-fallback flight it reads a range of zero
        whatever the flight actually did, and the diagnostic of Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc}c,f
        is drawn over the barometric flights alone. Auditing this cut on the fallback
        minority needs the GNSS extremes in the scan, which is a full re-read of the
        archive rather than a re-query of the cache.} They are counted and dropped with
        the reason recorded.
  \item \rev{\emph{Plausibility of the cleaned record.} The mean ground speed of the
        retained track, $L/T$, must not exceed the discipline's own fix-level bound
        $v_{xy}^{\max}$. This is a sanity bound of the same kind as the upper duration
        cut above, and it is here for the same reason: a small number of logs are corrupt
        beyond what fix-level cleaning can repair---runs of the null position $(0,0)$
        scattered through the track---and every jump that survives adds thousands of
        kilometres of path that were never flown. Asking a \emph{whole-flight average}
        to stay under the bound that already declares a single step impossible is the
        weakest statement that catches them, and it is deliberately weak: no genuine
        flight comes close, since the median mean speed is under
        \SI{10}{\metre\per\second} and the bound sits at
        \SI{\PreprocMaxHSpeedParaMps}{\metre\per\second}. On the present archive it removes
        \emph{no} flight at all---%
        \num{\StatPipeParaDropMeanGroundSpeedAboveTheFixLevelBound} of either
        discipline---and that zero is a result rather than a redundancy. The criterion was
        written when a corrupt jump still entered the flown path; it no longer does, since
        an impossible step is made a segment boundary at the fix level and a long gap is
        excluded from the sum here, so by the time the path reaches this test it contains
        only motion. What such a record now fails is the reach criterion below. The cut is
        kept because it is the cheapest possible check on that chain: if a future source,
        or a change upstream, ever lets an impossible length back into the path, this is
        where it surfaces, and its count stops being zero.
        No new configuration value is introduced---the bound is the one already set for
        the fix level, read at the scale of a whole flight.}
  \item \rev{\emph{Reach.} No fix may lie further from the first than the bound could
        have carried it in the time elapsed since:
        $\Delta s(\text{fix}_0,\text{fix}_i)\le v_{xy}^{\max}\,t_i$ for every $i$, because
        the flight started there. It is stated per fix, and deliberately not as the
        extent $D=\max_i\Delta s(\text{fix}_0,\text{fix}_i)$ against the whole duration
        $T$: that weaker form licenses a fix \SI{100}{\kilo\metre} out ten seconds into
        the record on the grounds that the flight went on for three hours, and it is not
        the statement the written table is checked against (Sec.~\ref{impl:verify}). One
        criterion, one form, in both places. Where the previous criterion bounds the path,
        this one bounds the displacement, and the difference is exactly what makes it
        necessary. A flight whose \emph{first} fix is the corrupt one
        has no impossible step at all once that step is made a boundary and its length is
        no longer summed into $L$: it passes on duration, on path, on altitude and on mean
        speed, and it simply sits thousands of kilometres from where it says it began.
        Five paraglider flights in the first full-archive run did (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}),
        and since that first fix becomes the origin of the local frame
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}), the displacement they carried was not one bad step but every
        coordinate of the record. On the archive the criterion removes
        \num{\StatPipeParaDropExtentOutOfReachOfTheFirstFix} paraglider and
        \num{\StatPipeHangDropExtentOutOfReachOfTheFirstFix} hang-glider flights, of order
        $10^{-3}$ of either ensemble; on a sample of \num{4000} per discipline it touches
        nothing else. Like the previous one it introduces no configuration value, and it is
        checked again on the written tables as an invariant of the output
        (Sec.~\ref{impl:verify}), where it is the statement $|\mathbf{r}(t)|\le
        v_{xy}^{\max}t$ that the whole of Chapter~\ref{ch:global} assumes.}
  \item \emph{Glider class} (metadata). For class-stratified analyses, flights whose
        \texttt{aile\_class} cannot be normalised onto the discipline's system
        \rev{(Sec.~\ref{sec:glider})} are dropped from
        the class-resolved sub-analyses but kept in the pooled one. Stratification by glider
        \emph{type} (paraglider vs hang glider) is already possible.
\end{enumerate}
\rev{Every count quoted above is a generated macro, recomputed against the thresholds in
force from a cached full-archive scan; how, and by which code, is in
Sec.~\ref{impl:flightfilter}.}
\flow{The thin tail of very slow loggers is handled by the $\Delta t$ stratification
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}); a separate minimum-fix-count cut would be redundant, since
above the duration cut even a \SI{10}{\second} logger records hundreds of fixes.} A further criterion, sampling
regularity, is applied later, where a flight's time base is examined (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}) \impldetails{impl:flightfilter}.

both disciplines, computed on the full census (no subsampling).}

value.

\subsection{From geographic to local Cartesian coordinates}
\label{sec:enu}
The cleaned, trimmed track is next mapped from geographic to local Cartesian coordinates.
The two remaining steps are different in kind (Sec.~\ref{sec:preproc}):
resampling and smoothing operate on the coordinates
\emph{componentwise}\rev{---and the smoothing returns their derivatives in the same
componentwise way (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol})}---and therefore need actual Cartesian components to
act on. Latitudes and longitudes cannot play that role---they are angles on a curved
surface, and differencing them componentwise does not yield displacements. The mapping
proceeds in two steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: geodetic to ECEF.} Each fix is converted to Earth-Centred,
Earth-Fixed (ECEF) coordinates on the WGS84 ellipsoid (semi-major axis
$a=\SI{6378137}{\metre}$, flattening $f=1/298.257223563$, $e^2=f(2-f)$)~\cite{nga_wgs84,esa_navipedia_ellipsoidal_cartesian},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ecef}
N(\varphi)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}},\qquad
\begin{aligned}
X &= (N+h)\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\\
Y &= (N+h)\cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\\
Z &= \big(N(1-e^2)+h\big)\sin\varphi,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $N(\varphi)$ is the prime-vertical radius of curvature and $h$ the ellipsoidal height,
measured along the ellipsoid normal; the transform is derived step by step in
Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}.

\paragraph{Geodetic versus geocentric latitude.} The latitude $\varphi$
in~\eqref{eq:ecef} is the \emph{geodetic} latitude: the angle between the equatorial plane
and the ellipsoid \emph{normal} at the point (Figure~\ref{fig:ellipsoid}). It must not be
confused with the geocentric latitude $\psi$, the angle to the line joining the point to
the Earth's centre $O$. On an ellipsoid the two differ, because the normal does not pass
through $O$: the gap $\varphi-\psi\approx (e^2/2)\sin 2\varphi$ \rev{(derived in
Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}) peaks at $\varphi=45^\circ$, where $\sin2\varphi=1$, and there
reaches} $\approx\!0.19^\circ$ (about $11.5'$), which if the two were confused would misplace a fix
by up to ${\sim}\SI{20}{\kilo\metre}$ of ground distance.

We use the geodetic latitude throughout, and no conversion is needed: WGS84 -- hence the GPS receiver and the IGC
\texttt{B} records (Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}) -- reports geodetic latitude, and
\eqref{eq:ecef} with $N(\varphi)$ is precisely the geodetic-to-ECEF transform
that consumes it. These are also the $(\varphi_0,\lambda_0)$ that orient the local
frame of Step~2 (Figure~\ref{fig:enu-rotation}).

\paragraph{Step 2: ECEF to local ENU.} Taking the first fix
$(\varphi_0,\lambda_0,h_0)$ \rev{of the \emph{trimmed} track as the origin---the first fix
of free flight, which is close to but not the take-off point on the ground, since the
ground phase has been removed (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming})}, the ECEF displacement
$\Delta\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_0$ is \emph{rotated} into the local East--North--Up (ENU)
frame tangent to the ellipsoid at that point\rev{---the word is exact, not loose: the
origin shift has already been taken out by forming $\Delta\mathbf{X}$, and what is left is
an orthogonal matrix of unit determinant, so lengths and angles are preserved and only the
axes change},
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}E\\N\\U\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\sin\lambda_0 & \cos\lambda_0 & 0\\
-\sin\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0 & -\sin\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0 & \cos\varphi_0\\
\cos\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0 & \cos\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0 & \sin\varphi_0
\end{pmatrix}
\Delta\mathbf{X}.
\end{equation}
with the rotation matrix derived in Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}. The ENU frame is the
local Cartesian frame in which the whole analysis is carried out: $E$ points east, $N$
north, and $U$ along the local vertical (the ellipsoid normal at the origin). The ECEF
coordinates of Step~1 are only an intermediate representation of the computation---no
observable is ever expressed in them. Over the spatial extent of a single flight the
tangent-plane approximation is accurate to well below the GPS noise for the horizontal
coordinates: projecting onto the tangent plane shortens a distance $d$ along the surface
by $\approx d^3/(24R_\oplus^2)$, about \SI{1}{\centi\metre} at $d=\SI{20}{\kilo\metre}$
and \SI{13}{\centi\metre} at \SI{50}{\kilo\metre}, against metre-scale GPS noise
(Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}).

For the vertical, the analysis does not use the rotation's $U$ component as its
working coordinate: it keeps the adopted altitude channel itself

\subsection{Uniform sampling within a flight}
\label{sec:uniform}
The smoothing and differentiation below assume a \emph{uniform} sampling interval, so each
surviving flight's time base is examined next. Loggers record at a fixed nominal rate --
paragliders mostly at \SI{1}{\second} (\StatScanParaDtOneSecPct\,\% of flights), hang
gliders
spread across several rates from \SIrange{1}{10}{\second} with a thin tail beyond
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sampling}) -- but the realised series can carry jitter and
drop-outs. We keep
each flight at its \emph{native} rate\rev{; a common cadence is unnecessary, because the
derivatives are not taken by finite differences across flights but read off the local
polynomial that the smoothing fits to each flight separately, and the flight's own
$\Delta t$ enters only as the factor that turns that polynomial's coefficients into
physical units (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}).} What matters is uniformity \emph{within} a flight, not across
flights.

Per flight, let $\Delta t$ be the median inter-fix interval. Three cases arise:
\begin{description}
  \item[Uniform.] All intervals equal $\Delta t$ to tolerance: the series is used as is.
  \item[Mildly irregular.] Isolated jitter or single drop-outs -- no gap beyond the
        split bound $g_{\max}=\min\!\big(\PreprocMaxGapFactor\,\Delta t,\
        \max(\SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second},\,2\,\Delta
        t)\big)$ (its two scales are motivated in the paragraph \emph{The split
        bound has two scales} below) and less
        than \SI{\PreprocMaxMissingPct}{\percent} of the grid missing
        (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}): the flight is resampled onto the uniform grid at its
        native $\Delta t$, interpolating across the small gaps only, and the
        interpolated fraction is recorded.
  \item[Broken by a long gap.] A gap longer than $g_{\max}$ -- native, or opened where
        cleaning removed a frozen-lock run (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}), or a position
        discontinuity such as a re-acquisition offset, where the track jumps and stays:
        both sides are genuine and only the transition between them is unknown, so it is
        handled exactly like a long gap -- is not bridged:
        interpolating across it would fabricate dynamics the smoothing could not tell
        from real motion. The flight is \emph{split} at the gap into independently
        analysed segments, each carried forward on its own; what a split means for the
        analysis is stated below.
\end{description}
The missing-fraction check runs here, and not among the flight-level cuts of
Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter}, because it needs the time base and the splits to exist
first. \rev{It needs the time base because \enquote{missing} is only defined once there is
a grid to be missing from: the quantity counted is the share of points of the uniform grid
at the flight's own $\Delta t$ that carry no recorded fix, and neither the grid nor its
spacing exists before this step. It needs the splits because without them a single long
gap---which is not going to be interpolated at all---would dominate the count and condemn
a flight whose segments are each perfectly well sampled.} It is evaluated \emph{per
segment}, after splitting: a segment whose residual
missing fraction (the grid points that would have to be interpolated) exceeds
\SI{\PreprocMaxMissingPct}{\percent} is dropped alone, and the flight is lost only if no segment survives.
A flight with no long gap is a single segment, so a gap-free flight missing more than
\SI{\PreprocMaxMissingPct}{\percent} of its grid is dropped whole by the same rule.

\paragraph{The split bound has two scales.}
The relative part, $\PreprocMaxGapFactor\,\Delta t$, tolerates \rev{isolated dropped fixes}
in proportion to the cadence
and caps the number of interpolated grid points at ten. Alone, however, it would let a
slow logger bridge very long holes: \SI{30}{\second} at $\Delta t=\SI{3}{\second}$,
\SI{100}{\second} at $\Delta t=\SI{10}{\second}$. The harm of a bridged gap is set by the
motion's own timescales, not the logger's: an interpolated stretch is a straight line, so
a gap approaching the thermalling period ($\sim\SI{30}{\second}$) could replace a full
circle, and the segmentation downstream would read the invented straight stretch as a
glide. The absolute cap of \SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second} is pinned by three constraints:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item at least $2\times$ the slowest common cadence (\SI{10}{\second},
        Fig.~\ref{fig:sampling}), so a single missed fix never splits a flight;
  \item at most about two thirds of a thermalling period, so a bridged gap can never
        hide a full circle;
  \item well below $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}=\SI{\PreprocFrozenTauS}{\second}$, for consistency: an
        excised frozen-lock run of that length is split, so a native gap of comparable
        length cannot be bridged.
\end{itemize}
One refinement keeps the first constraint true at \emph{every} cadence: the cap
is floored at $2\,\Delta t$, i.e.
$g_{\max}=\min\!\big(\PreprocMaxGapFactor\,\Delta t,\
\max(\SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second},\,2\,\Delta t)\big)$, so the
thin tail of very slow loggers (Fig.~\ref{fig:sampling}) also tolerates a single missed
fix. \rev{Which of the
three terms sets $g_{\max}$ depends on the cadence: at the dominant
\SI{1}{\second} rate it is the relative bound, $g_{\max}=\SI{10}{\second}$; from
$\Delta t=\SI{2}{\second}$ to \SI{10}{\second} it is the absolute cap,
$g_{\max}=\SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second}$; above that it is the $2\,\Delta t$ floor.}

\rev{On the census, the two-scale bound $g_{\max}$ just defined leaves
\StatScanParaGapSplitCombPct\,\% of paraglider
and \StatScanHangGapSplitCombPct\,\% of hang-glider flights with at least one split; had
the relative part $10\,\Delta t$ acted alone, without the absolute cap, the shares would
be \StatScanParaGapSplitRelPct\,\% and \StatScanHangGapSplitRelPct\,\%. The increase sits
at the slow cadences, where a \SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second} hole is only a few missed fixes
(generated macros, Sec.~\ref{impl:uniform}).} \rev{A split is cheap for the ensemble
observables (below) but costly for the \emph{phase} statistics: every split censors the
phase in progress when the gap opens, so splitting too eagerly preferentially removes
long phases from the duration distributions---the same tail the cleaning protects. For
this reason the absolute cap is treated as a working value like the
detector thresholds: it is swept in the validation procedure of Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}
and frozen where the split fractions plateau. The sweep is reported as a figure, not only
as a number: the fraction of flights with at least one split, and the fraction of phases
censored, plotted against the cap for each discipline, with the adopted value marked. That
plot is what shows the plateau, and therefore what justifies the choice.}

The bound is the same for every channel, altitude included. One might expect the
altitude to tolerate wider gaps, since the vertical speed is about an order of magnitude
smaller than the horizontal ($v_z\sim\SI{1}{\metre\per\second}$ against
$v_{xy}\sim\SI{10}{\metre\per\second}$); it does not, for two reasons. First, the
interpolation error over a gap is set by the \emph{curvature} of the signal, not by its
speed: a fast but straight motion is interpolated exactly, a slow but turning one is not,
so a slower channel is not automatically a smoother one. Second, and more decisively, the
altitude feeds $v_z$, the segmentation's discriminant. A long altitude gap interpolated
linearly returns one average slope, which erases any climb--glide transition inside
it---and transitions are exactly where $v_z$ swings fastest: \rev{a paraglider's} typical glide sink is
$1$--$1.5$\,\si{\metre\per\second} and \rev{its} climb in a working thermal
$2$--$4$\,\si{\metre\per\second} \rev{(hang gliders sink faster, at
$1.5$--$2$\,\si{\metre\per\second}, and climb over the same range); both sit well inside
the bulk of the $|v_z|$ distribution of Fig.~\ref{fig:fixlevel}b, far from the tail where
the absolute bound is placed}, so a
thermal entry swings $v_z$ by several \si{\metre\per\second} within a couple of seconds,
the worst case for interpolation. A real asymmetry between the channels does exist, but it
concerns \emph{isolated} missing samples, not gap width: a single missing altitude is
reconstructed far more reliably than a single missing position, because the barometric
signal is much smoother---that is what justified \enquote{drop the altitude only} at
cleaning (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}); it does not justify a wider gap bound for $z$.

\paragraph{Consequences of a split.}
The observables of Ch.~\ref{ch:global} are affected differently by a split. In
practice a split is bookkeeping rather than a new trajectory: the conversion to ENU
(step~v) runs before this step, so every fix already
carries coordinates and elapsed time referred to the parent origin, and the split only
assigns a segment identifier. \textbf{The key fact is that a segment keeps its
parent's origin and clock} (Sec.~\ref{sec:notation}): a GPS position is absolute, so the
positions after the gap remain exactly as valid as those before it. Ensemble quantities
(the MSD, the propagator) need the horizontal position at a given elapsed time, not the path
travelled in between, so a split costs them nothing---the flight simply contributes no
data at the lags that fall inside the gap. Quantities that instead \emph{accumulate
along the path}---the path length, a phase duration, a step length, any time-averaged
window---cannot be evaluated across a stretch whose true course is unknown: sums and
windows for these quantities are always taken inside one segment, never straddling a
gap. Three cautions follow.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item \emph{Censoring at the boundary.} A phase in progress when the gap opens is
        truncated: its duration is a lower bound, not a measurement. Boundary-truncated
        durations are flagged as censored and simply \emph{excluded}---from the
        $\psi(\tau)$ fits and from the phase-duration histograms alike---rather than
        counted as complete, which would let every split masquerade as a spuriously short
        wait. \rev{The censored phases are identified, then, exactly as a survival analysis
        would identify them; what is not done is to feed them back in through a
        censoring-aware estimator---a Kaplan--Meier survival curve, or a likelihood with a
        right-censored term, which would use the information that the true duration exceeds
        the observed one instead of discarding the observation. As long as the censored
        fraction stays small (it is recorded per discipline), plain exclusion
        keeps the estimators transparent at negligible cost, and the recorded fraction
        tells us if that assumption ever fails---if it does, the machinery to switch is
        the standard one just named.}
  \item \emph{A segment is not a fresh flight.} For observables referenced to the
        time since the flight began---the time-averaged MSD stratified by duration, for
        instance (Ch.~\ref{ch:global})---a segment starting at parent time $t_0>0$ is
        not re-zeroed into a new flight that starts at $t=0$. It is used for what it is:
        an observation window $[t_0,t_1]$ onto a process already $t_0$ old. Re-zeroing
        would mix statistics of young and aged trajectories, which an aging analysis must
        keep apart.
  \item \emph{No second pass of the flight-level cuts.} The duration and path cuts
        of Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter} were passed by the parent flight and are not
        re-applied to its segments. Re-applying them would be biased in a specific way:
        gaps concentrate in particular conditions (weak reception late in long flights,
        for example), so discarding every sub-\SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute} segment
        would throw away
        precisely the data recorded around gaps. A segment has to satisfy only what the
        downstream machinery physically requires---enough duration to support the
        smoothing window and the statistics computed within it. \rev{That minimum has a
        value and a place in the pipeline, and it belongs here rather than only in the
        configuration: a segment shorter than
        $T_{\mathrm{seg}}=\SI{\PreprocMinSegmentS}{\second}$
        (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}) cannot carry a smoothing window of
        $\tau_c=\SI{\PreprocSavgolTauCS}{\second}$ with enough points on either side of it,
        and is dropped alone; the rest of the flight is retained.}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpolation across short gaps.}
The scope of this step is set by the split bound: a gap longer than $g_{\max}$ splits
the flight and is never
interpolated, so what is filled here are only the \emph{short} holes, up to $g_{\max}$, that
remain inside a segment. The fill is done per channel, where a channel is one of the three
scalar series on the uniform grid: the horizontal coordinates $E(t)$ and $N(t)$ and the
adopted altitude $z(t)$.

\begin{revblock}
The sentence above is true of the \emph{time base} and not of a channel, and the two come
apart in exactly one place. $g_{\max}$ bounds the interval between two consecutive
\emph{fixes}; it says nothing about an interval over which a fix exists but carries no
value. That case is not hypothetical and is not rare: the vertical channel can be absent
while the horizontal record is perfect---a logger that writes no barometric value, or a
value the fix-level cleaning removed (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}, where the whole point of
the asymmetry is that such a fix is \emph{kept}). No time gap opens there, so no split is
declared, and the linear fill spans the hole whatever its length.

Two things follow, and both are decided here rather than left implicit. First, such a
hole does \emph{not} split the flight. The horizontal trajectory, which is what
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} measures, is intact across it; cutting it because the vertical
is missing would discard good data for a defect that does not touch it. Second---and this
is the correction the check demanded---the reconstruction must be visible. It was not: a
grid point sitting on a real fix whose altitude had to be invented was reported as
\emph{measured}, because the \texttt{interpolated} flag is a statement about the time base
alone. On a test flight missing \SI{500}{\second} of altitude the filled $z$ departed from
the truth by up to \SI{540}{\metre}, the smoothing differentiated the straight line into a
vertical velocity that was pure invention, and the record read
$\texttt{frac\_interpolated}=0$ with \texttt{was\_resampled} false---the flight declaring
itself uniform as recorded. The vertical therefore carries its own per-fix flag,
\texttt{z\_reconstructed}, with the per-segment share \texttt{frac\_z\_reconstructed} and,
per flight, the longest unbroken run \texttt{z\_gap\_max\_s}. The run length is reported
next to the fraction because a fraction cannot separate the two cases that matter: a
hundred holes of two seconds, which a straight line bridges well, from one hole of twelve
minutes, which it does not. Any analysis of the vertical---the climb/sink discriminant of
the segmentation above all---excludes on that flag; the horizontal analyses are untouched
by it, and that is the reason the flag is per channel rather than one for all three.
\end{revblock} The two horizontal coordinates are interpolated independently,
which is the componentwise interpolation of the horizontal position; the barometric and
GNSS altitudes are never mixed here---only the flight's adopted channel is filled
(Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}).

The horizontal coordinates, which the transport analysis measures directly, are
interpolated with a \emph{monotone piecewise cubic} (a shape-preserving Hermite
interpolant): one cubic per interval, \rev{whose slopes are chosen so that the interpolant
reproduces the monotonicity of the samples themselves---where the data increase, the curve
increases, and it does not turn back on itself between two points. The construction needs
no derivative data: the slope at each sample is estimated from the neighbouring samples
and then limited to whatever value keeps the monotonicity condition satisfied, which is
what distinguishes it from an unconstrained cubic spline and is the whole reason for using
it here. A spline free to choose its slopes can swing outside the range of the points it
passes through, and such an excursion would be indistinguishable, to the smoothing step,
from a real manoeuvre.}
The altitude is interpolated linearly\rev{\footnote{\rev{The bound follows from the standard
error formula for linear interpolation. On an interval of width $g$ the difference between
a $C^2$ function $z$ and the chord through its endpoints is
$z(t)-p(t)=\tfrac12\,\ddot z(\xi)\,(t-t_0)(t-t_0-g)$ for some $\xi$ in the interval; the
quadratic factor is largest at the midpoint, where it equals $g^2/4$, giving
$|z-p|\le\max|\ddot z|\,g^{2}/8$.}}}. The linear
error over a hole of width $g$ is bounded by $\max|\ddot z|\,g^{2}/8$; \rev{holes are
bridged inside a phase far more often than across a transition between phases, and within
a phase the barometric signal has a nearly constant slope---a steady climb or a steady
glide---so $|\ddot z|$ is small there (the low high-frequency content of the barometric
channel, Fig.~\ref{fig:altnoise}c, is the same statement in the frequency domain)}. So the error is at the metre scale even at the
\SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second} cap and far smaller for typical holes---transitions, where $\ddot z$ is
large, are handled by the flag below.

\begin{revblock}
The altitude is interpolated linearly and the horizontal coordinates by a monotone
cubic, and the asymmetry follows from what each channel is used for. The altitude is
differentiated once more than the horizontal coordinates before it is read---$v_z$ is the
segmentation's discriminant---so an interpolation artefact in $z$ propagates into a phase
assignment, whereas the same artefact in $E$ or $N$ is absorbed by the smoothing. Linear
interpolation cannot overshoot, and its error over a hole of width $g$ is bounded by
$\max|\ddot z|\,g^2/8$; a cubic is more accurate where the second derivative is smooth and
worse exactly where it is not, which is at the transitions a phase assignment turns on.
\end{revblock}

The result is precisely the completeness invariant promised at cleaning
(Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}): \emph{within a retained segment, every grid time carries a
defined $(E,N,z)$}, so \rev{the segmentation, and the analysis more generally, is} never handed a missing value. The
altitude values dropped at cleaning (a barometric spike, a \texttt{V} fix on a
GNSS-fallback flight) are the ones restored here. Every filled point carries a
flag---\texttt{interpolated} where the time base had no fix, \rev{\texttt{z\_reconstructed}
where a fix was there but its altitude was not}---with a concrete consequence: downstream
statistics can
distinguish measured from reconstructed samples, and a phase boundary that the
segmentation places inside a bridged stretch is treated like one at a segment
boundary---the true transition could lie anywhere inside the hole, so the adjoining phase
durations count as censored and are excluded from the duration fits, exactly as at a
split.

\rev{The gap bound $g_{\max}$ is the criterion called \enquote{sampling regularity} in the
flight-level filtering (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter});}
Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} makes the fraction of flights it leaves whole auditable. One caveat carries
downstream: a few observables are intrinsically $\Delta t$-dependent\rev{, the turning
angle above all. The dependence is in the observable's own definition: a turning
angle is the heading change over one step, so two loggers recording the \emph{same} motion
at \SI{1}{\second} and \SI{10}{\second} produce systematically different distributions of
it. The velocity is a partial exception, because it is not formed as a finite difference
here but read off the smoothing polynomial (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}); what remains
$\Delta t$-dependent in it is the effective width of that polynomial's window, which is
set in seconds and therefore spans a different number of samples at each cadence.

\subsection{Smoothing and differentiation}
\label{sec:savgol}
Velocity and acceleration drive the kinematics and the segmentation, but differencing raw GPS
\rev{coordinate series} amplifies the high-frequency noise characterised in Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}. A
\textbf{Savitzky--Golay} filter~\cite{savitzky1964} solves both problems at once: it fits a low-order polynomial
to a sliding window by least squares and reads off that polynomial, or its derivatives, at
the window centre, returning smoothed position, velocity and acceleration
in one pass per component.\footnote{\rev{Concretely: in the
window of $w$ samples centred on $t_i$, the coefficients of the polynomial
$P(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_k\,(t-t_i)^k$ minimize $\sum_j\big(x_j-P(t_j)\big)^2$; the smoothed
value is $a_0$, the velocity $a_1$, the acceleration $2a_2$. Because the grid is uniform,
the $a_k$ are fixed linear combinations of the window samples, so the whole filter is a
convolution with precomputed coefficients~\cite{savitzky1964}.}} \rev{The fit is done in
units of samples, so its coefficients come out per sample; the velocity and acceleration
in physical units are $a_1/\Delta t$ and $2a_2/\Delta t^{2}$, with the flight's own
$\Delta t$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). This is the only place the cadence enters, and it is
why flights of different cadences need no common grid.}

\rev{The window never crosses a segment boundary. Near a boundary, where a
centred window no longer fits, the polynomial is fitted on the nearest full window inside
the segment and evaluated off-centre (the standard boundary treatment of the filter). An
off-centre evaluation is an extrapolation of the fitted polynomial rather than an
interpolation, and the variance of a least-squares polynomial grows towards the ends of
its fitting interval, so the first and last few samples of a segment carry a larger
uncertainty than the interior ones. They are marked as such---the same per-sample flag
mechanism as the interpolated points---so that an observable sensitive to it (an
acceleration extreme, say) can be computed on interior samples only. Whether the exclusion
is worth making is an empirical question: with windows of five to a few tens of samples
and segments of at least \SI{\PreprocMinSegmentS}{\second}, the affected samples are a
small fraction of a segment, and the check is simply to recompute the observable with and
without them.}

\rev{The filter runs on the resampled grid
of Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}, so it sees a uniform time base with the short holes already
filled. The filled points carry no independent information---each is a smooth function of
its neighbours---so within a window they mildly correlate the samples; over holes this
short that introduces no bias, and their fraction is recorded per flight. Flights that
needed a great deal of reconstruction never reach this step: the interpolated share of a
segment is capped at \SI{\PreprocMaxMissingPct}{\percent} at resampling
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}) and the airborne share a flight may lose to cleaning at
\SI{\PreprocIntegrityMaxPct}{\percent} at the integrity gate
(Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}); what is \emph{recorded} here, and not merely thresholded, is
the per-flight value of that share, so a downstream statistic can be stratified on it.}

The filter has two hyperparameters, the window length $w$ (odd, in samples) and the
polynomial order $p$, fixed from the measured noise spectrum rather than by eye:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item From the ensemble Welch power spectrum of the raw ENU components on a sample of
        flights (the diagnostic of Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel} applied to $E$, $N$, $U$), read
        the frequency $f_c$ of the knee where the dynamical signal meets the flat GPS noise
        floor. Its reciprocal is a smoothing timescale $\tau_c=1/f_c$: dynamics slower than
        $\tau_c$ are signal, faster fluctuations are noise.
        \rev{Then set the window per flight from that timescale. Making $w$ span $\tau_c$
        is a \emph{choice}---the natural one, since it puts the filter's cut-off at the
        measured knee---not a result: $w$ is $\tau_c/\Delta t$
        rounded up to the nearest odd integer (odd, so that the window has a centre sample
        to evaluate at), with a floor at $w=5$,
        the smallest window on which a cubic fit is meaningfully determined. At the dominant \SI{1}{\second} cadence
        the two rules coincide: $w=5$ samples spans exactly $\tau_c$, and
        the smoothing acts at the measured noise scale. At every slower cadence the floor
        is the binding rule (already at $\Delta t=\SI{2}{\second}$, where
        $\tau_c/\Delta t<5$), and the five-sample window then spans \emph{more} than
        $\tau_c$---about \SI{10}{\second} for a \SI{2}{\second} logger, \SI{25}{\second}
        for a \SI{5}{\second} one---so slow loggers are smoothed somewhat more than the
        noise scale requires. The cost is accepted: at those cadences the noise band sits
        at or beyond the Nyquist frequency $1/(2\Delta t)$, so there is little to remove
        at the noise scale in the first place, and these flights are in any case excluded
        from the $\Delta t$-sensitive observables by the rate stratification
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).}
  \item \rev{Fix $p$ as the lowest order that works. An acceleration requires curvature
        in the fit, so $p\geq2$. One order more is needed for the sake of the
        \emph{velocity}: on a symmetric window, adding an even-order term leaves the
        odd-order coefficients untouched (a parity property of least squares on a
        symmetric grid), so the velocity of a $p=2$ fit is identical to that of a
        straight-line fit. What $p=3$ adds is the cubic coefficient $a_3$, which is
        proportional to the third derivative---the rate of change of the acceleration---and
        which shifts $a_1$: the velocity of a cubic fit is the first one that responds to
        the acceleration \emph{varying} across the window, rather than assuming it
        constant. Going higher only lets the fit follow more
        noise. Hence $p=\PreprocSavgolOrder$.}
  \item Treat the vertical separately from the horizontal, and condition it on the altitude
        \emph{source}. \rev{The a priori expectation was asymmetric: the smooth barometric
        channel would need a \emph{shorter} window than the horizontal coordinates, and
        the noisy GNSS vertical a \emph{longer} one than them. The measurement said
        otherwise: the three knees coincide at
        $f_c\approx\SI{0.2}{\hertz}$ ($\tau_c=\SI{\PreprocSavgolTauCS}{\second}$).
        \rev{This is a measured value and it carries the status of one: it comes from the
        per-channel Welch spectra of Sec.~\ref{impl:savgol}, read on a sample of the
        \emph{present} archive, and the plot that shows the three knees falling together
        is the evidence for it. Two qualifications belong with the number. It is read on a
        sample, not on one flight, because a single spectrum is too noisy to locate a
        knee; and it is read on data that have not yet passed the fix-level and
        flight-level stages, so it is provisional---the spectra are recomputed, and
        $\tau_c$ re-read, once the cleaned ensemble exists.} The reason is trivial:
        the flat high-frequency floor of every channel comes from the rounding the IGC
        format applies when the file is written---altitudes to whole metres, coordinates
        to thousandths of an arc-minute---rather than from receiver noise, and rounding
        noise flattens the spectrum at the same frequency whatever channel it sits on.
        What distinguishes the noisy GNSS minority is how \emph{high} its floor sits, not
        \emph{where} the knee is (Sec.~\ref{impl:savgol}). So the answer to whether
        $E$, $N$ and $z$ get different smoothing timescales is currently no: the
        per-channel machinery exists, for the noisy GNSS minority, but the adopted
        windows are equal.}
\end{enumerate}
\rev{The choice is then checked on a held-out sample---flights not used to place
$f_c$---with three acceptance criteria: the residual spectrum (raw minus smoothed) must be
flat above $f_c$, showing that what was removed is the noise band and nothing below it;
the smoothed kinematics must stay within physical bounds---the acceleration first, since
it is the highest derivative and therefore the most sensitive to the window, but the speed
and the climb rate on the same footing, against the same envelopes the fix-level bounds
use (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}); and the results must be
stable when $w$ is changed by one step} \impldetails{impl:savgol}.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{A fourth criterion, on the observable itself.}
The three above are properties of the filter. A fourth is a property of what the filter
is for: the smoothing must not remove the displacement the transport analysis is about to
measure. The concern is not visible in $\tau_c$ alone, because $\tau_c$ is not what sets
the window for most of the archive. The window is floored at
\PreprocSavgolMinWindow\ samples---one more than a cubic needs, so that it stays a
least-squares fit rather than an interpolation---and that floor binds for every logger
slower than \SI{1}{\hertz}. In \emph{seconds} the window is therefore
$\max(\tau_c,\,\PreprocSavgolMinWindow\,\Delta t)$: \SI{5}{\second} at a
\SI{1}{\second} cadence, \SI{50}{\second} at \SI{10}{\second},
\SI{150}{\second} at \SI{30}{\second}---which reaches the lower end of the range over
which Chapter~\ref{ch:global} fits its exponent.

Measured rather than argued, on synthetic trajectories of known exponent passed through
the adopted filter and read the way the pipeline reads them---displacement from the frame
origin, which is the \emph{raw} first fix (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}), not the smoothed
one---the answer is that the filter is transparent to the motion. For a superdiffusive
trajectory ($\alpha=1.8$, the regime Chapter~\ref{ch:global} finds) and no measurement
noise, the filtered MSD sits within \SI{2.3}{\percent} of the true one at a lag of two
samples and within \SI{1}{\percent} at every lag from three samples upwards. The reason
is structural rather than fortunate: a cubic Savitzky--Golay filter reproduces any locally
cubic trajectory \emph{exactly}, and a strongly correlated trajectory is locally close to
cubic, so it passes through untouched.

What does move the short-lag MSD is the opposite of smoothing, and in the opposite
direction. Independent measurement noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ per component
adds ${\approx}2\sigma^2$ to the MSD at every lag; where the true displacement is
comparable to that, the measured curve is a noise floor rather than motion. With
$\sigma=\SI{3}{\metre}$ the same experiment gives a measured-to-true ratio of $8.1$ at one
sample, $1.26$ at five, $1.05$ at twelve and $1.003$ at sixty-four---and the smoothing is
what pulls it down, the one-sample ratio falling from $8.1$ to $3.6$ when the window goes
from $5$ samples to $11$. So the filter does not erase the observable, it de-biases it;
the floor it leaves is set by $2\sigma^2\approx\SI{18}{\metre\squared}$, which the
measured MSD exceeds by orders of magnitude everywhere in the fitted range. The
consequence for reading Fig.~\ref{fig:msd} is that its first decade is instrument, not
transport, and the fit does not begin there. A second effect points the same way and
belongs beside it: a flight answers a lag with the position at \emph{its own} nearest
fix, so the curve's tread at short lags is that flight's $\Delta t$ rather than the
\SI{1}{\second} the lag grid is snapped to. For every logger slower than \SI{1}{\hertz}
the short-lag curve is a staircase, and the local slope of a staircase alternates between
zero and a spike. Both effects die out well below the lower end of the fit range, which is
why they are a caveat on reading panels~(a) and~(c) at short lag rather than on the
exponent.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Notation}
\label{sec:notation}
Throughout, $\mathbf{r}(t)=\big(x(t),y(t)\big)=\big(E(t),N(t)\big)$ denotes the
\emph{horizontal} position in this local frame and $z(t)$ \rev{the adopted altitude
channel (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}), kept at its measured value (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu})}. By
construction each trajectory starts at the horizontal origin, $\mathbf{r}(0)=\mathbf{0}$, with
the clock reset to $t=0$ at \rev{the start of free flight (the first fix of the trimmed
track, Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming})}, so $t$ is the elapsed flight time (a segment created by
a split at a long gap, Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}, keeps its parent flight's origin and clock). The vertical is
\emph{not} re-zeroed: \rev{the measured height at the start of free flight} is retained
\flow{(Sec.~\ref{sec:enu})}. The horizontal
velocity is $\mathbf{v}(t)=\dot{\mathbf{r}}(t)$, with magnitude the horizontal speed
$v_{xy}=|\mathbf{v}|$; the vertical velocity is $v_z=\dot z$. The horizontal displacement over
a lag $t$ is $\Delta\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}(t_0+t)-\mathbf{r}(t_0)$.
\rev{A terminological rule holds throughout the document: when a statement concerns
$\mathbf{r}$ it says \emph{horizontal position} explicitly, and when it concerns the full
three-dimensional point it says \emph{3D position}; a bare \enquote{position} is avoided.}

\label{sec:prelim}

\paragraph{Isotropy, and what it costs.}
The transport analysis would like to study the one-dimensional marginal of the propagator
in place of the full two-dimensional density (Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global}), a substantial
simplification. The reduction is legitimate only if the process is isotropic, and isotropy
cannot be assumed here. The CFD flights are not races flown together---each is a solo
cross-country flight, later entered into a scoring ladder---but a single flight is
strongly directed: a chosen route, the orography, the valley winds. Isotropy can therefore
hold at best at the level of the \emph{ensemble}, and the check is run there.

The primary test is the per-component variance ratio: for an isotropic process
$\langle E(t)^2\rangle=\langle N(t)^2\rangle=\mathrm{MSD}(t)/2$ at every $t$, so the ratio
$\langle E(t)^2\rangle/\langle N(t)^2\rangle$ should be identically $1$.
Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-strata}a shows it is not. Over the lags the transport analysis
reads it sits at \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMedian\ for paragliders, between
\StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMin\ and \StatPrelimParaIsoRatioMax\ and never touching unity: the
north--south spread exceeds the east--west by about a quarter, steadily and across three
decades. The hang gliders are anisotropic the other way, at
\StatPrelimHangIsoRatioMedian. A Kolmogorov--Smirnov comparison of the empirical
distributions of $E(t)$ and $N(t)$ at \StatPrelimParaIsoKsLags\ lags agrees, with
distances from \StatPrelimParaIsoKsMin\ to \StatPrelimParaIsoKsMax; at this sample size
the associated $p$-values are numerically zero and carry no information, which is why the
distance is quoted and the test is not.

The consequence is operative. The two marginals $P(E(t)=x)$ and $P(N(t)=x)$ are analysed
\emph{separately} rather than pooled, and the pooled 1-D marginal is not used as a stand-in
for the 2-D propagator. That is the natural fallback and is informative in itself: unequal
scaling exponents $H_E\neq H_N$, or equal exponents with different scaling functions,
signal geometrically anisotropic transport, which for flights channelled along Alpine
valleys is the expected outcome rather than a nuisance. The anisotropy is also a reason to
read the ensemble MSD with the care Sec.~\ref{sec:obs-global} gives it, since
$\langle|\mathbf{r}|^2\rangle$ sums two components that do not grow alike.

\paragraph{Compatibility across strata.}
The dataset spans more than two decades, two glider types and a range of performance
classes (Sec.~\ref{sec:glider}), from recreational EN\,A/B wings to CCC racing wings,
whose kinematics differ substantially. Before treating the flights of one discipline as
draws from a single statistical process, the subpopulations are checked. The test is the
horizontal MSD computed within each stratum and read against the pooled curve over the
same lags; the two disciplines are never pooled, and a stratum below \num{2000} flights is
not drawn as if it were a measurement. The class normalisation of Sec.~\ref{sec:glider}
runs first, since as long as one wing class appears under several spellings a stratum is
not a well-defined set of flights.

Figure~\ref{fig:prelim-strata}b--d gives three axes and two answers. Across the
\num{\StatPrelimParaClassStrata} wing classes the curves separate: the typical stratum
departs from the pooled curve by \SI{\StatPrelimParaClassTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent}
and the extreme by \SI{\StatPrelimParaClassMaxDeparturePct}{\percent}, with the CIVL
competition wings above the pooled curve throughout and the EN\,A wings well below it.
Across the \num{\StatPrelimParaGroupStrata} orographic groups they separate further,
by \SI{\StatPrelimParaGroupTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} typically and
\SI{\StatPrelimParaGroupMaxDeparturePct}{\percent} at the extreme, with the Massif Central
at half the pooled curve over the intermediate lags and the Alps above it. Across the
\num{\StatPrelimParaSeasonStrata} seasons they do not: the typical departure is
\SI{\StatPrelimParaSeasonTypicalDeparturePct}{\percent} and the largest
\SI{\StatPrelimParaSeasonMaxDeparturePct}{\percent}, and the latter belongs to the
smallest season in the archive.

So the ensemble may be pooled over time and may not be pooled over wing class or over
terrain. Season is not a relevant stratum and the archive's two-decade span is not a
confound. Class and geography are relevant strata, and the difference is itself a result
rather than a nuisance: a competition wing covers ground faster than a recreational one,
and thermals over a mountain range are anchored to slopes and organised along ridges
whereas over flat terrain they drift with the wind, so the persistence of the glides and
the spacing of the climbs need not be the same. Every transport statistic of
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} is therefore reported per class and per orographic group as
well as pooled, and a pooled exponent is read as an average over strata that are known to
differ, not as a property of a homogeneous population.
\end{revblock}

\section{Pipeline summary}
\label{sec:pipelinemap}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipelinemap} closes the chapter with the whole processing chain in one
map: every step the data undergo between the raw IGC tracks of Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}
and the ensemble handed to the transport analysis, in the order the pipeline applies
them. Table~\ref{tab:pipelinemap} pairs each stage with the level it acts at, its
effect, and the adopted parameters; its last column leads back to the section where
each choice is argued.

Two decisions organise the chain. First the order: every check that runs on the scalar
record as logged --- times, geodetic coordinates, altitude --- comes first
(stages~1--4), and the vector geometry is built only from what survives
(stages~5--7); a coordinate conversion neither repairs a corrupt record nor has to be
undone when one is dropped (Sec.~\ref{sec:preproc}). Second the level: each stage acts
per fix, per flight or per segment, and never on more than one flight at a time.
\rev{The point is not that pooling flights would be tempting---it would not---but that
each stage's parameters are set from the flight it is acting on. The smoothing window
comes from that flight's own $\Delta t$, the outlier scale from its own local MAD, the
resampling grid from its own cadence; none is an archive-wide constant. What is shared
across flights is only the thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}, and those are
fixed a priori, not estimated from the ensemble---so no flight's cleaning can depend on
which other flights happen to be in the dataset.}

The parameters in the table are the adopted operating point of
\path{configs/preprocessing.yaml}, typeset through macros that
\path{generate_census_stats.py} regenerates from that same file (implementation
details: Table~\ref{tab:impl-repro}), so the summary follows the configuration in
force.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{What the chain retains.}
Run over the whole archive, the cascade retains
\num{\StatPipeParaKept} of \num{\StatPipeParaAttempted} paraglider flights
(\StatPipeParaKeptPct\,\%) and \num{\StatPipeHangKept} of \num{\StatPipeHangAttempted}
hang-glider flights (\StatPipeHangKeptPct\,\%). Table~\ref{tab:pipecensus} says where the
rest went. The numbers are a \emph{cascade}, not a set of marginal effects: a flight
counted against one criterion was still standing when that criterion tested it, so the
table reads in pipeline order and the counts do not add up to what each cut would remove
on its own (those are the \texttt{\textbackslash StatScan*} numbers quoted in
Secs.~\ref{sec:flightfilter} and~\ref{sec:uniform}, measured on the raw population).

Inside the retained flights the cleaning is light, which is what the design asked for:
it deletes \StatPipeParaRemovedPct\,\% of paraglider fixes and
\StatPipeHangRemovedPct\,\% of hang-glider ones, and invalidates an altitude on a further
\StatPipeParaAltInvalidPct\,\% and \StatPipeHangAltInvalidPct\,\%. \rev{The local outlier test
flags far more than is deleted, and by design: it never gates, so
\StatPipeParaFlaggedKeptPct\,\% and \StatPipeHangFlaggedKeptPct\,\% of fixes are flagged
and \emph{kept}. The flag is a per-fix record and a per-flight anomaly count, and what
condemns a fix is the impossibility gate (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}).}
Resampling reconstructs \StatPipeParaInterpPct\,\% and \StatPipeHangInterpPct\,\% of grid
points. A split is common but not the rule: \StatPipeParaSplitPct\,\% of retained
paraglider flights and \StatPipeHangSplitPct\,\% of hang-glider ones carry at least one,
the difference following the cadences of Fig.~\ref{fig:sampling} as
Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform} anticipated.
\end{revblock}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{The analysis ensemble, defined.}
\label{par:ensemble}
Everything downstream reads one object, and this is it. The \emph{analysis ensemble} is
the set of trajectories written to \path{fixes.parquet}: one row per grid point of every
\emph{retained} segment of every \emph{retained} flight, carrying the elapsed time $t$ in
the parent clock and the local Cartesian coordinates $(E,N,z)$ with their first two
derivatives. A flight is retained when it passes stages 1--4 of
Table~\ref{tab:pipelinemap} and at least one of its segments survives stage 6; a segment
is retained when it is long enough and dense enough on its own uniform grid. That leaves
\num{\StatPipeParaKept} paraglider flights of \num{\StatPipeParaAttempted}
(\SI{\StatPipeParaKeptPct}{\percent}) in \num{\StatMsdParaSegments} segments, and
\num{\StatPipeHangKept} hang-glider flights of \num{\StatPipeHangAttempted}
(\SI{\StatPipeHangKeptPct}{\percent}) in \num{\StatMsdHangSegments}. No consumer re-applies
a retention rule: a flight or segment that should not be analysed has no rows to read.
Every segment keeps its parent's origin and clock, so a position is always measured from
that flight's take-off and a time is always elapsed since it, whether or not the record is
continuous up to that point. Section~\ref{sec:prelim} characterises this ensemble,
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} analyses it, and \path{docs/guide/data-on-disk.md} states which
columns each estimator reads and what a segment boundary means to it.
\end{revblock}
