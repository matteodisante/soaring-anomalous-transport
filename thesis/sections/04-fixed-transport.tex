\chapter{Horizontal transport and displacement scaling}
\label{ch:fixed-transport}
\input{generated/ch3_transport_values}

Horizontal displacement measures how efficiently a flight converts time into spatial
exploration. We first describe its second moment, then resolve the contribution of
circuit type and initial altitude. Quantiles, moments of different orders and kurtosis
probe whether the growth of the second moment reflects a common rescaling of the
whole displacement distribution. Paragliders and hang gliders are analysed separately.

\section{Mean-square displacement}
\label{sec:fixed-msd}
\subsection{Estimator and temporal resolution}

Let $\boldsymbol{x}_f(t)=(E_f(t),N_f(t))$ be the cleaned horizontal trajectory of flight
$f$. Within its retained continuous segments, define
$\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{fi}(\tau)=\boldsymbol{x}_f(t_i+\tau)-\boldsymbol{x}_f(t_i)$
and $R_{fi}(\tau)=|\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{fi}(\tau)|$. With $n_f(\tau)$ valid origins,
\begin{equation}
 m_f(\tau)=\frac{1}{n_f(\tau)}\sum_{i=1}^{n_f(\tau)}R_{fi}(\tau)^2,
 \qquad M_2(\tau)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{f=1}^{N}m_f(\tau).
 \label{eq:fixed-msd}
\end{equation}
We use this \emph{equal-flight} average because the statistical unit of interest is a
flight: each contributes total weight $1/N$, irrespective of its duration or recorder
cadence. Within a flight, the temporal average pools valid overlapping increments
from its selected segments. Segment boundaries never form artificial adjacent fixes.

For $10\leq\tau\leq10000$ s, positions lie on a 10 s grid starting at each segment's
first fix. Records with native cadence above 10 s are excluded. A common grid gives
identical physical lags across recorder cadences and limits the cost of the calculation.
Positions at missing grid times are linearly interpolated within a segment. For example,
a native 8 s cadence requires interpolation between 8 and 16 s for the position at
10 s. No interpolation crosses a segment boundary. The 33 measured lags are approximately
logarithmic multiples of 10 s, including exactly 100 and 1000 s. Markers in every figure
identify these evaluations; connecting lines guide the eye.

\subsection{General transport and uncertainty}
\label{sec:fixed-general}

Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-general} compares Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixed-msd} with displacement
from the trajectory origin after trimming the take-off phase. For the latter, elapsed
time is measured on the retained parent clock, including gaps; a gap gives missing
support, rather than resetting the origin. Positions at requested elapsed times are
linearly interpolated within segments, with native cadence no larger than that time.
At each elapsed time we average the squared
ENU distance over available flights, and also report its median and 5th--95th percentile
range. This is an empirical ensemble over the archive's mixture of sites, days and
flight conditions. It is a meaningful ensemble of that mixture, although it does not
represent repeated launches under identical conditions. Changing duration support can
also change the mixture with elapsed time.

The shaded range in the origin-distance panels describes \emph{scatter among flights}.
All other shaded bands in this chapter are pointwise bootstrap intervals for the
plotted statistic. We group flights by catalogue take-off site and calendar date;
site names are normalised and disambiguated by department. Missing site/date keys
produce singleton groups. From the $G$ groups in the eligible archive, each of
$B=1000$ replicates samples $G$ groups with replacement and retains every flight
in each selected group. The replicate is then restricted to the relevant cohort.
If group $g$ is drawn $b_g$ times, its equal-flight mean is
\begin{equation}
 M_2^{(b)}(\tau)=
 \frac{\sum_{f\in\mathcal C}b_{g(f)}m_f(\tau)}
      {\sum_{f\in\mathcal C}b_{g(f)}}.
 \label{eq:fixed-bootstrap}
\end{equation}
The same group multiplicities are used across lags, observables and cohort comparisons;
ratios and fits are recomputed in each replicate. The 5th and 95th percentiles of the
replicate estimates define a nominal 90\% confidence interval at each lag. Under the
sampling assumptions, its repeated-sample coverage is approximately 90\%; it neither
contains 90\% of flights nor gives simultaneous coverage of an entire curve.
For the sparsely supported tail of the general TA-MSD, bands are omitted below
20 contributing site--day groups; the descriptive points remain visible.

Cluster resampling permits dependence between flights in a group and between
increments of a flight. Its justification requires sufficiently many approximately
independent groups, a relevant sampling population, and stable statistics; independence
alone is insufficient~\cite{cameron_miller2015}. The archive is observational, so inference
concerns its represented flight population. Table~\ref{tab:fixed-cohorts} gives group
counts for each cohort. \input{generated/ch3_transport_cluster_context}
Nearby sites and consecutive flying days can share weather, so geographical and temporal
separation support only an approximate independence assumption. The intervals quantify
sampling variability under this grouping; residual dependence and the selection of
uploaded flights are not included.

For any second-moment curve we define an effective exponent
$H_{\rm loc}(\tau)=\tfrac12\,d\log M_2/d\log\tau$. Numerically, this is the slope of
a local least-squares fit within $\pm0.25$ decades of each lag, using at least three
points, divided by two. A single summary exponent is obtained from
\begin{equation}
 \log_{10}\!\left[M_2(\tau)/(1\,\mathrm{m}^2)\right]
 =a+2H\log_{10}\!\left[\tau/(1\,\mathrm{s})\right]
 \quad (10\leq\tau\leq10000\,\mathrm{s}).
 \label{eq:fixed-fit}
\end{equation}
Ordinary least squares gives each evaluated lag equal weight. Correlated lags are
handled by refitting the coupled bootstrap curves. We report the root-mean-square
logarithmic residual in dex to distinguish an effective global slope from an accurate
single power law.

A renewed increase of the local slope after its minimum is consistent with the
resumption of gliding after the initial search for lift and climb. The minimum thus
provides a rough timescale for the completion of that combined phase in the aggregate;
identifying a mean duration for individual flights would require phase labels. The
mean exceeds the median because large displacements contribute disproportionately.
Pointwise flight percentiles do not track a fixed subset of trajectories through time.

\subsection{Fixed population and the effect of duration selection}
\label{sec:fixed-population}

At larger lags, short flights and short segments leave the average. This can alter
its slope through a changing proportion of local flights, distance flights and launch
sites, even before any change in their own displacement scaling. We therefore hold
both flights and segment identities fixed for the controlled comparisons.
For a maximum lag $\tau_{\max}$, select segments satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \tau_{\max}\leq0.8T_s,
 \label{eq:fixed-support}
\end{equation}
where $T_s$ is the elapsed duration on the actual 10 s grid. A flight enters if at least
one of its segments qualifies. The main cohort, $\mathcal C_{10000}$, therefore uses
segments of at least 12500 s. This leaves at least one fifth of each segment's duration
available for origins at the largest lag. In a flight with segments of 14000 and
3000 s, only the 14000 s segment contributes, including at a lag of 10 s. Thus the
short segment cannot enter at small lags and disappear at large ones. The ordinary
number $n_f(\tau)$ of within-segment increments still decreases with lag; the flight's
total weight remains $1/N$.

\section{Transport by circuit type and initial altitude}
\label{sec:fixed-circuit}

The catalogue's declared circuit class separates open distance flights from closed
routes such as triangles and out-and-return flights. We cross this distinction with
initial cleaned GNSS altitude: Plains ($z_0<300$ m), Hills ($300\leq z_0<800$ m),
Low mountains ($800\leq z_0<1500$ m), and High mountains ($z_0\geq1500$ m).
These are launch-altitude classes; they do not classify the terrain crossed later.
This comparison uses paragliders and the same $\mathcal C_{10000}$ support rule in
all eight cells. Counts and fitted exponents are given in Table~\ref{tab:fixed-task}.

For open routes, Plains and Hills have larger $H$ than either mountain class. Thus

\input{generated/ch3_transport_task_contrasts}
The closed-route exponents also differ between altitude classes: the present intervals
do not support a common value across them. A return objective can suppress net
horizontal advance on scales approaching a circuit's duration, although the declared
class does not guarantee exact closure of the trimmed trajectory. Greater persistence
in low-altitude open flights is consistent with fewer topographic constraints on
horizontal progression. This observational comparison does not isolate orography
from weather, route selection, pilots or equipment.

The overall MSD combines these distinct behaviours. For an exhaustive partition
into groups with $p_g=N_g/N$, including unclassified flights,
\begin{equation}
 M_2(\tau)=\sum_g p_g M_{2,g}(\tau),\qquad
 H_{\rm loc}(\tau)=\sum_g
 \frac{p_gM_{2,g}(\tau)}{M_2(\tau)}H_{{\rm loc},g}(\tau).
 \label{eq:fixed-mixture}
\end{equation}
The effective slope is therefore weighted by each group's contribution to the second
moment. It is not the arithmetic mean of the fitted exponents. The sizeable open and
closed populations make this mixture physically relevant to the general curve.

Wind can contribute to both amplitude and apparent scaling. For an additive constant
velocity $\boldsymbol u$, write $\Delta\boldsymbol x=\Delta\boldsymbol y+\boldsymbol u\tau$:
\begin{equation}
 \langle|\Delta\boldsymbol x|^2\rangle
 =\langle|\Delta\boldsymbol y|^2\rangle
 +2\tau\boldsymbol u\cdot\langle\Delta\boldsymbol y\rangle+|\boldsymbol u|^2\tau^2.
 \label{eq:fixed-wind}
\end{equation}
Even a uniform constant drift can change the slope of this uncentred MSD through its
ballistic term. A common deterministic shift cancels from the centred covariance;
drifts differing between flights need not cancel from the mixture. A variable wind
field can additionally modify velocity correlations. Wind is not separated quantitatively
in this comparison.

\section{Scaling of displacement distributions}
\label{sec:fixed-scaling}

A superdiffusive MSD alone does not determine the displacement law. We now ask whether
small and large displacements grow at the same rate, and whether a common exponent
accounts for moments of different orders. Every observable below uses exactly the
main cohort's flights, segments, lag grid and equal-flight measure.

\subsection{A common empirical distribution}

At lag $\tau$, an increment has weight
$w_{fi}(\tau)=1/[Nn_f(\tau)]$. This divides a flight's fixed total weight among its
available origins. For an amplitude $Y\in\{|\Delta E|,|\Delta N|,R\}$, define
\begin{equation}
 \widehat F_{Y,\tau}(y)=\frac1N\sum_{f=1}^N\frac1{n_f(\tau)}
 \sum_{i=1}^{n_f(\tau)}\mathbf1\{Y_{fi}(\tau)\leq y\},\qquad
 Q_{Y,p}(\tau)=\inf\{y:\widehat F_{Y,\tau}(y)\geq p\}.
 \label{eq:fixed-cdf}
\end{equation}
Operationally, we calculate the fraction below $y$ within each flight and average
these fractions across flights, then invert the resulting weighted empirical CDF.
The quantile is an observed displacement amplitude at the first threshold crossing;
no interpolation between ranks is applied. Averaging individual-flight quantiles
would not invert this mixture distribution.

Absolute components resolve displacement amplitudes along east and north, while
$R=(\Delta E^2+\Delta N^2)^{1/2}$ is invariant under rotation and characterises the
full horizontal displacement without assuming isotropy. In particular,
$\Pr(|\Delta E|\leq a)=\Pr(-a\leq\Delta E\leq a)$: small absolute quantiles describe
proximity to zero displacement, which need not be the centre of a drifting signed law.

\subsection{Quantiles, growth exponents and relative spread}
\label{sec:fixed-quantiles}

A scale family $Y(\tau)\overset{d}{=}\tau^H Y(1)$ predicts
$Q_{Y,p}(\tau)\propto\tau^H$ for every $p$. We measure $p=0.25,0.50,0.75,0.90$ and fit
each logarithmic quantile curve on the same 33 lags. Its slope is $H_{Y,p}$; no factor
of two enters because the observable is a length. The ratio $Q_{Y,0.25}/Q_{Y,0.90}$
compares the growth of small amplitudes with that of large ones. Its logarithmic
slope satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\log(Q_{Y,0.25}/Q_{Y,0.90})}{d\log\tau}
 =H_{Y,0.25}(\tau)-H_{Y,0.90}(\tau).
 \label{eq:fixed-ratio}
\end{equation}
The same identity holds for the fitted slopes when both logarithmic curves use the
same lags and least-squares weights. Ratio drift and unequal quantile exponents
therefore express the same comparison in complementary forms.

\subsection{Moment spectrum and finite-range scaling}
\label{sec:fixed-moments}

Using the same empirical measure, the moment of order $q$ is
\begin{equation}
 M_{q,Y}(\tau)=\frac1N\sum_f\frac1{n_f(\tau)}\sum_iY_{fi}(\tau)^q,
 \qquad \log M_{q,Y}(\tau)\simeq a_{q,Y}+\zeta_Y(q)\log\tau.
 \label{eq:fixed-moments}
\end{equation}
For each order $q\in\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1,1.5,2,2.5,3,3.5,4\}$, we first calculate
$M_{q,Y}$ at every lag, then fit its logarithm against $\log\tau$. Plotting the resulting
slope against $q$ constructs the moment spectrum. A common scale family with finite
moments predicts $\zeta_Y(q)=qH$; the reference line in Fig.~\ref{fig:fixed-spectrum}
is fitted through the origin with equal weight for the ten orders. Larger $q$ gives
more weight to rare large displacements. The radial spectrum includes the MSD itself:
\begin{equation}
 M_{2,R}(\tau)=M_{2,|\Delta E|}(\tau)+M_{2,|\Delta N|}(\tau)=M_2(\tau).
 \label{eq:fixed-second-identity}
\end{equation}
This identity is checked for both the measured curves and every bootstrap replicate.

\subsection{Excess kurtosis of the signed components}
\label{sec:fixed-kurtosis}

To assess departures from Gaussian marginal increments, we retain the signs of east
and north displacements. With the same weighted average $\langle\cdot\rangle_\tau$
as above, set $\bar X_j=\langle\Delta x_j\rangle_\tau$ and
\begin{equation}
 K_j(\tau)=
 \frac{\langle(\Delta x_j-\bar X_j)^4\rangle_\tau}
      {\langle(\Delta x_j-\bar X_j)^2\rangle_\tau^2}-3,
 \qquad j\in\{E,N\}.
 \label{eq:fixed-kurtosis}
\end{equation}
Centring uses the mean of the full equal-flight mixture at each lag. This Fisher
excess has Gaussian reference zero~\cite{nist_kurtosis}. Folding a Gaussian component
into its absolute value, or taking the radius of a Gaussian vector, changes its law
and removes that zero reference. Signed components therefore answer the intended
question directly. A nonzero excess excludes a Gaussian marginal when reliably
estimated; zero excess alone does not establish Gaussianity, and Gaussian marginals
do not establish a Gaussian joint process.

Mixing flights with different conditional means and variances can itself generate
excess kurtosis. These measurements characterise the empirical mixture; attributing
its shape to a universal within-flight mechanism requires further conditioning.

\subsection{Implications for self-similarity}

\input{generated/ch3_transport_scaling_conclusion}
For a stochastic process, self-similarity requires equality of all finite-dimensional
laws, $\{\boldsymbol X(at)\}_t\overset{d}{=}\{a^H\boldsymbol X(t)\}_t$~\cite{embrechts_maejima_selfsimilar}.
Part of the observed scale dependence can arise from mixing circuit classes with
different growth rates. Here, the observables concern the distribution of increments
at one lag, averaged
over available origins. Constant quantile ratios and a linear moment spectrum would
support a common scale family on the measured range; they would not determine its
multi-time laws or establish stationarity of increments. The fixed population makes
this comparison interpretable by removing a change of flight membership from the
lag dependence.
