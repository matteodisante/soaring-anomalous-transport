\section{Experimental: joint factorial analysis}
\label{sec:conditional-factorial}

\noindent\textbf{Experimental analysis.}
This exploratory extension brings the altitude, circuit and equipment
comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:fixed-circuit} into one table. Its purpose is to
describe departures from additivity in the effective
exponent; the coverage of its uncertainty intervals remains conditional on the
resampling assumptions. We retain \num{\FactorialN} flights in
$\mathcal C_{10000}$ with an open or closed circuit and an admitted equipment
class, excluding \num{\FactorialExcludedN} other cohort members. The four
initial-altitude classes, two circuits and two equipment groups define 16
disjoint cells. Each $\widehat H_{ijk}$ is fitted to that cell's equal-flight MSD
over 10--10000 s, with the estimator and retained segments of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:conditional-msd}.

\paragraph{Factorial model and additive projection.}
We use the fixed-effects factorial decomposition into main effects, two-factor
interactions and a three-factor interaction, with the sum-to-zero constraints
of Mohr, Wilson and Freund~\cite[Sections~9.3.1 and~9.7]{mohr2022statistical}.
Writing the empirical components without hats to simplify notation,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \widehat H_{ijk}={}&\mu+\alpha_i+\gamma_j+\eta_k\\
 &+(\alpha\gamma)_{ij}+(\alpha\eta)_{ik}
   +(\gamma\eta)_{jk}+(\alpha\gamma\eta)_{ijk},
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:factorial-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes altitude, $j$ circuit and $k$ equipment. Every effect sums to
zero over each of its own indices. These constraints make the decomposition
unique. Bars below denote unweighted means of the fitted cell exponents:
$\mu=\overline H_{\cdot\cdot\cdot}$,
$\alpha_i=\overline H_{i\cdot\cdot}-\mu$, with analogous expressions for
$\gamma_j$ and $\eta_k$; for example,
$(\alpha\gamma)_{ij}=\overline H_{ij\cdot}
-\overline H_{i\cdot\cdot}-\overline H_{\cdot j\cdot}+\mu$.
The three-factor term is the remaining component.

The flights are unbalanced across cells. As discussed in
Section~9.3.7 of the same reference, balanced-design sums of squares cannot be
applied unchanged to such flight-level data. Here equal weights instead define
a descriptive projection of the \emph{16 estimated exponents}. They give the
same importance to each combination, irrespective of flight count or precision.
Consequently the marginal effects average cell exponents; they are generally
different from fitting the MSD of a pooled or reweighted flight population.
The logarithm of a mixture of MSDs does not equal a mixture of their logarithms.

The additive approximation is the ordinary least-squares projection onto the
grand mean and main effects under this equal-cell metric:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \widehat H^{\rm add}_{ijk}
 &=\overline H_{i\cdot\cdot}+\overline H_{\cdot j\cdot}
   +\overline H_{\cdot\cdot k}-2\overline H_{\cdot\cdot\cdot},\\
 r_{ijk}&=\widehat H_{ijk}-\widehat H^{\rm add}_{ijk}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:factorial-additive}
\end{equation}
It has six free coefficients. The residual occupies the ten-dimensional
interaction space: $3+3+1$ dimensions for the three pairs and three for the
three-factor interaction. The complete decomposition reconstructs the observed
table exactly, while sampling uncertainty remains in every estimated component.
We do not use the residual as an estimate of a common error variance or apply
the classical ANOVA $F$ tests. In particular, bootstrap draws are not independent
experimental replications of a cell; the missing-error issue for an unreplicated
factorial table is discussed in~\cite[Section~9.6]{mohr2022statistical}.

The orthogonal components partition the squared variation between the cell
estimates. For each component $u$, Table~\ref{tab:factorial-decomposition}
reports its share $\sum_{ijk}u_{ijk}^2/
\sum_{ijk}(\widehat H_{ijk}-\mu)^2$ and its root-mean-square magnitude.
The additive share is
\begin{equation}
 R^2_{\rm add}=1-
 \frac{\sum_{ijk}r_{ijk}^2}
      {\sum_{ijk}(\widehat H_{ijk}-\mu)^2},
 \qquad
 R_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{16}\sum_{ijk}r_{ijk}^2}.
 \label{eq:factorial-variation}
\end{equation}
These are descriptive summaries of estimated cell variation, which includes
sampling noise. They do not measure a causal fraction of transport explained.

The additive terms account for \FactorialAddPct\% of this variation, with
residual RMS \FactorialResidualRms{}. Altitude $\times$ circuit alone accounts
for \FactorialAcPct\%, almost all of the nonadditive component. The altitude
main effect contributes only \FactorialAltPct\%: this small marginal share
reflects compensation between circuit-specific patterns, rather than an
absence of altitude dependence. The residual map has opposite signs for open
and closed circuits, reversing between lowlands and mountains in both equipment
groups (Figure~\ref{fig:factorial-cells}).

\paragraph{Simple effects and interaction contrasts.}
Following the distinction between marginal comparisons and comparisons within
interacting factors~\cite[Section~9.4]{mohr2022statistical}, we examine the
simple effects
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \Delta_C(i,k)&=\widehat H_{i,\mathrm{open},k}
                 -\widehat H_{i,\mathrm{closed},k},\\
 \Delta_E(i,j)&=\widehat H_{i,j,\mathrm{experts}}
                 -\widehat H_{i,j,\mathrm{beginners}}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:factorial-simple}
\end{equation}
For a compact interaction contrast, let
$D_k=\Delta_C(\mathrm{Plains},k)-\Delta_C(\mathrm{High\ mountains},k)$.
This difference of differences measures how much the circuit separation
decreases between the endpoint altitude classes at fixed equipment. Its change
$D_{\rm E}-D_{\rm B}$ is one contrast in the three-factor interaction space;
it does not exhaust that three-dimensional space. These are exploratory
comparisons informed by the earlier stratified results, rather than hypotheses
specified before examining the archive.

For uncertainty we reaggregate the saved per-flight MSDs using the original
1000 paired site--day cluster-bootstrap draws, then refit each cell in every
draw. Each linear contrast is evaluated on the same draw across all cells.
We construct \emph{single-step bootstrap simultaneous confidence intervals}
using the maximum absolute standardized deviation, following the joint-region
construction of Romano and Wolf~\cite[Sections~3--5]{romano_wolf2005}.
For a declared family $\mathcal F$ of 19 contrasts---the eight $\Delta_C$,
eight $\Delta_E$, two $D_k$ and $D_{\rm E}-D_{\rm B}$---our implementation is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 q_{0.90}&=Q_{0.90}\!\left[
  \max_{\ell\in\mathcal F}
  \left|\frac{\widehat\theta_\ell^{(b)}-\widehat\theta_\ell}
                  {\widehat s_\ell}\right|\right],\\
 I_\ell&=\widehat\theta_\ell\pm q_{0.90}\widehat s_\ell,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:factorial-simultaneous}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat s_\ell$ is the standard deviation across bootstrap draws.
We use fixed marginal bootstrap scales, a first-order plug-in standardization,
without recomputing a standard error inside each draw or performing stepdown
testing. The nominal 90\% simultaneous level applies to this declared family
within one fit window and resampling scheme; it does not cover all earlier or
subsequently selected comparisons. Its population coverage still requires an
adequate cluster-resampling model~\cite{cameron_miller2015}.

All eight circuit contrasts remain positive under these simultaneous intervals.
Their decrease from lowlands to mountains survives equipment stratification:
$D_{\rm B}=$\FactorialDB{} and $D_{\rm E}=$\FactorialDE{}.
The equipment contrasts are smaller. The intervals include zero for closed
circuits in Plains and Hills, while the other six are positive under this
resampling model. The endpoint three-factor contrast is
$D_{\rm E}-D_{\rm B}=$\FactorialTriple{}; its interval includes zero, leaving
the difference between the two endpoint attenuations unresolved.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and experimental status.}
We repeat the joint bootstrap using calendar days across all sites, three-day
calendar blocks, and the 50 km cell $\times$ altitude $\times$ month-of-year
groups of Section~\ref{sec:fixed-bootstrap-reliability}, pooling years in the
last case. Each alternative uses 1000 draws and two seeds, with the whole
eligible archive as sampling frame before selecting the fixed cohort and cells.
The point estimates are unchanged. The endpoint contrasts stay positive and
their equipment difference stays unresolved (Table~\ref{tab:factorial-sensitivity});
the inclusion or exclusion of zero also remains unchanged for the 16 simple
contrasts across these alternatives. These checks assess sensitivity, not
independence of the clusters or nominal coverage. The smallest cell has 65
flights in 64 site--day groups, falling to 44 contributing groups under the
spatial/seasonal partition; support cannot be judged from flight count alone.

\section{Experimental: scaling of displacement distributions}
\label{sec:fixed-scaling}

\noindent\textbf{Experimental analysis.}
This section was moved from Chapter~\ref{ch:fixed-transport}. Its results depend on
the same cohort and resampling assumptions as the MSD analysis there, and they are
not part of the main argument.

The MSD measures one moment of the displacement law. To test whether the whole law
rescales with one exponent, we use quantiles, a spectrum of moments and excess kurtosis.
All use the main cohort's flights, segments and weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixed-weights}.

\subsection{Self-similarity and its observable consequences}
\label{sec:fixed-selfsimilar-definition}

A process $\boldsymbol X(t)$, with $\boldsymbol X(0)=0$, is $H$-self-similar if,
for some $H>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \bigl(\boldsymbol X(at_1),\ldots,\boldsymbol X(at_m)\bigr)
 \overset d= a^H\bigl(\boldsymbol X(t_1),\ldots,\boldsymbol X(t_m)\bigr)
 \quad(a>0).
 \label{eq:fixed-selfsimilar}
\end{equation}
This identity must hold for every $m\geq1$ and $t_1,\ldots,t_m\geq0$.
Equality in distribution concerns positions at any collection of times, including
their dependence~\cite{embrechts_maejima_selfsimilar}. Gaussianity and independence
of increments are not required.

Two separate hypotheses stand between this definition and a measurement, and each
does different work. Stationary increments make a pooled lag statistic informative
about $\boldsymbol X$ itself: with $\boldsymbol X(0)=0$ they give
$\boldsymbol X(\tau)\overset d=\boldsymbol X(t+\tau)-\boldsymbol X(t)$, so
displacements collected from many origins estimate the law of the position reached
after an elapsed time $\tau$. The archive supplies no repeated releases from a common
initial condition, which leaves this the only route from the increments we observe
to the process we describe. Self-similarity then compresses the resulting family of
laws: one exponent and one shape determine every lag, including elapsed times the
cohort does not cover, and that exponent is the quantity stochastic transport
models predict. Without it this chapter reports a separate
propagator at each measured lag, with no relation between them. Testing
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixed-selfsimilar} is therefore a test of whether one anomalous
exponent describes these flights at all, and the sections below report necessary
consequences of that identity rather than a proof of it.

To infer a lag-only scaling law, we additionally assume stationary increments:
their joint laws must be unchanged by shifting the origin.
Self-similarity alone does not impose this condition. With stationary increments,
each amplitude $Y\in\{|\Delta E|,|\Delta N|,R\}$ obeys
\begin{equation}
 Y(\tau)\overset d=(\tau/\tau_0)^H Y(\tau_0),\qquad
 \begin{aligned}
 Q_{Y,p}(\tau)&=(\tau/\tau_0)^H Q_{Y,p}(\tau_0),\\
 M_{q,Y}(\tau)&=(\tau/\tau_0)^{qH}M_{q,Y}(\tau_0).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:fixed-scale-family}
\end{equation}
Here $Q_{Y,p}$ is a population quantile and $M_{q,Y}$ a finite moment of order $q$;
below, the same symbols denote their equal-flight estimates.
Thus every quantile has the same exponent, quantile ratios are constant, and the
moment spectrum is $\zeta_Y(q)=qH$. Signed-component excess kurtosis is also constant
when the fourth moment exists and variance is nonzero; it need not vanish. These are
necessary consequences for the lag distributions. A finite selection of such
statistics cannot establish the joint-law identity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixed-selfsimilar}.

\subsection{A common empirical distribution}

With the weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixed-weights}, the common empirical distribution is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat F_{Y,\tau}(y)=\frac1N\sum_{f=1}^N\frac1{n_f(\tau)}
 \sum_{i\in\mathcal I_f(\tau)}\mathbf1\{Y_{fi}(\tau)\leq y\},\qquad
 Q_{Y,p}(\tau)=\inf\{y:\widehat F_{Y,\tau}(y)\geq p\}.
 \label{eq:fixed-cdf}
\end{equation}
We average the within-flight fractions below $y$, then invert the resulting CDF.
The quantile is the first observed amplitude at which the cumulative weight reaches
$p$, without interpolation between ranks. This order of operations defines the
quantile of the equal-flight mixture.

Absolute components measure amplitudes along east and north. For example,
$\Pr(|\Delta E|\leq a)=\Pr(-a\leq\Delta E\leq a)$, so small absolute quantiles
measure proximity to zero displacement. The radius $R=(\Delta E^2+\Delta N^2)^{1/2}$
is invariant under rotation and measures horizontal displacement without assuming
isotropy.

\subsection{Quantiles, growth exponents and relative spread}
\label{sec:fixed-quantiles}

We evaluate $p=0.25,0.50,0.75,0.90$ and fit each quantile over the same 33 lags.
Writing $\mathcal B$ for the least-squares slope against $\log_{10}\tau$
used in Section~\ref{sec:fixed-general},
\begin{equation}
 H_{Y,p}=\mathcal B\!\left[\log_{10}\frac{Q_{Y,p}}{1\,\mathrm m}\right],\qquad
 \mathcal B\!\left[\log_{10}\frac{Q_{Y,0.25}}{Q_{Y,0.90}}\right]
 =H_{Y,0.25}-H_{Y,0.90}.
 \label{eq:fixed-ratio}
\end{equation}
No factor of two enters a length exponent. The ratio compares small and large
amplitudes at each lag, while the fitted exponents summarise their growth across
10--10000 s. The equality follows from linearity of least squares on identical lags;
ratio drift and the exponent difference are therefore linked diagnostics.
Figures~\ref{fig:fixed-quantiles-para} and~\ref{fig:fixed-quantiles-hang} give the
quantile curves and their ratio for each discipline, and
Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-quantile-h} collects the fitted quantile exponents.

\subsection{Moment spectrum and finite-range scaling}
\label{sec:fixed-moments}

For each amplitude and order we calculate
\begin{equation}
 M_{q,Y}(\tau)=\frac1N\sum_f\frac1{n_f(\tau)}\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_f(\tau)}Y_{fi}(\tau)^q,
 \qquad \zeta_Y(q)=\mathcal B\!\left[\log_{10}\frac{M_{q,Y}}{(1\,\mathrm m)^q}\right].
 \label{eq:fixed-moments}
\end{equation}
We use $q\in\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1,1.5,2,2.5,3,3.5,4\}$. Each point of the spectrum is
obtained by first computing a moment at all lags, then fitting its log--log slope.
The reference $\zeta=qH$ is fitted through the origin with equal weight for the ten
orders. Higher orders emphasise large displacements. The radial spectrum contains
the MSD, with the identity
\begin{equation}
 M_{2,R}=M_{2,|\Delta E|}+M_{2,|\Delta N|}=M_2
 \label{eq:fixed-second-identity}
\end{equation}
verified at every lag and in every bootstrap replicate.
Table~\ref{tab:fixed-fits} reports the main-cohort MSD exponents,
Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-local} compares their local and global values, and
Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-spectrum} gives the moment spectra.
Table~\ref{tab:fixed-scaling} collects the paired quantile and moment contrasts that
test $\zeta=qH$.

\subsection{Excess kurtosis of the signed components}
\label{sec:fixed-kurtosis}

Departures from Gaussian shape are assessed on the signed components: taking an
absolute value or a radius changes even a Gaussian law. With the equal-flight mean
$\langle A\rangle_\tau=\sum_f\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_f(\tau)}w_f(\tau)A_{fi}$, define
\begin{equation}
 \mu_j(\tau)=\langle\Delta x_j\rangle_\tau,\qquad
 K_j(\tau)=\frac{\langle(\Delta x_j-\mu_j)^4\rangle_\tau}
 {\langle(\Delta x_j-\mu_j)^2\rangle_\tau^2}-3,\qquad j\in\{E,N\}.
 \label{eq:fixed-kurtosis}
\end{equation}
Centring uses the mean of the full mixture at each lag. A Gaussian marginal has
$K_j=0$~\cite{nist_kurtosis}; nonzero excess measures a departure in its standardised
fourth moment. Zero excess alone does not establish Gaussianity. For self-similarity,
the relevant prediction is constancy with lag, regardless of the value.
Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-kurtosis} shows $K_j(\tau)$ for both components.

\subsection{Assessment of self-similarity}

The quantile curves and the nearly straight moment spectra can be reconciled through
\begin{equation}
 M_{q,Y}(\tau)=\int_0^1 Q_{Y,p}(\tau)^q\,dp.
 \label{eq:fixed-moment-quantile}
\end{equation}
Each moment averages over probability levels; the order $q$ controls the emphasis
on larger amplitudes. Fitting its slope
across three decades further averages over crossovers. Changes in different parts
of the distribution can consequently leave the fitted spectrum nearly linear.
Moreover, Table~\ref{tab:fixed-scaling} resolves small departures from $\zeta=qH$;
the spectrum is not exactly linear under the adopted bootstrap.

For these data, the lag-dependent quantile ratios provide the more direct evidence
against a fixed distributional shape. Their large, nonmonotonic changes are visible
without fitting a power law, and their estimation does not require finite high-order
moments. Unequal fitted quantile exponents support this reading, but the curved
quantile trajectories limit their interpretation as constant exponents. The change
in kurtosis supplies complementary evidence. Visual linearity of ten fitted moment
slopes is therefore insufficient to outweigh the observed shape changes.

Over 10--10000 s, a single self-similar model with stationary increments does not
adequately describe the pooled displacement law.
Approximate scaling over narrower intervals remains possible. Fixed flights and
segments remove changing membership, and the contrast below measures how much of the
remaining structure follows from where within a segment the origins fall. The present
analysis does not determine multi-time laws. It therefore does not exclude every
nonstationary self-similar process, nor establish an asymptotic multifractal law.

\paragraph{Does the increment law depend on the origin?}
\label{sec:fixed-origin-dependence}

Equation~\eqref{eq:fixed-scale-family} reaches the lag distributions through
stationary increments, and the estimators above pool every admissible origin inside
a selected segment. That pooling estimates one population law only if the law of
$\boldsymbol X(t+\tau)-\boldsymbol X(t)$ does not depend on $t$. We test this
directly. Within each selected segment the admissible origins at lag $\tau$ are
split into three contiguous blocks of equal size, and the same equal-flight
amplitude statistics are measured separately in each. The early and late blocks
receive identical origin counts in every segment, so each cohort flight contributes
to both and the contrast is paired inside the site--day draws of
Sec.~\ref{sec:fixed-bootstrap-reliability}. Under stationary increments the
late-to-early ratio equals one at every lag. Pooling the three blocks returns the
equal-flight moments of Sec.~\ref{sec:fixed-scaling} exactly, which fixes the
diagnostic to the published measurement rather than to a separate estimator.

For hang gliders, the late-origin second moment differs from the early-origin one at
\ChThreeHangOriginResolved{} of 33 lags. The largest departure is a ratio of
\ChThreeHangOriginPeak{} (\ChThreeHangOriginPeakInterval) at
$\tau=\SI{\ChThreeHangOriginPeakLag}{\second}$, where the two blocks are separated by
\SI{\ChThreeHangOriginPeakLever}{\second} on average. Fitting $H$ over 10--10000 s in

95\% interval from $\ChThreeHangOriginDeltaHLow$ to $\ChThreeHangOriginDeltaHHigh$.

The ratio departs from one over the whole lag range in both disciplines, so the
increments of these segments are not stationary. Displacements measured from late
origins exceed those from early origins across most of the range, with the largest
excess near $\tau\approx400$ s, and the excess closes at the longest lags. The two
blocks are separated by about 12000 s at small lags and by roughly half that at
$\tau=10000$ s, because the admissible origins shrink as the lag consumes the
segment; the contrast at the longest lags therefore rests on a shorter lever arm and
is not comparable in strength to the rest of the curve.

Two consequences follow for this chapter. First, the amplitude of the displacement
law is a property of the flight phase from which origins are drawn, so the pooled
one-lag laws of Sec.~\ref{sec:fixed-scaling} describe a mixture over flight phases
rather than one stationary increment law. Second, the fitted exponent is far less
sensitive to this mixture than the amplitude: the block-to-block change in $H$ is
resolved but small against the amplitude ratios in the same figure. Reading $H$ as a
lag exponent of the pooled mixture survives the test; reading it as the
self-similarity exponent of a stationary-increment position process does not.

The sign of the effect does not match waiting-time ageing, which suppresses late
displacements in a subordinated walk. Launch and initial circling confine the early
part of a flight, while route-following occupies its later part, and this ordering
alone produces the observed direction. The diagnostic separates origin position from
lag; it does not identify the mechanism, and the flight-phase, wind and pilot
explanations are not distinguished here.
