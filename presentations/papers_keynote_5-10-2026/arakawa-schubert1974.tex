% 1: goal and roadmap --------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[plain]\frametitle{Arakawa--Schubert (1974): goal of the paper}\startslide{Arakawa--Schubert (1974): goal of the paper}
 \lead{\textbf{Large-scale models need the combined heating and moistening effects of unresolved cumulus clouds.}}
 \head{7}{24.5}{14}{Goal}
 \txt{20}{24.5}{133}{Compute these cloud effects from the large-scale atmosphere itself.}
 \head{7}{30.5}{60}{How: three steps}
 \stepbox{7}{Step 1: budgets}{slide 2}{The environmental budgets reduce the cloud effects to three vertical profiles.}{3 profiles: $M_c(z)$, $D(z)$, $\hat l(z)$}
 \stepbox{58}{Step 2: cloud types}{slides 3--4}{Compute each cloud type, then combine them with one mass-flux spectrum.}{1 spectrum: $\MB(\lambda)$}
 \stepbox{109}{Step 3: closure}{slides 5--7}{A buoyancy balance determines the spectrum. Tropical data test it.}{none: the system is closed}
 \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]},pisablue,line width=.9pt](51.6,46.5)--(57.4,46.5);
 \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]},pisablue,line width=.9pt](102.6,46.5)--(108.4,46.5);
 \bottomline{The environment sets the cloud activity, which in turn changes the environment}
 \credit{Arakawa \& Schubert, J. Atmos. Sci. 31, 674--701 (1974). Abstract, \S1, \S8. Symbols: slides 2--4.}
\notetext{Interaction of a Cumulus Cloud Ensemble with the Large-Scale Environment, Part I, Arakawa and Schubert (1974), J. Atmos. Sci. 31, 674--701. A large-scale disturbance contains many cumulus clouds whose time and space scales are much smaller than its own. Because of this scale separation, the paper aims to predict the large-scale fields by describing only the collective influence of the clouds, not each cloud. The paper calls this the goal of cumulus parameterization. The result is meant for prognostic models of the large-scale atmosphere, such as the UCLA general circulation model.\par\smallskip The reduction has three steps. First, budgets for the environment show that the clouds change its dry static energy $\bar s$ and water vapour $\bar q$ only through three profiles: the total cloud mass flux $M_c(z)$, the total detrainment $D(z)$ and the liquid water $\hat l(z)$ in the detraining air. In the subcloud mixed layer they act only on its depth. Second, a spectral ensemble of entraining plumes computes all three profiles from the environment and a single unknown function, the mass flux $\MB(\lambda)$ of each cloud type at the top of the mixed layer. Third, the quasi-equilibrium of the cloud work function gives an integral equation for $\MB(\lambda)$, and the system is closed. Slide 7 shows the observational test of quasi-equilibrium.\par\smallskip The theory describes a two-way interaction: the clouds modify the environment (step 1), and the environment controls the clouds (steps 2 and 3). Earlier parameterizations were largely empirical. Ooyama (1971) had reduced the problem to a cloud-base dispatcher function but left its determination open, so his parameterization was not closed. The bottom line states this two-way interaction. Source: Abstract, Sections 1 and 8.}
\finishslide\end{frame}

% 2: environmental budgets --------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[plain]\frametitle{Step 1: clouds enter through three profiles}\startslide{Step 1: clouds enter through three profiles}
 \lead{Budgets of the air between clouds: the model knows every term except $M_c$, $D$ and $\hat l$.}
 \txt[font=\fontsize{11}{13}\selectfont]{7}{23}{72}{$\begin{array}{@{}r@{\;}c@{\;}c@{\;}c@{\;}c@{\;}c@{\;}c@{}}
  \rho\,\partial_t\bar s&=&\textcolor{teal}{D(\hat s-\bar s-L\hat l)}&+&\textcolor{wine}{M_c\,\partial_z\bar s}&+&\mathrm{LS}_s\\[1.6mm]
  \rho\,\partial_t\bar q&=&\textcolor{teal}{D(\hat q^*+\hat l-\bar q)}&+&\textcolor{wine}{M_c\,\partial_z\bar q}&+&\mathrm{LS}_q
 \end{array}$}
 \symbox{7}{37}{68}{%
  \sym{$s=c_pT+gz$,\ $q$}{dry static energy; vapour mixing ratio}
  \sym{$\bar s,\,\bar q$}{large-scale means, i.e.\ the environment}
  \sym{$M_c(z)$}{total upward cloud mass flux per unit area}
  \sym{$D(z)$}{mass detrained per unit volume and time}
  \sym{$\hat s,\ \hat q^*$}{detrained-air values (star: saturation)}
  \sym{$\hat l(z)$}{liquid water in the detrained air}
  \sym{$L,\ \rho$}{latent heat of vaporization; air density}
  \sym{$\mathrm{LS}$}{large-scale advection (+ radiation for $s$)}}
 \asmodel
 \bottomline{Unknowns left: three profiles, $M_c(z)$, $D(z)$ and $\hat l(z)$}
 \credit{\S2--4, Eqs. (7), (12), (72)--(75), (126). Figure: schematic redraw of Fig. 1. Colours match the equation terms.}
\notetext{These are the equations the large-scale model must integrate. All terms are known to it except the cloud terms. Cloud $i$ covers the area fraction $\sigma_i$ and carries the mass flux $\rho\sigma_iw_i$. The sum $M_c$ drives compensating environmental subsidence. In a stably stratified atmosphere ($\partial_z\bar s>0$) this warms, and since $\partial_z\bar q<0$ it dries. Detrainment supplies cloud air and liquid water, whose evaporation cools ($-L\hat l$) and moistens. The latent heat released inside the clouds does not warm the environment directly: it keeps the clouds buoyant and so maintains $M_c$ and the subsidence warming.\par\smallskip The variables are the dry static energy $s=c_pT+gz$, conserved by a dry parcel moving adiabatically, and the water-vapour mixing ratio $q$. Hats denote detraining-air values, the star denotes saturation, and $L$ is latent heat. $\hat s$ and $\hat q^*$ are not further unknowns: Eqs. (72)--(73) express them through $\bar s$, $\bar q$, $\bar q^*$ and $\hat l$. For legibility the slide groups the large-scale terms as $\mathrm{LS}_s=-\rho\bar{\mathbf v}\cdot\nabla\bar s-\rho\bar w\partial_z\bar s+\tilde Q_R$ and $\mathrm{LS}_q=-\rho\bar{\mathbf v}\cdot\nabla\bar q-\rho\bar w\partial_z\bar q$. $\tilde Q_R$ is the radiative heating, including that inside the clouds, which the paper sets aside.\par\smallskip In the subcloud mixed layer the clouds do not act on temperature and moisture directly. They drain mass through its top, and this cumulus-induced subsidence makes it shallower (Eq. 126). The equations assume a small active cloud fraction and a horizontally uniform environment. The figure redraws the paper's conceptual Fig. 1; it is a schematic, not a measured cloud map. Read it with the equations: the wine arrows are the mass flux $M_c$ rising in the clouds and the subsidence it forces between them, the teal arrows are the detrainment $D$ at the cloud tops. The updraft arrows widen upward because entrainment adds mass at every height. At the dotted level $z$, $M_c(z)$ sums the deep and medium clouds that cross it, and $D(z)$ comes from the medium cloud whose top is there: this is why the cloud terms are profiles in $z$. Section 4 opens with the conclusion on the slide: the parameterization problem is reduced to finding $M_c(z)$, $D(z)$ and $\hat l(z)$. Source: Sections 2--4, Figure 1, Equations (7), (12), (72)--(75) and (126).}
\finishslide\end{frame}

\notetext{Cloud types are labelled by the fractional entrainment rate $\lambda$, constant with height. Each type starts at the mixed-layer top $z_B$ with mixed-layer properties, $h_c(z_B,\lambda)=h_M$ (Eq. 131). The first equation gives $\eta=e^{\lambda(z-z_B)}$ up to the top: entrainment adds mass as the plume rises. The second is the moist-static-energy budget of an entraining plume (Eqs. 83--89), with $h=s+Lq$: entrained air pulls $h_c$ toward the environmental $\bar h$. Its integral form is $\eta h_c=h_M+\lambda\int_{z_B}^{z}\eta\bar h\,dz'$ (Eq. 97). Dividing by $\eta$ gives $h_c(z)=e^{-\lambda(z-z_B)}h_M+\lambda\int_{z_B}^{z}e^{-\lambda(z-z')}\bar h(z')\,dz'$: a weighted mean of the mixed-layer value and of the environment below $z$, with the nearest levels weighted most. The plume forgets $h_M$ over a length $1/\lambda$, 5 km for $\lambda=20\%$ km$^{-1}$; for $\lambda=0$ it keeps $h_M$. It never reaches $\bar h$: $\bar h$ changes with height, and since $\bar h<\bar h^*$ the plume stops first, where it meets $\bar h^*$.\par\smallskip Why $h_c>\bar h^*$ means buoyant: the cloud is saturated, and Eq. (98) gives $s_c-\bar s=(h_c-\bar h^*)/(1+\gamma)$ with $\gamma=(L/c_p)(\partial q^*/\partial T)_p>0$. At the same height $s_c-\bar s=c_p(T_c-\bar T)$, so the plume is warmer than its surroundings exactly when $h_c$ lies right of $\bar h^*$, and the top is where they meet. This neglects the vapour and liquid-water corrections, written out on slide 5, and the small difference between $\hat h^*$ and $\bar h^*$.\par\smallskip Reading Figure 6 (Jordan's mean hurricane-season sounding). The vertical axis is pressure, so up is higher. Solid curves are the environment: $\bar h$ has its minimum near 650 mb, the dry middle troposphere; $\bar h^*$ is the value it would have if saturated. Every plume starts at 950 mb with $h_M=82$ cal g$^{-1}$. The line $\lambda=0$ is undiluted and meets $\bar h^*$ only near the tropopause. As $\lambda$ grows, the plume is pulled toward the low $\bar h$, crosses $\bar h^*$ earlier and has a lower top, around 600 mb for $\lambda=20\%$ km$^{-1}$ ($=0.2$ km$^{-1}$). The horizontal gap to $\bar h^*$ is proportional to the temperature excess. The wine dots, added on the slide, mark these crossing points; the arrow marks the leftward pull of entrainment. The paper's Fig. 8 collects the crossing points as $p_D(\lambda)$. Simpson's tower model gives $\lambda=2\alpha/R$ (Eq. 94): large $\lambda$ means a narrow cloud. Source: Sections 4--5, Equations (83)--(98), (129)--(131), Figures 6--8.}

\notetext{Summing the cloud types gives the three profiles needed in the environmental budgets. The spectrum is a mass-flux density over entrainment rate: $\MB(\lambda)d\lambda$ is the mass flux at the mixed-layer top of all clouds with entrainment rate between $\lambda$ and $\lambda+d\lambda$ (the paper's sum over $\lambda_i\in(\lambda,\lambda+d\lambda)$ evaluated at $z_B$, Eqs. 78 and 81). Multiplication by $\eta(z,\lambda)$ gives its mass flux at height $z$.\par\smallskip $M_c(z)$ integrates the types still rising at that level, those with $\lambda<\lambda_D(z)$ (Eq. 77). Detrainment $D(z)$ comes from the types whose top lies at $z$ (Eq. 79). The derivative converts a cloud-type interval to a height interval, and the minus sign gives positive detrainment because $\lambda_D$ decreases with height. Each plume detrains all its mass in a thin layer at its top. The detrained liquid water is that of the type ending there, $\hat l(z)=l(z,\lambda_D)$ (Eq. 91). The liquid water comes from the total-water budget of the plume, $\partial_z[\eta(q_c+l)]=\lambda\eta\bar q-\eta r$ (Eq. 85, using $\partial_z\eta=\lambda\eta$): entrainment brings in environmental vapour, rain $r$ removes water. Above cloud base the cloud vapour $q_c$ is the saturation value, fixed by $h_c$ (Eq. 90), so what remains of the total water is liquid. Appendix B closes the budget with the crude rule $r=C_0l$, a constant fraction $C_0$ rained out per unit height, which gives $\eta l=\int_{z_B}^{z}e^{-C_0(z-z')}\{-\partial_{z'}(\eta q_c)+\lambda\eta\bar q\}\,dz'$ (Eq. B7): water condensed at each level, depleted exponentially by rain on the way up.\par\smallskip For a given environment and these model assumptions, the unknown cloud contribution has therefore reduced from three height profiles to one mass-flux spectrum. This spectrum does not separately prescribe cloud number or lifetime. The next two slides introduce the energy measure and the balance used to determine it. Source: Sections 4--5, Equations (77)--(91), (129)--(131), Appendix B (B7).}

 \bottomline{Positive $A$ allows buoyant energy production, but does not set the cloud activity}
 \credit{\S3, \S6, Eqs. (58), (132)--(133). $\mathcal K$, $\mathcal D$: kinetic energy and dissipation. $\MB$: mass-flux spectrum (slide 4).}
\notetext{The cloud work function is the buoyancy integral weighted by normalized mass flux. It measures kinetic-energy generation per unit mass flux at the reference level, as the kinetic-energy budget reads $d\mathcal K/dt=A\MB-\mathcal D$, with $\mathcal K$ the kinetic energy and $\mathcal D$ dissipation. It is not the kinetic energy already stored in the clouds. Its units are energy per mass, for example J kg$^{-1}$.\par\smallskip The integral is Eq. (133). The buoyancy term uses the virtual static energy $s_v=s+c_p\bar T(\delta q-l)$ with $\delta=0.608$ (Eq. 58). Water vapour lightens the air and liquid water adds weight. The subscript $c$ denotes the cloud and the bar denotes the environment. The normalized mass flux $\eta$ weights the contribution of each height. The paper's later algebra (Eqs. 134--139) temporarily drops the virtual corrections and writes buoyancy through $h_c-\bar h^*$, the gap seen in Fig. 6 on slide 3.\par\smallskip The right panel is a schematic, not data. It plots the buoyancy against height for two cloud types: the shaded area is what $A$ adds up, with each height weighted by $\eta$. A larger $\lambda$ dilutes the plume faster, so its buoyancy is weaker and vanishes lower, as in Fig. 6. The kinetic-energy budget of Eq. (132) explains the name: buoyancy generates kinetic energy in type $\lambda$ at the rate $A\MB$, so $A$ is the kinetic energy produced per unit mass flux.\par\smallskip The plume model determines buoyancy and the cloud top for a given environment, including the mixed layer. Thus $A(\lambda)$ is a property of that environment for each type. Positive $A$ permits buoyant energy production, but production also requires cloud activity, $\MB>0$, and triggering. For a non-entraining cloud, $\lambda=0$, the integral is close to modern CAPE. Entraining clouds also depend on environmental moisture. Source: Sections 3 and 6, Equations (58)--(59), (132)--(139).}

\notetext{The tendency equation separates the cloud response, $\int_0^{\lambda_{\max}}K(\lambda,\lambda')\MB(\lambda')\,d\lambda'$, from the large-scale forcing $F$. The prime labels the cloud type producing the response, while unprimed $\lambda$ labels the work function affected. $F$ includes large-scale ascent and advection, radiation and surface fluxes through both the cloud layer and mixed layer.\par\smallskip The diagram reads Eq. (142) as a sum of two signed rates, forcing and cloud response, giving the actual tendency $dA/dt$. The cloud term carries its sign through $K$. It is a balance of tendencies, not a sequence of causal steps.\par\smallskip The kernel (Eq. 141): $K(\lambda,\lambda')\MB(\lambda')d\lambda'$ is the rate at which type-$\lambda'$ clouds change $A(\lambda)$ by modifying the environment. Each type changes the air that all other types rise through, so each work function feels all types. The paper splits it into three parts (Eq. 144). The vertical mass-flux kernel $K_V$ is dominated by the subsidence that type $\lambda'$ forces between the clouds: it warms the environment, so type $\lambda$ is less buoyant and $A(\lambda)$ falls. Neglecting smaller effects, $K_V$ is symmetric (Fig. 11). The detrainment kernel $K_D$ is zero for $\lambda'<\lambda$, because deeper clouds detrain above the top of type $\lambda$, and nearly always positive for $\lambda'>\lambda$: shallower clouds detrain saturated air and liquid water whose evaporation cools and moistens the layer that deeper clouds cross, raising their buoyancy (Fig. 12). The mixed-layer kernel $K_M$ carries the effect of the cloud-base mass flux on the mixed-layer depth $z_B$. The paper calls $K$ typically negative: the net effect of clouds is to use up $A$.\par\smallskip With the timescale separation of slide 7, $dA/dt$ is small compared with the cloud and forcing terms. Dropping it gives the integral equation for the unknown spectrum. The equality applies to active types with $\MB>0$. Absent types satisfy the paper's inequality, and the full spectrum is nonnegative (Eq. 158). Temperature and moisture can still evolve.\par\smallskip Cloud number, lifetime and single-cloud mass transport are not needed separately: only their combination $\MB$ enters (Eq. 145). The closure, a Fredholm integral equation of the first kind, is the main result of Part I; its solution is deferred to Part II. Source: Sections 6--7, Equations (141)--(145), (146)--(150), (158), Figures 11--12, and Section 8.}

\notetext{$\tau_{\rm adj}$ is the response time of the whole cloud ensemble: with negligible forcing, the clouds alter their environment until $A$ reaches zero. $\tau_{\rm LS}$ is the time over which the large-scale forcing $F$ changes substantially. Neither is an individual cloud lifetime.\par\smallskip Neither time has a formal definition. The paper introduces both in words in Section 7 and uses them only as orders of magnitude. $\tau_{\rm adj}$ is estimated, not derived: with cloud excess temperatures of a few degrees, $A\sim(10^{-1}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})H_c$ (Eq. 152), with $H_c$ the cloud depth; subsidence warming gives $[dA/dt]_C\sim(10^{-4}$--$10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-3}})H_c$ (Eq. 153); their ratio is $\tau_{\rm adj}\sim10^3$--$10^4$ s (Eq. 154). $\tau_{\rm LS}\gtrsim10^5$ s is stated as typical, without derivation.\par\smallskip $dA/dt$ is the net change, cloud plus large-scale terms. The paper estimates $|[dA/dt]_C|\sim A/\tau_{\rm adj}$ (Eq. 146), while $|dA/dt|\lesssim A/\tau_{\rm LS}$ (Eq. 147). Thus the net tendency is smaller than the cloud term by a factor of order $\tau_{\rm adj}/\tau_{\rm LS}\ll1$ (Eq. 148). The reasoning in words: left alone, the clouds would remove all of $A$ within $\tau_{\rm adj}$, so the cloud term alone is of size $A/\tau_{\rm adj}$. But in quasi-equilibrium $A$ follows the current forcing, which changes only over $\tau_{\rm LS}$, so $A$ itself changes at the much smaller rate $A/\tau_{\rm LS}$. Since $dA/dt$ is the sum of the cloud term and $F$, a sum much smaller than either term means the two nearly cancel. Writing $dA/dt\simeq0$ drops this residual; $A$, temperature and moisture may still evolve slowly.\par\smallskip Figure 13 (Marshall Islands data of Yanai et al. 1973, $\lambda=8\%$ km$^{-1}$; axes in cal g$^{-1}$ day$^{-1}$, 1 cal g$^{-1}\approx4.2$ kJ kg$^{-1}$) plots the observed $dA/dt$ against the cloud-layer forcing $F_C$. The dashed line $dA/dt=F_C$ is what forcing alone would give. The observed tendency stays near zero while $F_C$ reaches about 1.5 cal g$^{-1}$ day$^{-1}$; the panel for $\lambda=16\%$ km$^{-1}$ agrees. This supports quasi-equilibrium in this tropical setting, as a partial test: the mixed-layer forcing $F_M$, part of $F=F_C+F_M$, could not be computed from conventional data. Figure 14 shows temperature and moisture evolving while $A$ stays small. Source: Section 7, Equations (146)--(154), Figures 13--14, and Section 8.}
