\notetext{Figure 4a shows the normalized size density in logarithmic bins. Below the scale break, the three cases have approximately the same slope. The fitted log-bin slope is $-0.70$, corresponding to the exponent $b=-1.70$ of $\mathcal N(l)\propto l^b$ because $\mathcal N^*=l\ln(10)\mathcal N$. The supported range spans roughly one decade between the grid scale and the break.\par\smallskip Above $\ell_d$, the density decreases faster. Table 2 gives 700 m for BOMEX and 1050 m for SCMS. During the ARM afternoon, the break increases from 400 to 1250 m. Increasing the number of sampled clouds does not remove the break. Its physical origin remains unresolved. It is a cloud-size scale, not an inter-cloud distance. Source: Section 4a, Figure 4a, Table 2, Equations (8)--(9).}

% 3: collapse and area weighting --------------------------------------
\begin{frame}[plain]\frametitle{The size break sets the dominant cloud cover}\startslide{The size break sets the dominant cloud cover}
 \txt[text=pisablue]{7}{18}{73}{Rescaling by $\ell_d$: collapse}
 \txt[font=\fontsize{12.5}{15}\selectfont]{87}{17}{68}{$\alpha^p(l)=\dfrac{l^2\mathcal N(l)}{S}$}
 \figat{13}{28}{63}{45}{\figdir/fig6.png}
 \figat{93}{28}{61}{45}{\figdir/fig7a.png}
 \bottomline{Area weighting gives $\alpha^p\propto l^{\,b+2}$: the cover peaks near $\ell_d$}
 \credit{\S3--4b, Figs. 6 and 7a, Eqs. (4), (10). $S=L_xL_y$: domain area. $\alpha^p(l)$: projected-cover contribution per unit size.}
\notetext{Left: Figure 6 plots the three size densities against $l/\ell_d$. Their approximate collapse supports a common functional form for these simulated cumulus populations, with a case-dependent break. The scaling range and three tested cases limit this claim.\par\smallskip Right: Figure 7a gives the contribution to projected cloud cover per unit size. Each cloud contributes its projected area $l^2$, so $\alpha^p(l)=l^2\mathcal N(l)/S$, where $S$ is the horizontal domain area. For a power law with exponent $b$, the cover decomposition scales as $l^{b+2}$. Below the break, $b=-1.70$ gives a positive exponent, $0.30$. Above the break the density declines sufficiently rapidly that the cover decomposition decreases. The peak therefore lies at an intermediate size near the break.\par\smallskip This is a decomposition per unit linear size, not per logarithmic bin. The total projected cover is $a^p=\int_0^\infty\alpha^p(l)\,dl$. Source: Section 3, Section 4a--b, Figures 6 and 7a, Equations (3)--(4) and (10).}
\finishslide\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[plain]\frametitle{Larger cloud velocities shift the transport peak}\startslide{Larger cloud velocities shift the transport peak}
 \head{7}{17}{67}{Mean vertical velocity}
 \head{86}{17}{67}{Mass-flux contribution}
 \figat{10}{25}{64}{38}{\figdir/fig7b.png}
 \figat{91}{25}{63}{38}{\figdir/fig7c.png}
 \txt{7}{65}{60}{Small clouds carry little flux.}
 \txt[font=\fontsize{12.5}{15}\selectfont]{79}{65}{75}{$\mu(l)=\overline{\alpha(l,z)\,w(l,z)}$}
 \bottomline{Mass flux peaks at larger cloud sizes than projected cover}
 \credit{\S3--4b, Fig. 7b--c, Eqs. (5)--(7). $\alpha(l,z)$: cloud-fraction contribution. The bar averages over the cloud layer.}
\notetext{Figure 7b shows that cloud-mean vertical velocity generally increases with cloud size. Figure 7c shows the resulting mass-flux decomposition. At height $z$, $\mu(l,z)=\alpha(l,z)w(l,z)$, where $\alpha(l,z)$ is the cloud-fraction contribution per unit size. Averaging over the cloud-layer depth $h_c$ gives $\mu(l)=h_c^{-1}\int\alpha(l,z)w(l,z)\,dz$. Its integral over size is the total kinematic mass flux, in m/s under this paper's convention.\par\smallskip Cloud size is always the projected size, even when evaluating the flux at a specific height. Since $w$ increases with size, the mass-flux maximum occurs at somewhat larger cloud sizes than the projected-cover maximum. The smallest clouds contribute very little to total vertical transport.\par\smallskip The intermediate dominant size persists for tested grid spacings of 25--100 m. A 3.2 km domain truncates the largest clouds. Increased shear broadens projected sizes while leaving total mass flux almost unchanged. These sensitivity tests support the main transport result but do not identify the mechanism of the size break. Source: Section 3, Section 4b--e, Figure 7b--c and Figures 9--11.}
\finishslide\end{frame}

\notetext{Our reading sought distributions of thermals in a horizontal plane: their size at a fixed height, their positions and their separations. The diagram only defines these three quantities. Its circles, radii and separation are schematic, not data or a spatial model.\par\smallskip Neggers et al. report projected cloud sizes and cloud transport statistics. A cloud is a saturated object, whereas a thermal can be unsaturated and can feed a cloud without sharing its projected footprint. Projection also combines geometry over height and responds to tilt and shear. The paper does not establish a cloud-to-thermal size mapping and does not report thermal centres or inter-thermal distances. The exponent $-1.70$ and the break $\ell_d$ therefore remain results about the measured cumulus population. Source: Sections 3--5 and Appendix.}
