\section{Overview}

\begin{frame}{Two kinds of satellites}
\vfill

\column{.46\textwidth}

{\large A film of the whole day}\par\bigskip
An image every 2.5--15\,min\par

\column{.46\textwidth}
{\Large\textcolor{sevrss}{\textbf{Polar orbiting}}}\par\medskip
{\large A sharp photo, once or twice a day}\par\bigskip
Pixels of 10\,m--1\,km\par
Sentinel-2, Landsat, MODIS, VIIRS
\end{columns}
\vfill
\takeaway{Geostationary images follow the clouds through the day. A polar image, on the few days it exists, shows the single cumuli that the coarser pixels merge.}
\speaker{Geostationary: Meteosat SEVIRI and MTG FCI, fixed above the equator, imaging Europe every few minutes. Polar orbiting satellites pass at 700--800 km and see a given place once or twice a day: Sentinel-2 (10--60 m, every 2--5 days, about 10:30), Landsat 8 and 9 (30 m, every 8 days), MODIS (250 m--1 km, about 10:30 and 13:30), VIIRS (375 m, about 13:30). Sentinel-2 resolves single cumuli and is the best validation source. Other SEVIRI-based options: CM SAF CLAAS-3, a climate-quality cloud record every 15 minutes from 2004; the NWC SAF software of Meteo-France, which needs the full image files.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One pixel, two answers: image or cloud mask}
\vfill
\begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
\column{.28\textwidth}\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1mm,y=1mm]
 \fill[black!62](0,0)rectangle(24,40);
 \fill[white](15,27)circle(4.6);\fill[white](19.5,28.5)circle(3.8);\fill[white](11,29)circle(3.4);\fill[white](15.5,31.5)circle(3.8);
 \draw[pisablue,line width=.8pt](0,0)rectangle(24,40);
 \node[below,font=\footnotesize,align=center]at(12,-1){a $3\times5$\,km pixel,\\15\% cumulus};
\end{tikzpicture}
\column{.68\textwidth}
{\large\textcolor{sevrss}{\textbf{Image}}}\quad $0.85\times10\%+0.15\times70\%=\mathbf{19\%}$\par\smallskip
Brighter than clear ground (10\%): the cloud is still there.\par\bigskip
{\large\textcolor{feasible}{\textbf{Cloud mask}}}\quad cloud or clear, nothing in between\par\smallskip
Mostly ``cloud'' for all 15\,km$^2$; ``clear'' for the smallest cumuli.
\end{columns}
\vfill
\takeaway{We want the images, the HRV above all: the mask throws away how much cloud there is, and where.}
\source{Illustrative reflectances. Mask behaviour: Bley and Deneke (2013).}
\speaker{A pixel that a cloud covers only in part is a mixed pixel: its brightness is a weighted average of cloud and ground, so a small cumulus raises it from 10 to about 19 percent. The cloud mask is a product computed by EUMETSAT from the 3 km channels only, without HRV; it gives one class per pixel: cloud, clear land, clear water. Bley and Deneke compared it with the 1 km HRV channel: most partly cloudy 3 km pixels are classified cloudy, which overestimates cloud cover, while small convective clouds below the pixel size are missed. HRV, one of SEVIRI's 12 channels in the same images, gives about 70 brightness values per 10 km cell instead of about 13 yes or no answers, and the same cumulus fills more of a smaller pixel. The 10 and 70 percent reflectances are illustrative; EUMETSAT combines tests on several channels rather than one threshold.}
\end{frame}

\section{Limits}

\begin{frame}{Parallax: clouds appear north of where they are}
\vfill
\begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
\column{.52\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=.85mm,y=.85mm,font=\footnotesize]

\end{tikzpicture}
\column{.44\textwidth}
\large
\[\text{shift}=h\,\tan\theta\approx1.2\text{--}1.5\times h\]
{\small $\theta$: angle of the line of sight from vertical,\\ 50--57$^\circ$ over France}\par\medskip
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
Cloud at 2.5\,km & 3--4\,km north\\
Cloud at 4\,km & 5--6\,km north\\
\end{tabular}
\end{columns}
\vfill
\takeaway{A cloud above a 10\,km cell can show up in the next cell to the north.}
\speaker{How theta is found, treating the Earth as a sphere (R = 6371 km; satellite at Rgeo = 42164 km from the centre, above the equator at longitude ls: 9.5 E for the SEVIRI rapid scan, 0 for MTG). (1) The angle c at the Earth's centre between the place (latitude phi, longitude l) and the point below the satellite: cos c = cos(phi) cos(l - ls). (2) The distance place--satellite: d squared = R squared + Rgeo squared - 2 R Rgeo cos c. (3) The angle from the local vertical: sin(theta) = (Rgeo / d) sin c. Example near Annecy (45.3 N, 5.9 E, SEVIRI): c = 45.4 degrees, d = 37960 km, theta = 52.3 degrees, tan(theta) = 1.29. Normandy (49 N, 0.45 W): theta = 57 degrees, tan 1.54, being further from the point below the satellite. Why theta is close to, but larger than, the latitude: in the triangle centre--place--satellite, theta = c + beta (exterior angle), where c is roughly the latitude (slightly more, because France is west of the satellite) and beta, about 7 degrees, is the angle under which the satellite sees the Earth's radius; beta would vanish only for a satellite infinitely far away. Annecy: c = 45.4, beta = 6.9, theta = 52.3 degrees; Normandy: c = 49.7, beta = 7.3, theta = 57.0 degrees. Seen from France, Meteosat stands 90 - theta, about 33--40 degrees, above the southern horizon. The shifts in km were computed with pyproj on the WGS84 ellipsoid, tracing the line from the satellite through the cloud to the ground; the angles agree within a fraction of a degree. The satellite assumes everything sits at sea level. A cloud at height h is seen along a slanted line, about 52 degrees from vertical over France, and appears further from the satellite, so north. Even 2000 m terrain appears about 2.5 km off. For our cells, at the highest climb in each: 3--3.7 km in the plains and hills, 4--5 km in the Alps and the Pyrenees. Height maps exist (SEVIRI cloud-top height every 15 minutes since 2005; MTG cloud-top height every 10 minutes since December 2025), but they come from infrared temperature, and EUMETSAT states for MTG: fractional clouds are excluded, because they are contaminated by radiation emitted by the underlying surface. Fair-weather cumuli are that case; the highest altitude reached in climbs is a better estimate of cloud base. satpy provides the per-pixel correction when a height map is available.}
\end{frame}

\vfill
\takeaway{The margin costs a little more data; the correction is one function call, and can change later without downloading again.}
\source{satpy.modifiers.parallax: get\_parallax\_corrected\_lonlats takes the satellite position, the pixel positions and a cloud height.}
\speaker{Clouds above the cell can appear up to about 6 km further north, so the crop is 12 by 17 km instead of 10 by 10: this is the north margin in our configuration (half size 5 km, north margin 7 km, side margin 1 km). The shift points almost due north for both satellites, slightly west for SEVIRI and slightly east for MTG, hence the 1 km side margins. The correction needs the cloud height h above sea level. Satellite cloud-top height maps exist, but they come from infrared temperature and EUMETSAT excludes fractional clouds from the MTG product; fair-weather cumuli are that case. The highest altitude reached in the climbs of that day and cell is close to cloud base. satpy's get\_parallax\_corrected\_lonlats accepts a single height as well as a map; its ParallaxCorrection class instead needs a full cloud-top height field. The stored data stay uncorrected, so the choice of height can change later.}
\end{frame}
