\notetext{A flight is sustained by an energy budget: the pilot trades altitude for horizontal distance, searches for rising air, and recovers altitude in lift. These are useful behavioural descriptions, not phase labels supplied by the recorder or results of a segmentation shown here. Ground motion includes both air-relative motion and wind. \notetext{GNSS supplies one altitude channel throughout, with an absolute-height reference that needs no pressure or temperature calibration. In supported paired records, high-frequency median spectra are comparable to barometry. This does not establish uniformly better absolute accuracy; logged vertical datums remain unaudited. Barometry corroborates cleaning decisions without replacing the output altitude.\par\medskip A fix records time, position and altitude at one instant. In the example, A is at 0 m at 0 s, B at 800 m at 1 s, and C at 10 m at 2 s. Reaching B from A requires an impossible 800 m/s. Removing B leaves 10 m in 2 s: 5 m/s. Deletion therefore passes both the impossibility and rejoin tests. The dashed chord tests reachability; it is not interpolation. The pipeline handles contiguous blocks similarly. Without a timely reachable rejoin, it splits instead of deleting. A Hampel flag alone never deletes a fix; timestamp, frozen-lock and altitude defects have separate rules.\par\medskip The timeline distinguishes take-off, intermediate landing, relaunch and final landing. Grey ground phases are removed; blue pieces remain segments of one parent flight. Outer boundaries use sustained speed. Interior stops also require eligible barometry supporting stable altitude: slow motion alone is insufficient. An excised stop becomes a boundary that later processing never bridges. Sources: thesis Sections 2.7--2.8 and cleaning ADRs.}

 \credit{Schematic vectors: blue at $t$, purple at $t+\tau$. Stationary normalisation; headings require nonzero speed. No empirical estimates.}
\notetext{Write horizontal velocity as speed times a unit heading. The dot product is the product of the two speeds multiplied by the cosine of their angular difference. Averaging and dividing by mean squared speed gives the displayed normalised velocity autocorrelation under stationary sampling. Aligned directions contribute positively, perpendicular directions give zero and reversals contribute negatively. Speed products weight those contributions, so velocity autocorrelation contains directional information but does not isolate it.\par\medskip Correlating unit vectors removes speed weights: $C_\theta=\langle\cos\Delta\theta\rangle$. At constant speed, $C_v=C_\theta$. With varying speed they generally differ; speed and angular averages cannot be factorised without additional assumptions. Headings require nonzero speed.\par\medskip Mean drift, including wind, can leave a positive long-lag baseline without memory in the fluctuations. The centred autocovariance is $K_v(\tau)=\langle\boldsymbol v(t)\cdot\boldsymbol v(t+\tau)\rangle-|\langle\boldsymbol v\rangle|^2$. It describes fluctuations around drift, whereas unit-vector correlation describes the original headings. State which convention is used.\par\medskip For stationary velocities, writing $R_v(\tau)=\langle\boldsymbol v(t)\cdot\boldsymbol v(t+\tau)\rangle$ gives $M_2(\tau)=2\int_0^\tau(\tau-u)R_v(u)\,du$. This connects velocity dependence to displacement growth, but a fitted H does not specify that dependence uniquely. Use physical-time lags, within-segment pairs and consistent weighting. This slide derives identities; it does not report measured correlations.}

\notetext{The reported confidence intervals use the current site--day cluster bootstrap. Each group combines flights from the same declared take-off site and calendar day. We sample groups with replacement, preserve all flights belonging to a sampled group, recompute the equal-flight estimator, and repeat this one thousand times. The same multiplicities are paired across lags and conditional comparisons. The 5th and 95th percentiles form nominal 90 percent intervals; for a curve they are pointwise, not a simultaneous confidence band.\par\medskip Clustering aims to keep dependent flights together and make different groups approximately independent. The current partition is too fine: nearby sites and neighbouring days can share weather and other conditions, so flights in different groups can still be dependent. In the current cohort, 71.1 percent of paraglider groups and 81.3 percent of hang-glider groups are singletons. The bands are consequently likely to understate uncertainty; their nominal level should not be read as demonstrated coverage.\par\medskip Validation with 50-km cells crossed with initial-altitude class and month of year gives standard errors for fitted H roughly four times the site--day value for paragliders and twice for hang gliders. Further aggregation changes the errors again; independence and uncertainty have not stabilised. These are diagnostic comparisons, not a validated replacement partition, and other chapter bands have not been recomputed with that scheme. All intervals in this presentation retain the original bootstrap convention. Sources: thesis Section 3.1.2; ch3\_transport\_grid\_bootstrap.json and its generated values.}
