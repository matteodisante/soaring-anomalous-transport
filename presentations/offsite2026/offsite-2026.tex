\notetext{Paragliders and hang gliders have no engine. A cross-country flight repeats three phases. In transition the pilot glides, trading height for horizontal distance. In search the pilot looks for rising air. In climb the pilot circles inside a thermal, a column of air rising where the sun has heated the ground, and regains the height spent. Repeating the cycle, with thermals as the only source of height, is what lets a pilot cover long distances without an engine.\par\medskip The illustration is the cover image: a glide, a short search and a helical climb in a thermal over a sun-warmed slope. The schematic is illustrative and has no numerical scale. These are behavioural descriptions, not phase labels supplied by the recorder; Vilpellet et al.\ provide a hidden Markov model that labels each fix as transition, search or climb from binary trajectory features, and slide \slideref{wip} returns to it. Ground motion includes both air-relative motion and wind. Source: thesis introduction, Sections 1.1--1.3.}

\begin{frame}[plain]\frametitle{How do we study the statistics of this motion?}\startslide{How do we study the statistics of this motion?}
 % The answer to the title question: the time-averaged MSD, along each flight, then over flights.
 \txt[text=pisablue,font=\bfseries\fontsize{12}{14}\selectfont]{8}{16}{72}{Mean-square displacement (MSD)}
 \txt[font=\fontsize{9}{11}\selectfont]{8}{26}{78}{%
  \stepitem{1}{Along each flight $f$, of duration $T_f$\par\vspace{.6mm}
   {\fontsize{10.5}{12.5}\selectfont\mbox{$\displaystyle m_f(\tau)=\frac{1}{T_f-\tau}\int_0^{T_f-\tau}\bigl|\boldsymbol{x}_f(t+\tau)-\boldsymbol{x}_f(t)\bigr|^2\,dt$}}}\vspace{7.5mm}
  \stepitem{2}{Over the $N$ flights:\quad{\fontsize{11}{13}\selectfont$M_2(\tau)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_f m_f(\tau)$}}}
 \txt[text=pisablue,font=\fontsize{10}{12}\selectfont]{8}{62.5}{78}{$M_2(\tau)\propto\tau^{2H}$:\; $H=\tfrac12$ diffusive,\; $H=1$ ballistic}
 % Reproduced figure: short label above it, full reference in the footer.
 \smalltxt[text=pisablue,font=\fontsize{9}{11}\selectfont]{86}{16}{67}{Vilpellet et al.: French flights, 2016--2021}
 \node[anchor=north,inner sep=0pt]at(119.5,20){\includegraphics[height=54mm]{assets/vilpellet2026-fig1-msd.png}};
 \bottomline{$M_2(\tau)\propto\tau^{2H}$ with $H\approx0.88$: superdiffusive over three decades, $10^1$--$10^4$\,s}
 \credit{J.\ Vilpellet, A.\ Darmon and M.\ Benzaquen, \emph{From Random Walks to Thermal Rides: Universal Anomalous Transport in Soaring Flights}, \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01293}{arXiv:2601.01293} (2026).}
\notetext{Vilpellet, Darmon and Benzaquen analysed publicly available French tracks from 2016 to 2021: CFD--FFVL for paragliders and hang gliders, NetCoupe for sailplanes. After requiring at least 1 Hz sampling, one hour of flight, no discontinuities and a 15 km horizontal range, 49,939 paraglider, 780 hang-glider and 9,203 sailplane trajectories remain. The paper states no terrain or regional selection. Their Figure 1 shows the horizontal MSD. The paper writes it as an expectation, $\delta^2(\Delta)=\mathbb E[(\boldsymbol x_{t+\Delta}-\boldsymbol x_t)^2]$, under a stationarity assumption; it is estimated as on the left: a time average along each flight, then an average over flights. The slide uses the notation of the rest of the talk: $\tau$ for the lag, $m_f$ per flight and $M_2$ over flights. Slide \slideref{fixed} applies the same estimator within the retained segments of each flight, and the exponent $H$ of the bottom line is the one compared there. Below about 10 s the growth is ballistic, $\delta^2\sim\Delta^2$. At longer lags it is sub-ballistic but faster than diffusion, $\delta^2\sim\Delta^{2H}$ with $\tfrac12<H\approx0.88<1$. The three curves differ mainly by a vertical shift set by their characteristic speeds, and the speed-rescaled curves in the inset nearly collapse. They attribute the exponent to the intermittent, two-dimensional and directionally correlated transition--search--climb cycle. They also provide a hidden Markov model that labels each fix as transition, search or climb from binary trajectory features; slide \slideref{wip} returns to it.\par\medskip The challenge is to explain whole-flight transport while respecting the energy budget of the previous slide. The present measurements are the first layer of that programme. An MSD exponent alone cannot distinguish all the candidate mechanisms: persistent motion, changes of behaviour and heterogeneous conditions can produce similar second moments. Source: thesis introduction, Sections 1.1--1.3.}
\finishslide\end{frame}

There the curve steepens: around 6,600 seconds the local slope of the all-flight curve gives H near 0.90 for paragliders, whereas the fixed cohort of the next slide gives about 0.70 at the same lag. Same estimator, different flights: the steepening comes from which flights are averaged, not only from how they move. An H fitted to the all-flight curve therefore mixes two effects, and we cannot say which one drives which feature. The same ambiguity affects any lag-dependent statistic, that is, any statistic computed as a function of $\tau$: local slopes, displacement quantiles and kurtosis, velocity autocorrelation.\par\medskip We therefore want one population with three properties: the same flights at every lag, the longest possible lag range, and a number of flights of the same order as at ten seconds, so that the statistics still rest on a large sample. The next slide gives our choice and what it costs. Below ten seconds, support depends on each logger's native cadence; slide \slideref{fixed} treats that range. Source: thesis Section 3.1.2, Figure 3.1 and Table 3.2; ch3\_transport\_report.json and cadence-support-report.json.}
\finishslide\end{frame}

\notetext{The reported confidence intervals use the current site--day cluster bootstrap. Each group combines flights from the same declared take-off site and calendar day. We sample groups with replacement, preserve all flights belonging to a sampled group, recompute the equal-flight estimator, and repeat this one thousand times. The same multiplicities are paired across lags and conditional comparisons. The 5th and 95th percentiles form nominal 90 percent intervals; for a curve they are pointwise, not a simultaneous confidence band.\par\medskip Clustering aims to keep dependent flights together and make different groups approximately independent. The current partition is too fine: nearby sites and neighbouring days can share weather and other conditions, so flights in different groups can still be dependent.
