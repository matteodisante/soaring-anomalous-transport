\section{Time-averaged transport by region and initial altitude}
\label{sec:grouped-tamsd}

Before examining directional structure, we ask whether the magnitude of horizontal
transport differs across the three take-off regions and four initial-altitude bands.
These are two separate groupings of the same eligible archive. The regional comparison
uses the Alps, Pyrenees and Channel Coast boxes. The altitude comparison uses every
eligible flight with a finite initial altitude, including flights outside those boxes.
It is therefore not a comparison of four altitude groups within one common region.

The altitude bands retain the names introduced in the dataset chapter:
Plains below \SI{300}{\metre}, Hills from 300 to below \SI{800}{\metre},
Low mountains from 800 to below \SI{1500}{\metre}, and High mountains from
\SI{1500}{\metre} upward. Here the grouping variable is the cleaned trajectory's
GNSS origin altitude, \texttt{alt0}. The earlier exploratory map used the first raw
altitude and allowed a pressure fallback; the present transport grouping uses neither
that fallback nor the instantaneous altitude along the route. These historical labels
do not measure local slope, relief or terrain exposure. A record beginning airborne
can enter a high-altitude band over a low launch region.

\subsection{Flight weighting and a fixed-segment control}

The measurement reuses the identified native-grid segment TA-MSDs for all
\num{\StatGroupedParaFlights} paraglider and \num{\StatGroupedHangFlights}
hang-glider flights with eligible segments. Segments contain at least eight fixes
and have native cadence at most \SI{10}{\second}. At each requested lag, the
within-flight average pools all supported displacement origins, and the group
average gives each contributing flight equal weight:
\begin{equation}
 m_f(\tau)=\frac{\sum_s n_{fs}(\tau)m_{fs}(\tau)}{\sum_s n_{fs}(\tau)},\qquad
 M_g(\tau)=\frac{1}{N_g(\tau)}\sum_{f\in g:\,n_f(\tau)>0}m_f(\tau).
 \label{eq:grouped-flight-tamsd}
\end{equation}
This differs from the equal-segment archive average introduced at the beginning
of the chapter. No displacement crosses a segment boundary. For $N_{fs}$ fixes
at interval $\Delta t_{fs}$, the requested lag is rounded to
$k=\operatorname{round}(\tau/\Delta t_{fs})$ native steps and
$n_{fs}=N_{fs}-k$. Stored support requires $1\le k\le\lfloor N_{fs}/2\rfloor$
and $\tau\ge\Delta t_{fs}$. The 59 requested lags span 10--10,000~s; the nearest
one to 1000~s is \SI{\StatGroupedReferenceLagS}{\second}. This grid must not be
relabeled as the exact decade grid used later for PCA and regional differences.

The main curve uses every segment where it supports the lag. A second calculation
keeps only segments supporting the maximum requested lag, \SI{10000}{\second},
and uses this same segment and flight set throughout. It fixes membership and
between-flight weights while the available origins and the segment weights within
a flight still change with lag. It is conditional on long continuous records,
not a replacement for the archive-wide comparison. The native rounding rule can
admit slightly different boundary cases from the exact common-grid controls.

Five hundred complete site--day bootstrap draws are paired across lags and the
two controls within each group. Shading gives pointwise 95\% percentile intervals
for the available-segment curve; at least 20 contributing groups and 90\% finite
replicates are required. Three paraglider flights have missing cluster keys and
become singleton groups. No missing origin-altitude labels remain in this eligible
population. The bands neither match weather or pilots nor calibrate simultaneous
between-group tests. Displayed curves require at least eight flights, and their
support is shown explicitly.

\subsection{Regional contrasts and changing population}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=.94\linewidth]{generated/ch3_tamsd_regions}
 \caption{Horizontal flight TA-MSD by take-off region, separately for the two
 disciplines. Top: squared-displacement mean. Middle: its square root divided
 by the requested lag, the RMS magnitude of the interval-mean ground velocity
 up to native-grid rounding. Bottom: contributing flights. Solid curves use
 available segments; dashed curves retain the same long segments over the full
 lag range. Shading is the pointwise site--day interval for the solid curve.
 Fixed-segment hang-glider curves for the Pyrenees and coast are omitted because
 each contains only two flights; their support remains shown.}
 \label{fig:tamsd-regions}
\end{figure}

At \SI{\StatGroupedReferenceLagS}{\second}, the paraglider RMS displacements
$\sqrt{M_g}$ are \SI{\StatGroupedParaAlpsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre} in the Alps,
\SI{\StatGroupedParaPyreneesRmsKm}{\kilo\metre} in the Pyrenees and
\SI{\StatGroupedParaChannelCoastRmsKm}{\kilo\metre} on the coast
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tamsd-regions}). With fixed long segments, they become
\SI{\StatGroupedParaAlpsRmsKmFixed}{\kilo\metre},
\SI{\StatGroupedParaPyreneesRmsKmFixed}{\kilo\metre} and
\SI{\StatGroupedParaChannelCoastRmsKmFixed}{\kilo\metre}.
The coastal ordering survives this control, while the Alpine--Pyrenean separation
becomes smaller. Long-flight selection also raises the amplitude within each region;
the conditional long-flight population already travels farther at intermediate lags.

The middle panels make a change of scaling easier to see than parallel-looking
log--log curves. A constant $\sqrt{M_g}/\tau$ would correspond to quadratic growth
of this moment. The available coastal curve turns upward at long lag. Its
descriptive 1000--10,000-s log-slope is
\num{\StatGroupedParaChannelCoastSlopeLong}; retaining fixed segments gives
\num{\StatGroupedParaChannelCoastSlopeLongFixed}. The apparent excess over
ballistic growth therefore does not persist in that fitted fixed-population
comparison. The support loss is part of the explanation, rather than evidence
for an intrinsic superballistic process. These fitted slopes describe separate
ranges of a bending curve; they are not Hurst estimates or calibrated hypothesis tests.

Hang gliders show the same regional amplitude ordering near the reference lag:
\SI{\StatGroupedHangAlpsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre},
\SI{\StatGroupedHangPyreneesRmsKm}{\kilo\metre} and
\SI{\StatGroupedHangChannelCoastRmsKm}{\kilo\metre}.
However, the coastal mean contains only
\num{\StatGroupedHangChannelCoastFlights} flights there. The two-flight
long-segment controls in both coastal and Pyrenean groups cannot establish the
regional ordering in the long-flight population.

\subsection{Initial-altitude bands and their regional composition}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=.94\linewidth]{generated/ch3_tamsd_altitude}
 \caption{The same TA-MSD estimators grouped by cleaned GNSS origin altitude.
 The names Plains, Hills, Low mountains and High mountains denote the stated
 altitude bands, not measured terrain classes. All eligible regions enter this
 grouping. Lines, shading and support conventions match Fig.~\ref{fig:tamsd-regions}.}
 \label{fig:tamsd-altitude}
\end{figure}

The available paraglider RMS displacements at the reference lag are
\SI{\StatGroupedParaPlainsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre},
\SI{\StatGroupedParaHillsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre},
\SI{\StatGroupedParaLowMountainsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre} and
\SI{\StatGroupedParaHighMountainsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre}, in increasing altitude-band
order. This is not monotonic: High mountains lies above Low mountains.
With fixed long segments, Plains and Hills remain above both mountain-labelled
groups, but their mutual separation becomes smaller. The apparent long-lag
growth above two in the available Plains and Hills curves is reduced from
\num{\StatGroupedParaPlainsSlopeLong} and \num{\StatGroupedParaHillsSlopeLong}
to \num{\StatGroupedParaPlainsSlopeLongFixed} and
\num{\StatGroupedParaHillsSlopeLongFixed}, respectively, in the fixed control.

For hang gliders the available reference-lag values decrease across the four
bands: \SI{\StatGroupedHangPlainsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre},
\SI{\StatGroupedHangHillsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre},
\SI{\StatGroupedHangLowMountainsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre} and
\SI{\StatGroupedHangHighMountainsRmsKm}{\kilo\metre}.
Yet the Plains/Hills ordering reverses in the fixed long-segment control.
Neither the band label nor one pooled ranking identifies an effect of altitude
on a pilot's dynamics.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/ch3_tamsd_region_altitude}
 \caption{Descriptive cross-classification at the requested
 \SI{\StatGroupedReferenceLagS}{\second} lag. Each cell gives
 $\sqrt{M_g}$ and its contributing flight count on available-segment support.
 Values with fewer than eight flights are suppressed; counts remain visible.
 The same colour scale applies to both disciplines. These cells carry no
 calibrated intervals and do not standardise task, weather or pilot identity.}
 \label{fig:tamsd-region-altitude}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:tamsd-region-altitude} shows why the two groupings are not
independent environmental controls. The coastal sample is concentrated in Plains,
while Alpine and Pyrenean records mostly occupy the two higher bands. Within the
Alpine paraglider sample, the four altitude-band amplitudes are much closer than
the corresponding pooled altitude comparison, and there is no monotonic decline.
Within the Pyrenees, the higher of the two mountain-labelled bands again has a
larger amplitude. The sparse coastal high-altitude cells cannot support a general
mountain-versus-coast interpretation. Logged origin altitude and regional membership
describe selection as well as possible environmental constraints.

The larger coastal transport amplitude can be compared qualitatively with the
smaller changes of interval-mean velocity measured later in
Sec.~\ref{sec:regional-variations}. Greater net progress with smaller velocity
changes is compatible with more persistent advancement and less turning, or an
added mean motion; it does not identify wind as the cause. The native-grid
TA-MSD and the matched-order difference diagnostic use different origins and
nearby, rather than identical, reference lags, so this is not an exact paired
decomposition. A quantitative mechanism test should align those observations and
add independent wind, route, terrain and pilot information. Directional moments
are needed next because a scalar TA-MSD does not describe the orientation of motion.
