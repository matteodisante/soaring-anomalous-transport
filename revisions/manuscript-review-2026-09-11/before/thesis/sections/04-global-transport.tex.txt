\chapter{Global transport}
\label{ch:global}

Whole flights combine circling, gliding and the search for lift, whose accumulated effect
appears in the displacement statistics. These statistics constrain any model of the motion: how far a glider moves over a
given time, how variable that distance is, which directions it favours and how long its
velocity remains correlated. We measure these properties before assigning phase labels,
then compare them with predictions of candidate stochastic processes.
Chapter~\ref{ch:phases} examines how the motion decomposes into individual behaviours.

The archive contains \num{\StatMsdParaFlights} paraglider and
\num{\StatMsdHangFlights} hang-glider flights in the retained transport snapshot. A
reproducible diagnostic subset is used for measurements spanning
\SIrange{10}{10000}{\second}, including regional principal axes and velocity correlations
at several resolutions. Subset results and archive-wide descriptions are identified
separately.

\FloatBarrier
\section{The measured displacement and its time scales}
\label{sec:estimator}
\label{sec:obs-global}

Let $\mathbf r_f(t)=(E_f(t),N_f(t))$ denote the horizontal position of flight $f$ in its
local east--north frame. A displacement is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf X_f(t,\tau)=\mathbf r_f(t+\tau)-\mathbf r_f(t),
  \qquad R_f(t,\tau)=|\mathbf X_f(t,\tau)|.
  \label{eq:displacement-global}
\end{equation}
East and north are two coordinates; east and west are opposite signs of the same
coordinate. The radial displacement $R$ combines both coordinates and is not a third
independent component.

For a continuous segment of duration $T_f$, the time-averaged MSD is
\begin{equation}
  \overline{\delta_f^2}(\tau)
    =\frac{1}{T_f-\tau}\int_0^{T_f-\tau}R_f(t,\tau)^2\,\mathrm dt.
  \label{eq:ta-msd}
\end{equation}
Only windows lying entirely within a retained segment are admitted, so an increment never
crosses a data gap. For flights with several segments, combining the segment sums and their
numbers of admissible windows gives a flight-level mean; averaging further over the
population requires a choice of weights:
\begin{equation}
  M_2^{\mathrm{flight}}(\tau)
   =\frac{1}{N_f(\tau)}\sum_f\overline{\delta_f^2}(\tau),
  \qquad
  M_2^{\mathrm{window}}(\tau)
   =\frac{\sum_f n_f(\tau)\overline{\delta_f^2}(\tau)}{\sum_f n_f(\tau)}.
  \label{eq:weights-global}
\end{equation}
The first gives each contributing flight equal weight, whereas the second gives each
admissible window equal weight and consequently weights longer flights more strongly.
Both averages still combine flights made under different meteorological and geographical
conditions.

The archive time average in Figure~\ref{fig:msd} uses a third convention: it gives each
contributing \emph{segment} equal weight. A flight split into several segments can
therefore contribute several times. Within a segment, all admissible starting fixes enter
the mean. The diagnostic subset used later keeps one segment per flight and uses starting
times $0,\tau,2\tau,\ldots$; its first-difference windows do not overlap. Its $V_1$ and
$V_2$ curves give each flight equal weight, whereas the baseline quantiles and higher moments pool
windows. Section~\ref{sec:quantile-population} adds quantile controls with fixed flights,
weights and origins. In discrete time, a segment of $n$ fixes at interval $\Delta t$ contributes
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\delta^2}(k\Delta t)
 =\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k-1}|\mathbf r_{j+k}-\mathbf r_j|^2,
 \qquad 1\le k<n.
 \label{eq:discrete-tamsd}
\end{equation}
The subset is evaluated on a \SI{10}{\second} grid; flights whose native interval
exceeds that value are excluded. Each admitted flight contributes its longest retained
segment. Its sampling design is therefore distinct from a uniform draw of all flights.

For completeness, Figure~\ref{fig:msd}a retains the launch-synchronised average
$\mathrm{MSD}_{\mathrm{launch}}(t)=\langle|\mathbf r_f(t)-\mathbf r_f(0)|^2\rangle_f$.
Its difference from the time average is unsurprising: these flights occur in different
locations, seasons and years, and are not established to be independent realisations of
one common law. It is a descriptive archive average, without a process exponent inferred
from it.

\subsection{Growth over ten seconds to ten thousand seconds}
\label{sec:exponent}
\label{sec:transport-measure}

The comparison interval is \SIrange{10}{10000}{\second}. For a positive observable
$Y(\tau)$ we distinguish its fitted slope and its local slope,
\begin{equation}
  \log Y(\tau)\simeq a+b\log(\tau/\tau_0),
  \qquad b_{\mathrm{loc}}(\tau)=\frac{\mathrm d\log Y}{\mathrm d\log\tau},
  \label{eq:effective-slope}
\end{equation}
Logarithms of an observable refer to its numerical value in the stated units,
equivalently $\log(Y/Y_0)$ for a fixed reference $Y_0$ with the same dimensions.
Here $\tau_0=\SI{1}{\second}$; changing reference units alters the intercept, not $b$.
A fit over three decades
provides a compact description of the curve, but if the local slope changes substantially,
that single value is an \emph{effective exponent} rather than evidence of an exact power law.

At the lower end, the native cadence and smoothing window can affect the displacement;
\SI{10}{\second} is not automatically outside that influence for every recorder.
At the upper end, a flight must be long enough to contribute, and the number of windows
per flight decreases. Coverage, local slopes and the open--closed split accompany the fit; any
comparison restricted to longer flights describes that selected population.

Sampling uncertainty and sensitivity to the analysis interval require separate treatment.
Repeated windows from one flight, and flights from the same site and day, can share
information; a final interval estimate should therefore resample whole site--day groups
with their complete curves. Changing the fitted interval instead tests sensitivity to curvature,
smoothing and selection. That sensitivity is reported separately from sampling uncertainty;
adding it to a standard error does not create a confidence interval. The present subset
plots are descriptive measurements, not calibrated hypothesis tests.

\subsection{What a second difference removes}
\label{sec:variations}

A constant velocity contributes a quadratic term to an uncentred MSD. If
$\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf v_d t+\mathbf Y(t)$ and the residual increment has zero mean, then
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E|\mathbf X(t,\tau)|^2
   =|\mathbf v_d|^2\tau^2+
      \mathbb E|\mathbf Y(t+\tau)-\mathbf Y(t)|^2.
  \label{eq:drift-msd}
\end{equation}
Without the zero-mean assumption there is also a cross term. The two contributions add
as functions of lag; their fitted exponents do not add.

A useful control is the second difference,
\begin{align}
  A_2\mathbf r(t;\tau)
    &=\mathbf r(t+2\tau)-2\mathbf r(t+\tau)+\mathbf r(t),
       \label{eq:filter}\\
  V_2(\tau)&=\left\langle|A_2\mathbf r(t;\tau)|^2\right\rangle.
       \label{eq:variation}
\end{align}
Since $A_2(\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_d t)=0$, this observable is exactly insensitive to a
constant velocity. It avoids estimating drift from the endpoints, which would impose a
return constraint on the residual trajectory. Its cost is a window of length $2\tau$.
We retain it for this specific purpose, alongside $V_1(\tau)=\langle R^2\rangle$.

To relate the second-difference slope to $H$, suppose the residual is a centred
finite-variance process with stationary increments and
$\mathbb E|\mathbf Y(t+\tau)-\mathbf Y(t)|^2=K\tau^{2H}$. Writing adjacent increments
as $\mathbf I_1$ and $\mathbf I_2$ gives
$2\mathbb E[\mathbf I_1\cdot\mathbf I_2]=K[(2\tau)^{2H}-2\tau^{2H}]$, hence
\begin{equation}
  V_2(\tau)=K\bigl(4-2^{2H}\bigr)\tau^{2H}.
  \label{eq:variation-hurst}
\end{equation}
Thus half the slope of $V_2$ estimates $H$ under these assumptions. Fractional Brownian
motion with $0<H<1$ is one example~\cite{mandelbrot1968}. The prefactor follows by
expanding the square of the difference of adjacent increments; it is not an additional
fit parameter that changes the exponent.

On a smooth curved trajectory,
$A_2\mathbf r=\ddot{\mathbf r}\,\tau^2+O(\tau^3)$, so $V_2\propto\tau^4$ at sufficiently
small lag. Half that slope is not the Hurst exponent of a stationary-increment position
process. Agreement between differences of order two and three is a consistency check;
it does not prove that all environmental trends have disappeared. In particular,
$b_1-b_2$ is not a measured fraction of transport due to wind or course geometry.

short-scale smoothness and changing support must be resolved before the constant-drift
control can yield an intrinsic $H$.

\subsection{Open and closed flights: geometry survives detrending}
\label{sec:strata-compat}

A declared closed task rewards return towards the starting region. An open task allows
continued progress away from it. This difference can change long-lag displacements even
if speed and short-time behaviour are similar. Task classes are compared separately in
Figure~\ref{fig:transport}; a declaration is not a measurement that a particular retained
segment closes exactly.

A circular trajectory makes the distinction precise. For
$\mathbf r(t)=a(\cos\omega t,\sin\omega t)$ with period $T_c=2\pi/\omega$,
\begin{equation}
  V_1(\tau)=4a^2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{T_c}\right),\qquad
  V_2(\tau)=16a^2\sin^4\!\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{T_c}\right).
  \label{eq:closed-circle}
\end{equation}
The displacement initially grows, then decreases as the lag approaches a complete return.
The second difference also retains this geometry. Removing constant drift cannot remove
closure, a turn, or a spatially varying forcing.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/closed_loop_schematic}
  \caption{Exact geometrical illustration, without flight data. A straight course and a
  circle have the same speed. The circular path is continued periodically when
  evaluating second differences. Its displacement vanishes after one period;
  its second difference is also sensitive to the return. Therefore a change of slope
  need not indicate a change of stochastic process, and second differencing does not
  neutralise a closed task.}
  \label{fig:closed-loop-schematic}
\end{figure}

The selected paraglider tasks include \num{\StatRevParaOpenFlights} open and
\num{\StatRevParaClosedFlights} closed flights; hang gliders contribute
\num{\StatRevHangOpenFlights} and \num{\StatRevHangClosedFlights}. Their effective
$b_1$ values are respectively \StatRevParaOpenAlphaOne\ versus
\StatRevParaClosedAlphaOne, and \StatRevHangOpenAlphaOne\ versus
\StatRevHangClosedAlphaOne. At order two the ordering reverses:
\StatRevParaOpenAlphaTwo\ versus \StatRevParaClosedAlphaTwo\ and
\StatRevHangOpenAlphaTwo\ versus \StatRevHangClosedAlphaTwo. This is consistent with
different route geometry affecting the two observables differently; it is not a
quantitative decomposition into drift and fluctuations.

A complementary geometrical descriptor is the closure ratio
\begin{equation}
  \chi_f=\frac{|\mathbf r_f(T_f)-\mathbf r_f(0)|}
                 {\int_0^{T_f}|\dot{\mathbf r}_f(t)|\,\mathrm dt}\in[0,1].
  \label{eq:closure-ratio}
\end{equation}
In the subset, median $\chi_f$ is \StatRevParaOpenClosure\ for open paraglider tasks
and \StatRevParaClosedClosure\ for closed ones; hang-glider medians are
\StatRevHangOpenClosure\ and \StatRevHangClosedClosure. The geometric contrast therefore
survives checking the actual paths, while the nonzero open-task medians remain far below
the value one of a straight flight.

The ratio is defined for a continuous observed trajectory: small $\chi_f$ indicates little net
progress relative to distance travelled, not necessarily a declared closed task. Comparing
both descriptors, matching duration and region, and studying $\tau/T_f$ as well as
physical lag would separate task geometry from changing composition more sharply. The
current task split is evidence of an association; it does not isolate a causal effect of
the scoring rule.

\section{What the displacement quantiles reveal}
\label{sec:transport-propagator}

\subsection{From self-similarity to a measurable prediction}

For an $H$-self-similar process starting at the origin,
$(\mathbf r(ct_1),\ldots,\mathbf r(ct_n))\overset d=
c^H(\mathbf r(t_1),\ldots,\mathbf r(t_n))$ for every $c>0$ and every finite choice of
times. Self-similarity therefore constrains joint distributions, beyond the MSD alone. With stationary increments, it implies the one-lag
prediction
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf X(t,\tau)\ \overset{d}{=}\
       (\tau/\tau_0)^H\mathbf X(t,\tau_0).
  \label{eq:increment-scaling}
\end{equation}
Writing $a_\tau=(\tau/\tau_0)^H$, and assuming the corresponding densities exist,
the two-dimensional and radial laws obey different Jacobians:
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf x;\tau)=a_\tau^{-2}F(\mathbf x/a_\tau),
  \qquad p_R(r;\tau)=a_\tau^{-1}f(r/a_\tau).
  \label{eq:radial-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The radial law follows by integrating the joint density over angle, including the polar
Jacobian $r$. It does not require isotropy. In particular, a two-dimensional density at
the origin would scale as $a_\tau^{-2}$, not as $a_\tau^{-1}$.

Define the radial quantile at probability $p$ by
\begin{equation}
  Q_p(\tau)=\inf\{r:\Pr[R(t,\tau)\le r]\ge p\},\qquad 0<p<1.
  \label{eq:quantile-definition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:increment-scaling} implies
\begin{equation}
  Q_p(\tau)=a_\tau Q_p(\tau_0),\qquad
  H_p=\frac{\mathrm d\log Q_p}{\mathrm d\log\tau}=H
  \quad\hbox{when }0<Q_p(\tau_0)<\infty.
  \label{eq:quantile-hurst}
\end{equation}
The median probes typical displacement; the lower quartile probes relatively small
displacements; the upper quartile and 90th percentile probe progressively larger ones.
They use ranks rather than powers of distance, complementing the moments without
requiring a fourth moment. They remain sensitive to sampling, flight composition and
finite support. Equality of several quantile slopes is necessary for the scaling form,
but does not prove self-similarity of the full process.

\subsection{Keeping the population fixed across lags}
\label{sec:quantile-population}

At a given lag, all probability levels refer to the same empirical distribution.
The 25th percentile is not an average over a class of slow flights, nor is the 90th
percentile an average over fast flights. Different flights can supply observations
near those ranks, and their rank order can change with lag. This is compatible with
studying a distribution; it does not track individual objects moving from one quantile
to another.

A separate problem arises when the distribution itself changes because its sampling
population changes. If flight $f$ supplies $n_f(\tau)$ admissible increments, pooling
them defines
\begin{equation}
 \widehat F_\tau^{\rm pool}(x)
 =\sum_{f\in\mathcal F_\tau}
    \frac{n_f(\tau)}{\sum_g n_g(\tau)}\widehat F_{f,\tau}(x),\qquad
 \widehat F_{f,\tau}(x)=\frac1{n_f(\tau)}\sum_k
                   \mathbf1\{R_{fk}(\tau)\le x\}.
 \label{eq:quantile-mixture}
\end{equation}
Both membership $\mathcal F_\tau$ and weights can depend on lag: a short flight eventually
contributes nothing, while the number of non-overlapping windows decreases differently
between flights. Quantiles are nonlinear functionals of a distribution, so changing
these weights can change $H_p$ even when every flight follows its own unchanged scaling
law. Their effects cannot be removed by averaging the individual quantiles.

We therefore compare four sampling conventions in Fig.~\ref{fig:quantile-control}:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item All available flights, with the original pooled-window weights.
 \item A fixed cohort of segments at least \SI{20000}{\second} long, retaining pooled weights.
 \item The same fixed flights, giving each flight equal weight at every lag.
 \item The same flights and equal weights, now using the same starting times at every lag.
\end{enumerate}
For the last convention, let $\tau_{\max}=\SI{10000}{\second}$ and keep every starting
time $t_{fk}$ on the 10-s grid satisfying $t_{fk}+\tau_{\max}\le T_f$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \widehat F_\tau^{\rm fixed}(x)
 =\frac1{|\mathcal F_*|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal F_*}\frac1{n_f^*}
     \sum_{k=1}^{n_f^*}\mathbf1\{R_f(t_{fk},\tau)\le x\},\qquad
 \widehat Q_p(\tau)=\inf\{x:\widehat F_\tau(x)\ge p\}.
 \label{eq:quantile-fixed-population}
\end{equation}
The set of flights, origins and their weights are now independent of $\tau$, and the
same weighted observations define every percentile. All four conventions use this
inverse empirical-CDF definition without interpolation between order statistics.
The common origins overlap at most lags, which preserves the population but does not
make increments independent. The third convention isolates flight weights; the fourth
also controls which part of each flight supplies the starting observations.

If the ordering disappears under these controls, the original plot cannot be interpreted
as intrinsic shape evolution. If it persists, changing membership and mixture weights
no longer explain it, but other explanations remain: finite trajectories, systematic
changes of phase along a flight, environmental heterogeneity and sampling uncertainty.
The fixed cohort also selects flights lasting at least about five and a half hours; its
behaviour cannot be generalized to short flights without an additional shorter-lag
comparison. A stable ordering is therefore a property of a specified displacement
population and scale interval, before it is evidence against a stochastic model.

\subsection{A decreasing quantile exponent means decreasing relative spread}

The interpretation of an ordering such as $H_{0.25}>H_{0.90}$ follows directly from
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\log\tau}
       \log\!\left(\frac{Q_{0.90}(\tau)}{Q_{0.25}(\tau)}\right)
       =H_{0.90}(\tau)-H_{0.25}(\tau).
  \label{eq:quantile-ratio-growth}
\end{equation}
A negative right-hand side means that the ratio of large to small displacements decreases.
Both quantiles may increase in absolute distance. The lower one increases faster in
\emph{proportion}, so the distribution becomes less spread out by this relative measure.
For example, a slope difference of $-0.10$ maintained over a tenfold increase of lag
multiplies the ratio by $10^{-0.10}\simeq0.79$.

This is not a statement that the left tail becomes flatter. Quantiles describe cumulative
probability, whereas local flattening concerns a probability density and requires more
information. Nor does the 90th percentile represent the whole distribution: ten per cent
of increments exceed it. Finally, even with fixed flights and origins, the observations occupying a given rank
can change; a small displacement at one lag is not being followed as an individual object.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering

  data. Let $R(\tau)=(\tau/\tau_0)^{0.85}\exp[\sigma(\tau)Z]$, where $Z$ is standard
  Gaussian and $\sigma(\tau)=0.9-0.1\log(\tau/\tau_0)$ on the illustrated interval.
  Then $H_p=0.85-0.1\Phi^{-1}(p)$. All four quantiles grow, but the distribution narrows
  after division by its median. The declining ratio makes that change explicit.}
  \label{fig:quantile-schematic}
\end{figure}

If $H_p$ changes with $p$ in the controlled population, fitting a common $H$ to collapse
the curves hides some change of distribution shape over that interval. A nearly straight moment spectrum can coexist with this failure: moments
integrate across the distribution and need not resolve changes at specific probability
levels. Neither observation licenses calling the process exactly monofractal. The useful
model target is the measured family $Q_p(\tau)$, including its departures from common
scaling.

\subsection{East--north balance in the retained archive}

The east--north second-moment ratio provides an initial check for directional imbalance
in the retained archive. Isotropy would require the \emph{joint} displacement distribution
to be unchanged by
every rotation; equality of the two marginal distributions alone is insufficient.
A first necessary condition is equality of their second moments. For the positions
measured from launch, the archive diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{EN}(t)=\frac{\langle E_f(t)^2\rangle_f}{\langle N_f(t)^2\rangle_f},
  \qquad
  \langle E_f(t)^2\rangle_f
  =\operatorname{Var}_f[E_f(t)]+\langle E_f(t)\rangle_f^2.
  \label{eq:archive-directional-ratio}
\end{equation}
Thus $\rho_{EN}=1$ is necessary for isotropy about the launch point, but is not a full
test of it. The ratio includes mean displacement as well as spread: it does not isolate
anisotropy after drift removal. Here $t$ is elapsed time since launch, not a lag between
two fixes within a flight, and the contributing population changes with $t$.

\subsection{Why marginal scaling need not give radial scaling}

Good collapses of $|X_E|$ and $|X_N|$ do not establish a common scaling law for the vector.
Two failures are possible. First, the component exponents may differ. For centred independent
Gaussian components with variances $a\tau^{2H_E}$ and $b\tau^{2H_N}$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E R^2=a\tau^{2H_E}+b\tau^{2H_N},\qquad
  \frac12\frac{\mathrm d\log\mathbb E R^2}{\mathrm d\log\tau}
   =\frac{H_Ea\tau^{2H_E}+H_Nb\tau^{2H_N}}
          {a\tau^{2H_E}+b\tau^{2H_N}}.
  \label{eq:anisotropic-exponents}
\end{equation}
Each marginal has an exact exponent; the radial second moment has a changing effective
exponent. This counterexample requires no cross-correlation.

Second, even equal marginal exponents leave the dependence between the components
undetermined. The joint law of the rescaled pair may change with lag while both marginal
laws remain fixed. A covariance or correlation coefficient detects only part of that
change. Conversely, a common scaling law for the \emph{joint} vector implies the same
scaling for its modulus, even if the vector is strongly anisotropic. Anisotropy alone
therefore does not explain a failed radial collapse.

\subsection{What regional principal axes measure}

For a region $g$, define the centred displacement covariance
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol\mu_g(\tau)=\mathbb E[\mathbf X\mid g],\qquad
  \Sigma_g(\tau)=\mathbb E[(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_g)
                         (\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu_g)^\top\mid g]
          =U_g\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\,U_g^\top,
  \label{eq:pca-global}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2$. The leading eigenvector describes the direction of greatest
spread, modulo $180^\circ$; it is an axis, not a signed wind direction. The ratio
$\lambda_1/\lambda_2$ measures anisotropy of the covariance. An axis becomes poorly
determined when the eigenvalues are close.

At \SI{\StatRevParaDurationReferenceLagS}{\second}, the Alpine subgroup contains
\num{\StatRevParaPcaAlpsFlights} flights and has eigenvalue ratio
\StatRevParaPcaAlpsRatio, with principal axis
\SI{\StatRevParaPcaAlpsAngle}{\degree} counterclockwise from east. The Pyrenean subgroup

Rotation into the principal frame gives uncorrelated components at the lag at which the
covariance was fitted. It does not establish independence or remove temporal
correlations. It cannot improve radial collapse because
\begin{equation}
  |U_g^\top\mathbf X|=|\mathbf X|.
  \label{eq:pca-radius}
\end{equation}
Whitening with the reference covariance,
$\mathbf Z(\tau)=\Sigma_g(\tau_0)^{-1/2}[\mathbf X(\tau)-\boldsymbol\mu_g(\tau)]$,
changes lengths. It sets the covariance to the identity at the reference lag $\tau_0$; it need not
do so at other lags, and it does not imply a rotationally invariant distribution.
Whitening separately at each lag would enforce a part of the desired collapse by
construction. A meaningful control instead fits a transformation at a fixed reference
scale and tests it on other scales and flights.

freedom in route choice, but season, launch site, flight duration and wing performance
could also account for it. The comparison tests whether the pattern is present; attributing
it specifically to pilot ability would require independent experience information
and comparisons matched on flight conditions.

The kinematic relation
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf v_{\mathrm{ground}}=\mathbf v_{\mathrm{air}}+\mathbf w_h
  \label{eq:ground-air-wind}
\end{equation}
shows the identifiability problem. Without independent information about one term, ground
velocity alone cannot determine the other two. The mean velocity includes route choice,
net progress and wind. Terrain affects velocity through constrained headings and pilot
decisions, while wind affects position through its time integral. Acceleration removes a
strictly constant wind contribution but still contains changing wind and manoeuvres.
If the wind field is $\mathbf w_h(\mathbf r,z,t)$, its derivative along the trajectory is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf a_{\mathrm{ground}}
 =\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf v_{\mathrm{air}}}{\mathrm dt}
  +\partial_t\mathbf w_h
  +(\mathbf v_{\mathrm{ground}}\!\cdot\nabla_h)\mathbf w_h
  +\dot z\,\partial_z\mathbf w_h.
 \label{eq:wind-acceleration}
\end{equation}
Thus even a time-independent wind field contributes to acceleration when the glider
crosses spatial gradients.

The defensible next comparison is conditional: principal axes and displacement statistics
by region and site--day, then alignment with a terrain axis and an independently estimated
wind vector. A thermal-centre drift estimate may help after segmentation, subject to
assumptions about thermal motion; it is not an independent wind measurement by definition.
Randomly rotating flights would create a rotationally balanced pooled sample while
preserving each radial displacement. Such a control can diagnose geographical alignment,
but cannot reconstruct the underlying motion in still air or without mountains.

\subsection{The moment spectrum and the L\'evy-walk benchmark}
\label{sec:transport-shape}

For $q>0$, define a moment and its growth exponent by
\begin{equation}
  M_q(\tau)=\mathbb E[R(t,\tau)^q],\qquad
  M_q(\tau)\propto\tau^{\zeta(q)},\qquad \nu(q)=\zeta(q)/q.
  \label{eq:moment-spectrum}
\end{equation}
A common scaling law with finite moments gives $\zeta(q)=qH$. Increasing $q$ gives large
displacements more weight, so the spectrum tests a different aspect of the distribution
from any one quantile. Plain increments are used here: second differences of a straight
leg vanish and would change the physical observable being compared.

For an unbiased renewal L\'evy walk with independent isotropically chosen headings,
constant speed and leg-duration density
$\psi(t)\sim t^{-1-\beta}$, the superdiffusive regime $1<\beta<2$ predicts
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_{\mathrm{LW}}(q)=
  \begin{cases}
    q/\beta,&0<q<\beta,\\
    q+1-\beta,&q>\beta,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad \zeta_{\mathrm{LW}}(2)=3-\beta.
  \label{eq:levy-spectrum}
\end{equation}
At $q=\beta$ a logarithmic correction accompanies the common power. The bulk and the
rare long legs contribute different branches; their meeting produces strong anomalous
scaling~\cite{zaburdaev2015,rebenshtok2014}. This benchmark is an asymptotic model
prediction with stated renewal and speed assumptions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/moment_spectrum_schematic}
  \caption{Theoretical comparison. A self-similar process with $H=0.75$ and a standard
  L\'evy walk with $\beta=1.5$ have the same MSD exponent, $\zeta(2)=1.5$, but different
  moment spectra. The dashed vertical line marks $q=\beta$. These are asymptotic
  predictions, not simulated or empirical estimates.}
  \label{fig:levy-spectrum-schematic}
\end{figure}

The empirical spectra in Figure~\ref{fig:shape} are compared with these predictions.
For $q$ from $0.25$ to $4$, $\nu(q)$ ranges from \StatRevParaMomentNuMin\ to
\StatRevParaMomentNuMax\ in paragliders and from \StatRevHangMomentNuMin\ to
\StatRevHangMomentNuMax\ in hang gliders. The pooled-increment values
$\zeta(2)=\StatRevParaMomentZetaTwo$ and \StatRevHangMomentZetaTwo\ differ from the
equal-flight $b_1$ in Table~\ref{tab:orderscan} because the weights differ.
The $\beta=1.5$ curve in the figure is an illustrative reference, not a fitted null.
A nearly linear spectrum without the predicted change of branch is evidence against this
particular asymptotic description when the sampled scales and moments can resolve the
change. It does not exclude all L\'evy walks: the ballistic regime $0<\beta<1$, truncated
leg distributions, correlations and pre-asymptotic behaviour require separate comparisons.
Near-linearity must also be compared with the quantiles after controlling their sampling
population; it does not independently establish a common scaling law.

A quantitative rejection requires simulating the specified candidate at fitted parameters with
the observed durations, sampling and preprocessing, then compare the \emph{joint} moment
and quantile results. The 90th percentile alone need not follow the ballistic front,
whose probability changes with time. Its ordering is therefore not a second independent
rejection test. High-order moments also require a flight-level sensitivity check before an inferential
exclusion.

\subsection{Non-Gaussianity: what is measured, and what it cannot identify}
\label{sec:transport-gaussian}

At each lag, centre the increment and standardise by its full covariance:
\begin{equation}
  Q=(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf X-\boldsymbol\mu),
  \qquad K_M(\tau)=\mathbb E[Q^2]-d(d+2),\quad d=2.
  \label{eq:nongauss}
\end{equation}
This is Mardia's kurtosis excess~\cite{mardia1970}. A nonsingular Gaussian vector has
$Q\sim\chi^2_d$, hence $K_M=0$, including when it is anisotropic. Positive excess means
a larger fourth moment of standardised radius than this Gaussian reference, although it
does not by itself imply a power-law tail. Conversely, zero excess satisfies a necessary
Gaussian moment condition without establishing Gaussianity. To call a measured difference
from zero statistically significant also requires a reference calibrated for the finite
sample and its dependence.

The measured $K_M$ is not constant. Across the displayed interval its range is
\StatRevParaMardiaMin\ to \StatRevParaMardiaMax\ for paragliders and
\StatRevHangMardiaMin\ to \StatRevHangMardiaMax\ for hang gliders. The medians over
sampled lags are \StatRevParaMardiaMedian\ and \StatRevHangMardiaMedian, respectively;
these lag medians are descriptive summaries, not time averages. The change of sign near
the shortest lags and the rise where support is smallest discourage replacing the curve
with one claim about a tail.

Excess can also arise from heterogeneity even when the conditional motion is Gaussian.
For example, let $\mathbf X=\sqrt A\,\mathbf G$, where $\mathbf G$ is a centred Gaussian vector with
fixed covariance, $A>0$ is independent, and both required moments exist. Then
\begin{equation}
  K_M=d(d+2)\left[\frac{\mathbb E A^2}{(\mathbb E A)^2}-1\right].
  \label{eq:pooled-nongauss}
\end{equation}
This is a scale-mixture example, not an identity for arbitrary flights with different
means and differently oriented covariance matrices. Subtracting a trace-based width
mixture estimate from pooled kurtosis does not recover each flight's kurtosis.

The measured excess therefore tests a \emph{single homogeneous Gaussian law for the pooled
increments}. To assess Gaussian fluctuations conditional on environment or phase requires
separate centring and covariance estimates in those strata, adequate sample sizes and a
matched simulation. The peak of a pooled kurtosis curve may reflect changing mixtures or
geometry; it is not automatically a thermal timescale or evidence of central-limit
behaviour.

\subsection{Velocity memory at more than one resolution}
\label{sec:transport-memory}

A fix-to-fix velocity mixes recorder cadence, smoothing and physical turning. To examine
longer scales, define velocity averaged over a duration $h$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf v_h(t)=\frac{\mathbf r(t+h)-\mathbf r(t)}{h},\qquad
  C_h(\tau)=\frac{\langle\delta\mathbf v_h(t)\cdot
                             \delta\mathbf v_h(t+\tau)\rangle}
                         {\langle|\delta\mathbf v_h|^2\rangle}.
  \label{eq:vacf}
\end{equation}
The analysis compares $h=10$, $60$ and $\SI{300}{\second}$. In each selected segment,
it forms adjacent block velocities $\mathbf v_{f,j}$, subtracts their mean and computes
\begin{equation}
  \widehat C_{f,h}(kh)=
  \frac{(n_f-k)^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n_f-k-1}
         \delta\mathbf v_{f,j}\cdot\delta\mathbf v_{f,j+k}}
       {n_f^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n_f-1}|\delta\mathbf v_{f,j}|^2},
  \qquad
  \widehat C_h=\frac{1}{N_h(k)}\sum_{f\in\mathcal F_h(k)}\widehat C_{f,h}.
  \label{eq:vacf-estimator}
\end{equation}
Here $n_f$ is the number of complete velocity blocks and $\mathcal F_h(k)$ contains
segments with $n_f\ge8$, nonzero velocity variance and $k\le\lfloor n_f/4\rfloor$.
A mean of normalised correlations gives each contributing flight equal weight; it differs
from normalising a covariance pooled across all flights. Lags smaller than $h$ share averaging intervals
and can generate correlation from overlap alone; interpretation uses non-overlapping
intervals. Correlations at different $h$ answer different questions and need not coincide.
Subtracting a mean estimated on a finite segment can also depress the long-lag correlation.

Figure~\ref{fig:velocity-memory} places the positive correlations on logarithmic axes,
where a power-law interval would satisfy
\begin{equation}
 C_h(\tau)\simeq A_h\tau^{-\gamma_h}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \log C_h(\tau)\simeq\log A_h-\gamma_h\log\tau.
 \label{eq:vacf-loglaw}
\end{equation}
Curvature therefore matters as much as the apparent slope. The companion panels retain
the sign, since a negative correlation cannot be represented on a logarithmic ordinate
and taking its absolute value would change the physical question. The plotted quantity
is the mean of normalised flight correlations in Eq.~\eqref{eq:vacf-estimator}, rather
than a pooled velocity autocovariance. Within one flight, normalisation by the zero-lag
covariance leaves a positive power-law exponent unchanged; after averaging heterogeneous
flights, the weighting still matters.

At a separation of \SI{600}{\second}, the three resolutions give correlations
\StatRevParaVacfTenAtSixHundred, \StatRevParaVacfSixtyAtSixHundred\ and
\StatRevParaVacfThreeHundredAtSixHundred\ for paragliders; the hang-glider values are
\StatRevHangVacfTenAtSixHundred, \StatRevHangVacfSixtyAtSixHundred\ and
\StatRevHangVacfThreeHundredAtSixHundred. Persistence is therefore visible beyond
individual fixes and minute-scale averaging. A larger normalised correlation after
averaging also reflects the suppression of faster fluctuations, not necessarily an
increase in the underlying memory time.

For a stationary centred velocity with variance
$\sigma_v^2=\langle|\delta\mathbf v|^2\rangle$, the displacement variance satisfies
\begin{equation}
  M_{2,c}(\tau)=2\sigma_v^2\int_0^\tau(\tau-s)C(s)\,\mathrm ds.
  \label{eq:green-kubo}
\end{equation}
The velocity-variance factor is required because $C$ is dimensionless. This relation
concerns the centred displacement and the same stationary population; it cannot directly
identify the growth of an uncentred, heterogeneous archive MSD.

For example, an exponentially correlated velocity, $C(s)=e^{-s/t_p}$, gives
\begin{equation}
  M_{2,c}(\tau)=2\sigma_v^2t_p
      \left[\tau-t_p(1-e^{-\tau/t_p})\right].
  \label{eq:persistent-msd}
\end{equation}
It is ballistic for $\tau\ll t_p$ and diffusive for $\tau\gg t_p$. An intermediate
superdiffusive slope can therefore arise in a finite-persistence model. A distribution of
$t_p$ across flights broadens the crossover. Such a model is not excluded merely because
a fitted MSD slope exceeds one.

If instead $C(s)\sim s^{-\gamma}$ with $0<\gamma<1$ \emph{asymptotically},
Eq.~\eqref{eq:green-kubo} gives $M_{2,c}\propto\tau^{2-\gamma}$. Measurements on a finite
interval cannot establish convergence or divergence of the integral to infinity.
Consequently the measured memory is reported at the observed scales, without declaring
it non-integrable. Its persistence also does not prove correlation between successive
glides: that comparison requires the episode boundaries of Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}.

\section{Flight duration and equipment composition}
\label{sec:duration-check}

Longer flights may select different equipment, pilots and conditions. A change in their
MSD can therefore reflect a change of population rather than an effect of observation
length. We examine both duration thresholds and their equipment composition, using the
identified segment curves from the full archive: \num{\StatDurationParaFlights} paraglider
and \num{\StatDurationHangFlights} hang-glider flights have eligible segments with native
cadence at most \SI{10}{\second}. Here $T_f$ is the sum of the retained segment spans
of flight $f$; gaps and discarded time are not part of this observed duration.

First combine the available segment curves within each flight, weighting them by the
number of admissible time origins, then give every contributing flight equal weight:
\begin{equation}
 m_f(\tau)=\frac{\sum_s n_{fs}(\tau)m_{fs}(\tau)}{\sum_s n_{fs}(\tau)},\qquad
 M_{T_0}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N_{T_0}(\tau)}
      \sum_{f:T_f\ge T_0,\,n_f(\tau)>0}m_f(\tau).
 \label{eq:duration-flight-msd}
\end{equation}
For an eligible segment of $N_{fs}$ fixes at interval $\delta_{fs}$,
$n_{fs}=N_{fs}-\operatorname{round}(\tau/\delta_{fs})$; it is zero outside that segment's
stored lag support, which ends at $\lfloor N_{fs}/2\rfloor\delta_{fs}$. No displacement
crosses a segment boundary. This equal-flight average differs from the equal-segment
archive average introduced earlier. Requested lags are rounded to each segment's native grid.

To separate within-class behaviour from class proportions, write the exact mixture
identity over the two experience proxies,
\begin{equation}
 M_{T_0}^{\rm known}(\tau)=\sum_c p_c(\tau\mid T_0)M_{T_0,c}(\tau),\qquad
 p_c(\tau\mid T_0)=\frac{N_{T_0,c}(\tau)}{\sum_jN_{T_0,j}(\tau)}.
 \label{eq:duration-mixture}
\end{equation}
The weights depend on lag as well as duration. A standardized comparison instead holds
them fixed at $\pi_c=N_{0,c}/\sum_jN_{0,j}$, the class proportions among all eligible
known-equipment flights:
\begin{equation}
 M_{T_0}^{\rm fixed}(\tau)=\sum_c\pi_c M_{T_0,c}(\tau).
 \label{eq:duration-standardized}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:duration-composition} compares the four-hour/all-duration ratio before
and after this adjustment. Both ratios exclude the same unknown or other equipment;
otherwise changing the population would be confounded with changing the weights.
The adjustment is displayed only where every class has at least
\StatDurationMinFlights\ contributing flights.

If adjustment moves the ratio towards unity, equipment composition explains part of the

within equipment groups. Neither outcome isolates pilot skill or an effect of duration

These controls test a possible explanation of the original duration plot. They do not
establish independence from total flight duration in this heterogeneous archive.

\section{Chapter summary and constraints for the phase analysis}
\label{sec:transport-verdict}

The later model comparison must reproduce the displacement moments and quantiles over stated lags,
changes in relative distribution width, anisotropy by region, coarse velocity memory,
and differences associated with open and closed tasks. The second difference is retained
as a control with a precise invariance: it cancels a constant velocity. It does not
identify that velocity as wind or reconstruct an environmentally neutral flight.

The next chapter introduces phase labels. A subsequent analysis must measure durations,
displacements, altitude changes and dependencies within and between episodes. There is an
exact bookkeeping constraint on that work. Let $c$ partition \emph{all} admissible windows
into phase histories, including windows that cross a boundary. Then
\begin{equation}
  M_q(\tau)=\sum_c w_c(\tau)M_q(\tau\mid c),\qquad
  w_c=\frac{n_c}{\sum_{c'}n_{c'}},\qquad \sum_cw_c=1,
  \label{eq:phase-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
for a window-weighted observable. A flight-weighted version first applies the identity
within each flight. Restricting to windows wholly inside one of three phases omits
transition windows and will not, in general, reconstruct the global moment. The exponents
are not weighted sums of phase exponents because both amplitudes and weights depend on lag.

Any exhaustive partition satisfies this identity, including one with incorrect labels.
Reconstructing the global moments therefore checks the calculation; independent labels
are still needed to assess the phase assignment.
