\chapter{The dataset}
\label{ch:dataset}

This chapter follows the data end to end: how the raw archive is acquired, verified and
kept reproducible (Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}); what the records contain and what the
collection looks like (Secs.~\ref{sec:igcformat}--\ref{sec:dataquality}); and the
pre-processing that turns a raw tracklog into the analysis-ready trajectory the transport
analysis consumes (Sec.~\ref{sec:preproc}). The pipeline summary
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pipelinemap}) brings these steps together; a characterization of the
retained dataset closes the chapter (Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}).

\section{Data acquisition}
\label{sec:acquisition}

\subsection{Source: the FFVL Coupe F\'ed\'erale de Distance}

The data come from the \emph{Coupe F\'ed\'erale de Distance} (CFD), the cross-country
competition run by the French free-flight federation (FFVL). The federation operates two
parallel competitions on separate sites: one for paragliders
(\texttt{parapente.ffvl.fr}) and one for hang gliders (\texttt{delta.ffvl.fr}). Pilots
upload their flights; each entry carries metadata---among them pilot, date, take-off
and landing sites, scored distance, duration, and glider, with the full set of fields
listed in Sec.~\ref{sec:catalog}---and, in most cases, the raw GPS tracklog in IGC format
(the standard flight-recorder format, Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}).
A ``season'' is identified by its starting year,
e.g.\ the year~1999 denotes the 1999--2000 season; the paraglider archive starts in
1999--2000 and the hang-glider archive in 2001--2002.

\subsection{Collected data}

For every season the pipeline retrieves two things:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \textbf{season XML export}, archived verbatim as provenance
        (\texttt{raw\_xml/<year>.xml}); it is the authoritative list of flights and links;
  \item the \textbf{IGC tracklog files} referenced by the XML, one file per flight that
        has a track.
\end{itemize}
From these, a derived \textbf{catalog} is built (\texttt{catalog.csv}, one row per flight,
its columns detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:catalog}) together with a per-season index
(\texttt{seasons\_index.csv}). The raw data (tens of gigabytes) live on an external disk
and are never committed to the repository. The catalog is itself rebuildable from
the archived XMLs and likewise uncommitted (Sec.~\ref{sec:catalog}).

\subsection{Pipeline and tooling}

Acquisition is performed by a dedicated, installable Python package, exposed through two
command-line tools, one per discipline, sharing the same subcommands for fetching,
downloading, cataloguing, and verifying. The design favours robustness for a
long-running, large job: downloads are resumable and safe to interrupt, a response is
accepted only once validated as real IGC content, and download requests are rate-limited
so as not to load the server .

\subsection{Reproducibility}

The destination directory is selected through an environment variable or configuration
file. The local configuration contains the working disk path; an environment override
allows the same acquisition to run elsewhere. Archiving the season XMLs verbatim
preserves the acquisition provenance.

\subsection{Integrity}

Because the data sit on an external drive, integrity is re-checked independently after
download, rather than trusted at download time . Both
FFVL collections have been verified in full: all \num{\StatParaDownloaded} paraglider and
\num{\StatHangDownloaded} hang-glider downloaded tracklogs pass the same content check
applied at download --- a readable file whose first record is an \texttt{A} header and which
carries at least one \texttt{B} fix record (Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}). These are the tracklogs actually fetched; they
cover a subset of the flights that carry a track at all (Sec.~\ref{sec:coverage}).

\subsection{Further data sources}
The FFVL CFD is the primary source of the present study, but the dataset is not assumed to
be final. The paraglider data are the main analysis target; the hang-glider data are
collected in parallel and enable the cross-type comparison of Sec.~\ref{sec:glider}
(the counts for both are given in Sec.~\ref{sec:coverage}). Additional
public soaring repositories are
being pursued to enlarge and diversify the sample and, above all, to add the third glider
category, \emph{sailplanes} (Sec.~\ref{sec:glider}). A large sailplane source with a
machine-readable interface is \emph{WeGlide}, which exposes flight metadata and IGC tracks
through an API. The
default access is rate-limited (of order sixty requests per day), which rules out a bulk
export of the archive; a request for research access with a higher quota has been sent
and is pending. Paraglider and hang-glider coverage may likewise be broadened through
further portals such as XContest. A comparable research-access request has been sent
to XContest; as of 20 July 2026 neither WeGlide nor XContest has replied. All of these can
be acquired with the same approach, each producing a catalog in the same format.

\section{The IGC tracklog format}
\label{sec:igcformat}

\begin{revblock}
A flight track is stored as an \emph{IGC file}, the plain-text format defined by the
FAI/IGC Technical Specification for IGC-approved GNSS flight recorders~\cite{fai_igc_spec},
decoded from fixed character positions; every downloaded track is checked at acquisition to
be IGC content --- an \texttt{A} record followed by at least one \texttt{B} record
(Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}).

Each line is a \emph{record} whose type is set by its first character: \texttt{A} the
flight-recorder identifier, \texttt{H} header fields (date, pilot, glider, datum, \dots),
\texttt{I} the declaration of optional extra fields, and \texttt{B} the GPS fixes that form
the body of the file. A \texttt{B} record decodes to a UTC time, latitude and longitude on
the WGS84 datum, a fix-validity flag, and \emph{two} altitudes in metres --- barometric
(pressure) then GNSS. The flag describes the GNSS solution: \texttt{A} denotes a three-dimensional fix,
whereas \texttt{V} denotes either a two-dimensional fix or an absence of GNSS data
\cite{fai_igc_spec}. Thus \texttt{V} invalidates the adopted GNSS altitude but does
\emph{not} certify that the horizontal coordinates remain correct. The cleaning retains
those coordinates provisionally and subjects them to the horizontal and frozen-lock
checks below. A pressure reading in the same \texttt{B} record is a separate measurement;
its numerical presence alone does not establish that the sensor is working.

Decoding can succeed numerically on a corrupted byte that is not a legal encoding, so the
parser also checks format validity at decode time (a valid time, valid coordinate fields, a
valid hemisphere letter), dropping the record otherwise, on the same footing as a record of
the wrong length . This is distinct from the
\emph{plausibility} checks of fix-level cleaning (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}), which target
fixes that decode validly but are physically implausible given the rest of the trajectory.

The sequence of \texttt{B} records is a time-stamped three-dimensional trajectory at a
sampling period typically of a few seconds, not guaranteed uniform and varying across
recording devices --- a point the analysis must handle explicitly.
\end{revblock}

\section{Glider categories}
\label{sec:glider}

\begin{revblock}
Soaring kinematics depend strongly on the aircraft, so the glider must be identifiable
for any stratified analysis. The reference study distinguishes three aircraft families: paragliders with a flexible
canopy, hang gliders with a framed flexible or rigid wing, and sailplanes with a rigid
airframe. Their speed ranges, glide performance and turning geometry differ, which
motivates separate analyses rather than a single pooled transport law~\cite{vilpellet2026}.

The category is set by the \emph{data source}: the FFVL competition is split by discipline
(Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}), so the present dataset spans both paragliders and hang
gliders, already enough for the cross-type comparison of~\cite{vilpellet2026}. Sailplanes
are collected by different portals and not yet in hand.
\end{revblock}

Within each discipline, the catalogue field \texttt{aile\_class} provides equipment
groups (Table~\ref{tab:aileclass}). For paragliders it combines EN and older LTF labels,
for example \enquote{C ou 2}; the analysis harmonizes these catalogue codes under the
short labels EN A, B, C and D, with CCC and tandem/non-certified entries kept distinct.
This is a grouping convention, not an independent reassessment of a wing's certificate.

The EN categories describe responses in prescribed flight-safety tests and the demands
of recovery, as set out in the consulted 2012 final draft~\cite{fpren926_2_2012}. They do
not define a measured ladder of cruise speed or glide ratio. Equipment class can be
associated with performance and pilot selection, but those associations must be checked
in the observations. The published 2013 standard and subsequent amendments were not
compared here, so the table is not presented as a current certification specification.

The FAI hang-glider classes principally distinguish design and control systems~\cite{fai_sc7_2024}.
Our stratified comparisons require reproducible group membership; they do not assume
that classes are equally spaced on a performance scale. Neither
scheme measures an individual pilot's experience. Blank and placeholder entries remain
unclassified, and a tandem designation alone does not establish whether that wing has
an EN certificate.
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} uses the paraglider classes to define two operational
experience proxies, beginners (EN A/B) and experts (EN C/D/CCC), while retaining
this distinction between equipment requirements and individual skill.

\section{Data quality and caveats}
\label{sec:dataquality}

A few limitations are already known and will shape the analysis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flights without a track.} About \SI{8}{\percent} of paraglider entries and
        about \SI{27}{\percent} of hang-glider entries have no IGC file (older or manually
        declared flights); they carry metadata only. These are the flights outside the
        \enquote{With GPS} column of Tables~\ref{tab:seasons-para}
        and~\ref{tab:seasons-hang}.
  \item \textbf{A small residue of unrecoverable files.} A few flights advertise a track
        whose download fails or returns something that is not valid IGC content
        (broken or placeholder links); these are recorded as failures and excluded.
  \item \textbf{Heterogeneous logging.} Sampling rate, availability of the two altitude
        channels, and per-fix positioning noise depend
        on the device; trajectories must be resampled and quality-filtered before any
        ensemble statistic is computed.
  \item \textbf{Altitude channel.} A sizeable minority of loggers record no
        pressure altitude -- almost a third of paraglider and roughly a sixth of hang-glider
        flights. GNSS altitude is used for every admitted flight, with the channel
        recorded per flight (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}).
  \item \textbf{Glider metadata.} Flights are paragliders or hang gliders, tagged by source
        (Section~\ref{sec:glider}); the class field \texttt{aile\_class} must be normalised
        before it is used to stratify.
  \item \textbf{Incomplete dates.} Some historical entries carry the placeholder date
        \texttt{0000-00-00}, written as a sentinel at acquisition. Such entries cannot
        support a calendar-conditioned comparison until a date is recovered independently;
        the relative-time preprocessing does not itself enforce this metadata exclusion.
        The count is small: \num{\StatCatParaPlaceholderDateCount} of
        \num{\StatCatParaFlights} paraglider entries carry the sentinel
        (\StatCatParaPlaceholderDatePct\,\%), \num{\StatCatHangPlaceholderDateCount} of
        \num{\StatCatHangFlights} hang-glider ones, and the largest within-season fraction is
        only \StatCatParaPlaceholderWorstPct\,\% of its entries
        (\StatCatParaPlaceholderWorstSeason).
\end{itemize}
These caveats motivate the pre-processing described in the remainder of this chapter.

\section{Trajectory pre-processing}
\label{sec:preproc}
Each IGC file is parsed into a sequence of fixes $(t_i,\varphi_i,\lambda_i,h_i)$ -- UTC
time, geodetic latitude and longitude, and altitude. The pipeline then proceeds in a fixed order:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item choose the altitude channel (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel});
  \item clean individual fixes (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel});
  \item trim the ground phases (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming});
  \item filter out unfit whole flights (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter});
  \item convert to a local Cartesian frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu});
  \item enforce a uniform sampling interval within each flight (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform});
  \item smooth and differentiate the trajectory (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}).
\end{enumerate}
The output is a tidy, analysis-ready representation written to three tables on the
data disk: one carrying a row per fix, one a row per segment, one a row per flight
\emph{attempted}. What each file holds and how the disk is organised is set out in
Sec.~\ref{sec:pipelinemap}.

Steps~(i)--(iv) act on the \emph{raw geographic} coordinates, since none of them
needs a direction. A horizontal distance comes straight from $(\varphi,\lambda)$ through
the great-circle (haversine) formula of Eq.~\eqref{eq:haversine}; a speed is one such
distance over an elapsed time; an altitude is read as logged. The conversion~(v) to the
metric East--North--Up frame
(Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}) then runs on the cleaned, trimmed track, with the origin at the first
fix that survives trimming, so the tangent-plane frame is built from good data. Steps
(vi)--(vii) come last because they produce \emph{vector} quantities: 3D position,
velocity and acceleration components, which need the Cartesian axes step~(v) provides.


Every step below is governed by a handful of numerical thresholds. Each is stated where it
is argued, and the whole set is gathered, with the symbols used here, in
Table~\ref{tab:workingparams} in the pipeline summary (Sec.~\ref{sec:pipelinemap}).
All of them are \emph{working values}, specified independently of the transport
exponents and audited through their removal fractions (Table~\ref{tab:pipecensus}).

\subsection{Choice of altitude channel}
\label{sec:altchannel}

The analysis uses the logged \emph{GNSS} altitude for every admitted flight. This
avoids switching between pressure altitude and GNSS within the population, but it does
not establish a common vertical datum. IGC-approved recorders specify ellipsoidal
height; the published CIVL specification document for hang-gliding and paragliding
recorders prescribes geoid-referenced GNSS altitude~\cite{fai_igc_spec,fai_civl_s7h_2024}.
The published CIVL document contains draft annotations, and recorder compliance across
this historical archive has not been established. The parser currently preserves the
numerical field without resolving these conventions.
Throughout the analysis, $z$ therefore means \emph{logged GNSS altitude}.

Pressure altitude also depends on atmospheric pressure and temperature. The channel
choice is a consistent data-processing convention, not evidence that GNSS is always
more accurate. A constant height offset cancels from increments; a spatially varying
geoid correction or a changing sensor bias does not. Absolute-height comparisons need
a recorder-datum audit before they can be interpreted geodetically.
\paragraph{Comparing the barometric and GNSS channels.}
Figure~\ref{fig:altnoise} compares them on \num{3000} flights per discipline. Panel~(a)
shows one representative flight over a \SI{30}{\minute} window: the two raw channels
track each other with a varying offset. Panel~(b) is their
difference, mean removed -- the offset is not constant but drifts by tens of metres
already within this window. Such a change can include pressure and temperature effects, spatially varying
reference-surface differences, and GNSS error; these traces alone do not separate them.
Panel~(c) is the per-frequency \emph{median} Welch spectrum of each channel
(Appendix~\ref{app:psd}), pooled over both disciplines with the \numrange{10}{90}
percentile band across flights: at high frequency the two medians nearly coincide, both
compatible with contributions from metre-resolution recording and sensor noise.
Coincident median spectra do not establish equal accuracy on individual flights.

\paragraph{Variation between flights.}
Similar medians can conceal different upper tails. We therefore measure each flight's
median PSD in the \SIrange{0.35}{0.50}{\hertz} band and compare it with the median of
these values across barometric records.
Of the \num{\StatAltNoisePsdSampleFlights} paired flights contributing to the spectrum, \StatAltNoiseGnssExcessThreeXPct\,\% carry a GNSS floor above three times
the barometric channel's typical one, and \StatAltNoiseGnssExcessTenXPct\,\% above ten
times it -- a substantial minority. Multipath is one possible GNSS error source
\cite{garciacrespillo2024}; this comparison does not attribute the measured excess
to a unique physical mechanism.

\paragraph{Availability.}
A flight is admitted only when its GNSS channel is both present -- covering at least
\SI{\PreprocGnssPresentMinPct}{\percent} of its fixes -- and alive -- spanning at least
\SI{\PreprocGnssMinRangeM}{\metre} -- since a dead sensor writes the whole channel as
zero and a stuck one a constant (Sec.~\ref{sec:preproc}). Figure~\ref{fig:altnoise}d shows
where the population actually sits against the presence cutoff: nearly every flight is at
or near full presence, with only a thin population approaching it from below. A flight below either
bound fails the adopted vertical-data quality requirement and is excluded from this
analysis; this does not imply that every one of its coordinates is unusable. The presence criterion alone excludes \StatScanParaNoGnssPct\,\% of paraglider and
\StatScanHangNoGnssPct\,\% of hang-glider flights. Both channels fall below their
presence threshold in \StatScanParaNoAltitudePct\,\% and
\StatScanHangNoAltitudePct\,\%, respectively; this is not a count of channels with
every reading absent. Within an admitted flight, a fix with missing GNSS altitude keeps its horizontal
coordinates. Only its altitude is marked missing; resampling reconstructs that channel
and marks it \texttt{z\_reconstructed}. There is no substitution from the barometer.
The affected-flight fractions are \StatScanParaGnssMissFlightsPct\,\% for paragliders and
\StatScanHangGnssMissFlightsPct\,\% for hang gliders; the median number of missing
altitudes among affected paraglider flights is \StatScanParaGnssMissMedianFixes.

\paragraph{A witness must be available where it is used.}
The pressure channel has a separate role: it can corroborate a suspected frozen GNSS
position and an interior ground interval (Secs.~\ref{sec:fixlevel} and
\ref{sec:trimming}). Its flight-level eligibility requires at least
\SI{\PreprocBaroWitnessPresentMinPct}{\percent} nonzero finite readings and a range of at
least \SI{\PreprocBaroWitnessMinRangeM}{\metre}. The corresponding archive fractions,
\StatPipeParaBaroWitnessPct\,\% and \StatPipeHangBaroWitnessPct\,\%, describe eligibility,
not coverage of every candidate interval.

At fix $i$, define $b_i=1$ when a finite, nonzero pressure altitude accompanies the
horizontal position in the same decoded record, and $b_i=0$ otherwise. For a candidate
interval $I$, the barometric test is available only if
\begin{equation}
  B_I=B_{\mathrm{flight}}\prod_{i\in I}b_i=1.
  \label{eq:baro-coverage}
\end{equation}
Before duplicate timestamps are merged, zero pressure values are converted to missing:
a duplicate pair $\{0,1000\}$ supplies its one available \SI{1000}{\metre} reading,
not an invented \SI{500}{\metre} average. If both are missing, the merged witness
remains missing. This uses simultaneous readings, not interpolation across time.

A globally usable barometer with even one missing reading inside $I$ cannot certify that
interval: the barometric criterion abstains. The missing values are neither interpolated
for this decision nor treated as zeros. The pairing needed here is \emph{position and
pressure at the same timestamp}; requiring a valid GNSS altitude as well would remove
the independent witness precisely when the GNSS altitude fails.

Requiring both sensors on \emph{every flight before cleaning} would select a different
population of recording devices and discard usable horizontal trajectories. Instead,
GNSS-only flights remain eligible and the weaker recorder-declaration rule is stated
explicitly below. A sensitivity analysis restricted to $B_I=1$ would test how much a
result depends on this weaker evidence.

\subsection{Fix-level cleaning}
\label{sec:fixlevel}

Cleaning separates evidence of corruption from the action taken on the trajectory.
A horizontal defect changes which positions can be used; an altitude defect need not.
The three cases in Fig.~\ref{fig:defect-schematic} organize this distinction:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Off-the-trend horizontal defect.} A position spike or an out-and-back
        jump may receive a Hampel flag. Deletion depends independently on the
        impossibility gate: the position is unreachable at the speed bound and removal
        of its block permits a reachable rejoin. A flag alone never deletes a fix.
  \item \textbf{On-the-trend defect.} Repeated or backward timestamps and frozen
        positions can agree geometrically with their neighbours. They require time and
        recorder checks. Duplicate timestamps are merged; frozen runs produce a
        mandatory segment boundary.
  \item \textbf{Vertical defect.} A missing or implausible altitude is invalidated
        while its horizontal fix remains. Reconstruction is recorded separately from
        horizontal interpolation, so later vertical analyses can identify it.
\end{enumerate}
A retained fix is not guaranteed correct. Slowly varying errors and plausible-looking
corruption can escape every local test; smoothing does not prove that these errors are
small. The operational aim is to remove positively identified defects while making
removals, reconstruction and remaining uncertainty inspectable.

\paragraph{Geometry on the raw coordinates.}
The cleaning runs on the raw geodetic coordinates $(\varphi_i,\lambda_i)$, before the
Cartesian conversion (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}), so distances between fixes are great-circle
(haversine) distances~\cite{sklearn_haversine} on a sphere of mean radius
$R_\oplus=\SI{6371}{\kilo\metre}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:haversine}
\Delta s_i = 2R_\oplus\arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\!\frac{\varphi_{i+1}-\varphi_i}{2}
  + \cos\varphi_i\cos\varphi_{i+1}\,\sin^2\!\frac{\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_i}{2}}\,,
\end{equation}
giving the horizontal speed $v_{xy,i}=\Delta s_i/(t_{i+1}-t_i)$ and, from the adopted
altitude channel, the vertical velocity $v_{z,i}=(h_{i+1}-h_i)/(t_{i+1}-t_i)$; the same
distance also
yields the whole-flight \emph{path length} $L=\sum_i\Delta s_i$ and \emph{extent}
$D=\max_i\Delta s(\mathrm{fix}_0,\mathrm{fix}_i)$. The spherical approximation is ample:
replacing the WGS84 ellipsoid by a sphere perturbs an inter-fix distance by about
$\SI{0.3}{\percent}$ at the mid-latitudes of the main study regions\footnote{\rev{The ellipsoid's local
radius of curvature runs from the meridian value $M(\varphi)$ to the prime-vertical value
$N(\varphi)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:geo-ps}), which the haversine formula replaces by the single
radius $R_\oplus$. At $\varphi\approx45^\circ$, the latitude of the Alps sites that launch
most of this archive (Sec.~\ref{sec:prelim}), $N(45^\circ)=\SI{6388.83}{\kilo\metre}$ and
$M(45^\circ)=\SI{6367.38}{\kilo\metre}$ against $R_\oplus=\SI{6371}{\kilo\metre}$: a step is
shortened by approximately $\SI{0.28}{\percent}$ running east--west and stretched by
approximately $\SI{0.06}{\percent}$ running north--south, together the $\lesssim0.3\%$ above.
More generally the deviation is of order the flattening $f=1/298.257\approx\SI{0.34}{\percent}$
at any latitude, up to $\SI{+0.56}{\percent}$ for a meridional step at the equator and
$\SI{-0.45}{\percent}$ near the poles. Derived in Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}.}}, about
\SI{3}{\centi\metre} on a \SI{10}{\metre} step, far below the metre-scale noise of
consumer GNSS receivers.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{The Hampel identifier.}
Let the window of fix $k$ be the fixes within $\pm w$ of $t_k$, and
$\tilde\varphi_k,\tilde\lambda_k$ the component-wise window medians. The residual is the
great-circle distance from that median point,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hampelresid}
  r_k=\Delta s\big((\varphi_k,\lambda_k),(\tilde\varphi_k,\tilde\lambda_k)\big),
\end{equation}
with local scale $\operatorname{MAD}_k=\operatorname{med}\{r_j:|t_j-t_k|\le w\}$, and the
fix is flagged when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hampel}
  r_k > \max\!\big(k\,\sigma_k,\ \varepsilon_{\min}\big),
  \qquad \sigma_k = 1.4826\operatorname{MAD}_k,
\end{equation}
$\sigma_k$ a robust local scale~\cite{rousseeuw1993}. The factor $1.4826$ is the
usual one-dimensional Gaussian MAD normalization; for these two-dimensional radial
residuals it is a scale convention, not an exact standard deviation or a calibrated
false-alarm probability. The flag requires both a relative departure and an absolute floor $\varepsilon_{\min}$ of a few times the
GPS noise. The working values are $k=\PreprocHampelK$, a half-window
$w=\SI{\PreprocHampelWindowS}{\second}$ and
$\varepsilon_{\min}=\SI{\PreprocHampelEpsM}{\metre}$, with the test evaluated only where
the window holds at least \PreprocHampelMinFixes\ fixes. A flag does not delete: what deletes is the absolute $v_{xy}$
bound, which removes a fix when
\begin{itemize}
  \item it is unreachable from the last accepted fix at that bound, \emph{and}
  \item the removal of its block lets the track rejoin within it.
\end{itemize}
The identifier flags and
attributes, and supplies the per-flight anomaly count, but is not required for deletion: a genuine tight thermalling circle produces the same
local-median departure as a spike, at a fraction of the speed a spike would imply, so the
protection lies in the speed bound that does the deleting rather than in the flag.
\end{revblock}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{What the median vertical-speed test measures.}
Write the observed altitude as $h_i=z_i+\epsilon_i$. Even a smooth true trajectory
produces noisy finite differences,
\begin{equation}
 v_{z,i}^{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{h_{i+1}-h_i}{t_{i+1}-t_i}
 =\overline v_{z,i}^{\mathrm{true}}+
   \frac{\epsilon_{i+1}-\epsilon_i}{t_{i+1}-t_i}.
 \label{eq:altitude-difference-noise}
\end{equation}
For independent errors of variance $\sigma_z^2$ at uniform cadence $\Delta t$, the noise
term has variance $2\sigma_z^2/\Delta t^2$. This illustrative calculation explains why
one large derivative at a short cadence is weak evidence of a defect; independence is
not assumed for real GNSS noise.

The sustained-excess statistic is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:vzlocal}
 \tilde v_j=\operatorname{med}\{ |v_{z,i}^{\mathrm{obs}}|:
       |m_i-m_j|\le w_v,\ v_{z,i}^{\mathrm{obs}}\text{ finite}\},\qquad
 m_i=\tfrac12(t_i+t_{i+1}),
\end{equation}
with $w_v=\SI{\PreprocVzWindowS}{\second}$. It measures the typical \emph{magnitude}
of the local observed vertical increments, not a signed climb rate or a noise-free
velocity. Each value belongs to a \emph{step}, located at its midpoint $m_i$;
a record of $N$ fixes therefore supplies $N-1$ values. Figure~\ref{fig:vz-window}
shows the calculation: select the midpoints within the window, take the absolute
speeds, sort them, and read the middle value. For $2r+1$ finite values, the median
exceeds a bound only if at least $r+1$ values do. For an even number, the median
is the average of the two central values.

With fewer than \PreprocVzMinWindowFixes\ finite steps, the implementation does
not use the median. Writing $n_j$ for that count, the quantity used by the cleaning is
\[
 u_j=\begin{cases}
       \tilde v_j, & n_j\geq\PreprocVzMinWindowFixes,\\
       |v_{z,j}^{\mathrm{obs}}|, & n_j<\PreprocVzMinWindowFixes.
     \end{cases}
\]
The second branch is the sparse-window fallback. It can occur at a slow recording
cadence, near missing altitudes, or at the ends of a record. The sustained-excess
rule marks the altitude of an interior fix $k$ as missing when
\begin{equation}
  u_{k-1}>v_z^{\max}\quad\text{and}\quad u_k>v_z^{\max},
  \qquad v_z^{\max}=\SI{\PreprocMaxVSpeedMps}{\metre\per\second}.
  \label{eq:vz-censor}
\end{equation}
Step $k-1$ arrives at fix $k$ and step $k$ leaves it. Their statistics must
\emph{both} exceed the bound; equality does not trigger the rule
(Fig.~\ref{fig:vz-decision}). When both windows are populated, this tests the
surrounding speeds. It does not require either of the fix's own increments to
exceed the bound. The windows share observations, so agreement is not independent
confirmation, nor does it identify which altitude caused the excess.

below the chosen threshold. A sustained observed excess above it is censored; whether
it arose from noise or exceptionally strong physical motion cannot be decided from the
median alone. Correlated noise, clustered errors and a long missing interval therefore
remain relevant to phase segmentation.

\paragraph{An unreturned vertical step.}
A level jump can escape both tests: on a sufficiently low-speed background, one
large step need not raise the window median, and no opposite-sign return triggers
the spike rule. Candidate level shifts are counted as \path{n_alt_level_shift}, but are not automatically corrected. A persistent
offset affects absolute altitude and produces a transient after differentiation
and smoothing; its effect on the phase features must be assessed after the new
cleaning run. A small count alone would not establish a small dynamical effect.

The vertical-speed threshold is placed at
\SI{\PreprocMaxVSpeedMps}{\metre\per\second}, near the change from a steep decrease
to a more slowly decaying tail in Fig.~\ref{fig:fixlevel}(b). Around
\SIrange{9}{10}{\metre\per\second}, the paraglider density (blue) also begins to
exceed the hang-glider density (red). These visual features motivate an empirical
cleaning threshold: the transition is gradual, and neither the change of slope nor the
crossing establishes a physical maximum or proves that every larger value is an error.
Values in this tail are treated as candidate outliers by the two-window and spike
rules; only the altitude is invalidated, never the horizontal fix.
The full diagnostic sample has \SI{0.069}{\percent} of window medians above this
threshold, compared with \SI{0.014}{\percent} above the previous
\SI{30}{\metre\per\second} threshold. These are exceedance fractions, not removal
rates. Inspection of real examples and sensitivity to the cut remain necessary,
especially for extreme descents.
The altitude and horizontal-speed bounds are also checked against the observed
histograms. The Hampel and frozen-lock parameters are likewise working values
(Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}); removal fractions quantify their impact, not their
classification accuracy.

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Removal policy.}
A speed belongs to the step \emph{between} two fixes, so a bound exceedance alone
does not identify the erroneous endpoint. The horizontal rule removes an unreachable
block when its removal permits a rejoin within the bound. This convention uses the
local geometry to assign the removal; it does not prove which recorded positions are
wrong. What happens next depends on the channel that fails the rule, with
reconstruction deferred to the single resampling step (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). A corrupt
\emph{altitude} leaves the fix kept and only that channel marked missing, since the
horizontal position and timestamp are intact and still sample the path; a corrupt
\emph{horizontal position or time} deletes the whole fix, because keeping it with the
position marked missing would hide the hole from the gap rule that decides whether to
bridge or split (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), and an invented straight line could then be laid
across a full thermalling circle. The IGC validity flag needs
no rule of its own: a \texttt{V} flag declares an unavailable or degraded GNSS fix
(Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}), and since the adopted channel is GNSS on every flight
(Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}) it always reduces to the corrupt-altitude case.

\paragraph{An impossible step is a boundary, never flown path.}
After fix-level cleaning, any surviving pair whose inferred horizontal speed exceeds
the bound is separated by a segment boundary. Where a block
is kept because nothing could rejoin it, the step by which the track returns from it is
itself impossible and, left alone, enters a segment as flown motion: a jump of tens of
kilometres inside one second, in the archive. Such a step is a transition whose course is
unknown, so it is made a segment boundary on the same footing as a long gap
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}), counted per flight as \path{n_boundaried}
.
\end{revblock}

\rev{A corrupt first fix is a blind spot no bounded-removal test can close: the scan
carries the last accepted fix as an anchor and never questions its own first one, and that
fix becomes the origin of the local frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}), so the error does not
average out with record length: five paraglider flights left the first full-archive run
some \SI{4500}{\kilo\metre} from their own origin, enough to raise the ensemble MSD by
seven orders of magnitude at the shortest lags. Two attribution rules were tried and both
failed, in opposite directions , so the discontinuity is left as
a discontinuity and the guarantee is taken one stage later: a record out of reach of its
own first fix is refused whole (Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter}). The reach test acts on
spherical distances before projection and smoothing, with a \SI{50}{\metre}
tolerance. The written Cartesian trajectories undergo separate diagnostic checks
(Sec.~\ref{sec:dataset-checks}); those operations do not preserve the reach inequality
as an exact invariant.}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Frozen-position runs and the evidence for GNSS lock loss.}
A logger may repeat its last position after losing GNSS lock while timestamps continue.
An unremoved run then resembles a period of spatial confinement. The detector uses
three joint conditions,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:frozenlock}
 D_I<\delta_{xy},\qquad t_{\max I}-t_{\min I}\ge\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}},\qquad W_I=1,
\end{equation}
where $D_I$ is the great-circle diagonal of the latitude--longitude bounding box of
candidate run $I$, evaluated after longitude unwrapping. This is the implemented
extent proxy, not an exact all-pairs diameter. The working values are
$\delta_{xy}=\SI{\PreprocFrozenEpsM}{\metre}$ and
$\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}=\SI{\PreprocFrozenTauS}{\second}$.

Let $E_I$ denote exact equality of both coordinate fields throughout the run,
$d_I$ the fraction of its fixes with a \texttt{V} flag or missing GNSS altitude, and
$\Delta b_I$ the median pressure altitude of the final quarter minus that of the first
quarter. With $B_I$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:baro-coverage}, the witness is
\begin{equation}
 W_I=E_I\ \lor\
 \begin{cases}
  |\Delta b_I|<\delta_z, & B_I=1,\\
  \mathrm{false}, & B_{\mathrm{flight}}=1,\ B_I=0,\\
  d_I>1/2, & B_{\mathrm{flight}}=0,
 \end{cases}
 \qquad \delta_z=\SI{\PreprocFrozenDeltaZM}{\metre}.
 \label{eq:frozen-witness}
\end{equation}
The middle branch is essential: incomplete local pressure coverage is not evidence
of a flat trace. An exact coordinate repeat remains a separate signature even if the
pressure channel is missing. Once the run is removed, the trajectory is always split
there; no straight-line interpolation represents its unobserved path.

These criteria support a practical detector, not a proof of lock loss. Equal quantized
coordinates can also occur for a stationary receiver; with sparse raw sampling, a long
span between a few fixes is not proof of continuous repetition throughout the interval. A small net pressure change can
hide an up-and-down excursion, and a working barometer can continue climbing during a
real horizontal GNSS freeze. Moreover, pressure and GNSS are physically distinct sensors
but their recorded failures need not be statistically independent if the logger freezes
both channels. The word \emph{witness} therefore means corroborating evidence.

For scale, a coordinated turn in still air has
\begin{equation}
 R=\frac{v_{\mathrm{air}}^2}{g\tan\phi},\qquad
 T_{\mathrm{turn}}=\frac{2\pi R}{v_{\mathrm{air}}}.
 \label{eq:turn-radius-cleaning}
\end{equation}
At $v_{\mathrm{air}}=\SI{10}{\metre\per\second}$ and bank angle $\phi=35^\circ$,
$2R\simeq\SI{29}{\metre}$ exceeds $\delta_{xy}$, while a sustained
\SI{1}{\metre\per\second} climb changes pressure altitude far more than $\delta_z$.
These are reference scales, not universal minimum diameters or guarantees for every
wind, bank angle and recorder cadence.

The remaining errors have clear directions. A small genuine manoeuvre can be cut if
all signatures happen to agree; a jittering freeze can be kept without an adequate
witness; a short freeze is deliberately kept below $\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$.
A single missed short run has limited duration, but multiple missed runs can accumulate.
Their rates cannot be inferred from the number the detector removes: independent
inspection or labelled injections are required.
\end{revblock}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Completeness, the integrity gate, and validation.}
The segmentation (Ch.~\ref{ch:phases}) needs a defined position and altitude at every time
within a \emph{segment}, a maximal stretch between two splits, not everywhere;
cleaning does not guarantee this fix by fix, and the guarantee is established one step
later, at resampling (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). Cleaning also
counts how much it had to do over the airborne window (defects in a ground phase that
trimming removes anyway must not condemn the flight): a flight whose cleaning removed or
reconstructed more than \SI{\PreprocIntegrityMaxPct}{\percent} of its fixes is dropped
whole.

Validation must distinguish three questions. Removal fractions measure how much the
pipeline changes the data. Threshold sweeps measure sensitivity to working values.
Neither measures false positives or false negatives. Those require inspected real
intervals or known injected defects, with the limitation that injection tests establish
performance only for the injected shapes and backgrounds. The synthetic regressions and
the real examples below check explicit failure modes; a representative, independently
labelled audit remains needed for an archive-wide error rate.

\subsection{Trimming of ground phases}
\label{sec:trimming}
\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{Outer ground phases (take-off and landing).}
The logger starts recording before take-off and stops after landing; these ground phases
must be stripped before any kinematic analysis, using the horizontal speed $v_{xy}$ alone.
With $v_0=\SI{\PreprocTakeoffSpeedMps}{\metre\per\second}$ and
$T_0=\SI{\PreprocSustainedS}{\second}$ (Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}), the retained
airborne segment is $[t_{\mathrm{on}},t_{\mathrm{off}}]$ with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trimming}
  t_{\mathrm{on}}=\min\bigl\{t:\ v_{xy}>v_0\ \text{on}\ [t,\,t+T_0]\bigr\},
  \qquad
  t_{\mathrm{off}}=\max\bigl\{t:\ v_{xy}>v_0\ \text{on}\ [t-T_0,\,t]\bigr\},
\end{equation}
the same sustained-speed rule read forward and time-reversed. Here $v_{xy}$ is the
speed inferred on each observed step, not a continuously measured velocity.
Interior slow periods
do not define these outer boundaries, although slow flight at either end can be clipped. A flight with no sustained
stretch at all is discarded.

The persistence time $T_0$ reduces sensitivity to brief speed excursions. It does
not bound take-off or landing error by $T_0$: a sustained fast ground movement can be
retained, while a long airborne interval below $v_0$ at either end can be removed.
The trimmed fraction is recorded per flight, and such cases require direct inspection
.

\paragraph{Interior ground stints (mid-flight landings).}
A mid-flight landing and relaunch recorded in one log would otherwise survive as an
interior near-zero-speed stint and read downstream as a false long wait in the tail of
$\psi(\tau)$. An interior-ground detector, built like the frozen-lock rule, cuts a stint
only when the horizontal and barometric conditions agree:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Sustained low speed.} Successive observed steps remain below $v_0$
        for at least $T_{\mathrm{ground}}=\SI{\PreprocInteriorGroundMin}{\minute}$.
        This working persistence threshold reduces cuts of short slow intervals;
        it does not prove that a longer interval occurred on the ground.
  \item \emph{A flat barometer.} With complete paired pressure coverage throughout
        the candidate stint (Eq.~\ref{eq:baro-coverage}), its detrended central span stays within
        $\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}=\SI{\PreprocGroundFlatnessM}{\metre}$ over the whole
        stint, the same independent-sensor argument as Eq.~\eqref{eq:frozenlock}: the
        adopted GNSS altitude cannot itself supply this witness, its vertical noise
        already of the order of $\delta_z^{\mathrm{gr}}$. For independent Gaussian
        noise the full range typically increases with sample count. A central span
        is less sensitive to isolated extremes, although it still varies between
        finite samples .
\end{itemize}
The fitted pressure-altitude slope must also satisfy
$|\widehat\beta|\le\delta_z/\tau_{\mathrm{freeze}}$: removing a linear trend alone
would erase a steady real climb. Where the barometric witness is absent or locally
incomplete, the guard \emph{abstains} outright rather than fall
back to sustained stillness alone -- which is exactly the soaring-into-wind failure mode
the second signature exists to prevent -- at the cost of never flagging a mid-flight
landing on those flights .

A cut stint is excised and the flight split there, exactly as at a long gap
(Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). The flight-level cuts of Sec.~\ref{sec:flightfilter} are not
re-applied to the individual segments, but to the \emph{parent} flight, its airborne
duration and path length summed over its segments, since a segment is short because a
gap happened to fall there, which says nothing about whether the flight is genuinely
cross-country. Trimming therefore runs before the flight-level
cuts in the pipeline order (step~(iii) before step~(iv)), so a duration or path length is
only meaningful once the ground phases are gone.

Shorter intervals satisfying the same speed and barometric conditions are
\emph{flagged} when their duration reaches
\SI{\PreprocFrozenTauS}{\second}, the configured minimum passed to this stage
. They remain in the trajectories. The stored flags
support a future sensitivity analysis with and without these observations;
that comparison has not yet been carried out.
\end{revblock}

\subsection{Flight-level filtering}
\label{sec:flightfilter}
\begin{revblock}
The following thresholds define the population analysed in Chapters~\ref{ch:global}
and~\ref{ch:phases}. The retained-fraction curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc} show
how sensitive sample size is to each choice. A plateau does not establish that
excluded records are defective or that selection is unbiased. Duration selection,
for example, changes which flights can contribute at long lags and can therefore
change the estimated scaling.

The criteria act on the trimmed parent flight. Recorded duration $T_{\mathrm{rec}}$
and path length $L$ sum only within contiguous blocks, excluding forced boundaries
and gaps longer than $g_{\max}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}). Elapsed span
$T_{\mathrm{span}}=t_{\mathrm{last}}-t_{\mathrm{first}}$ includes those gaps
.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
  \item \emph{Duration:} $T_{\mathrm{rec}}\geq\SI{\PreprocMinDurationMin}{\minute}$
        and $T_{\mathrm{span}}\leq\SI{\PreprocMaxDurationH}{\hour}$. The upper bound
        screens long recording sessions, including loggers left running after
        landing. The raw census contains \num{\StatScanParaOverlongCount}
        paraglider records spanning
        \SIrange{\StatScanParaOverlongMinH}{\StatScanParaOverlongMaxH}{\hour},
        and none among hang gliders. These raw counts are not post-trimming
        removal counts.
  \item \emph{Path length:} $L\geq\SI{\PreprocMinPathKm}{\kilo\metre}$ restricts
        short-distance records. In the raw diagnostic population, among flights
        above the duration floor, \num{\StatScanParaShortPathCount} of
        \num{\StatScanParaLongEnoughCount} paraglider records and
        \num{\StatScanHangShortPathCount} of \num{\StatScanHangLongEnoughCount}
        hang-glider records fall below this path threshold. Neither duration nor
        path length alone identifies a flight as soaring.
  \item \emph{Altitude activity.} Where finite altitude is available, the adopted
        channel's whole-flight range must reach
        \SI{\PreprocMinAltRangeM}{\metre}. This selects appreciable vertical
        excursion; soaring at nearly constant altitude can fail the cut. The
        threshold is therefore a sample restriction, not a classifier of
        soaring versus non-soaring. The cut, and its diagnostic in
        Fig.~\ref{fig:preproc}c,f, act on the adopted GNSS channel over the whole
        population (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}) .
  \item \emph{Mean speed:} $L/T_{\mathrm{rec}}\leq v_{xy}^{\max}$, with consistent
        length and time units. If every included step already satisfies the same
        bound, its duration-weighted mean does too. This is a consistency check
        on the accumulated quantities, not independent evidence of data quality
        .
  \item \emph{Reach.} No fix may lie further from the first than the bound could have
        carried it in the elapsed time, $\Delta s(\text{fix}_0,\text{fix}_i)\le
        v_{xy}^{\max}\,t_i+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{reach}}$, with
        $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{reach}}=\SI{50}{\metre}$ and $t_i$ measured from the
        first trimmed fix. The test acts before projection and smoothing, for every
        $i$, not as the whole-flight
        extent against the whole duration, since that weaker form would license a fix
        \SI{100}{\kilo\metre} out ten seconds into the record on the grounds that the
        flight went on for three hours. This is what catches a corrupt first fix once its
        step is no longer summed into $L$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:fixlevel}): it removes
        \num{\StatPipeParaDropExtentOutOfReachOfTheFirstFix} paraglider and
        \num{\StatPipeHangDropExtentOutOfReachOfTheFirstFix} hang-glider flights, order
        $10^{-3}$ of either ensemble in the stored snapshot. The written Cartesian
        trajectories have separate checks with operational tolerances
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:dataset-checks}).
  \item \emph{Glider class} (metadata). Flights whose \texttt{aile\_class} cannot be
        normalised (Sec.~\ref{sec:glider}) are dropped from class-resolved sub-analyses
        but kept in the pooled one; stratification by discipline is already possible.
\end{enumerate}
A further criterion, sampling regularity, is applied later, where a flight's time base is
examined (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).
\end{revblock}

\subsection{From geographic to local Cartesian coordinates}
\label{sec:enu}
\rev{The cleaned, trimmed track is next mapped from geographic to local Cartesian
coordinates: resampling and smoothing operate on the coordinates \emph{componentwise}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:preproc}), and latitudes and longitudes cannot play that role, being angles
on a curved surface whose componentwise differences are not displacements. The mapping
proceeds in two steps.}

\paragraph{Step 1: geodetic to ECEF.} Each fix is converted to Earth-Centred,
Earth-Fixed (ECEF) coordinates on the WGS84 ellipsoid (semi-major axis
$a=\SI{6378137}{\metre}$, flattening $f=1/298.257223563$, $e^2=f(2-f)$)~\cite{nga_wgs84,esa_navipedia_ellipsoidal_cartesian},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ecef}
N(\varphi)=\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}},\qquad
\begin{aligned}
X &= (N+h)\cos\varphi\cos\lambda,\\
Y &= (N+h)\cos\varphi\sin\lambda,\\
Z &= \big(N(1-e^2)+h\big)\sin\varphi,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$N(\varphi)$ the prime-vertical radius of curvature and $h$ the ellipsoidal height along
the normal, derived step by step in Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}.

\paragraph{Geodetic versus geocentric latitude.} \rev{The latitude $\varphi$ is
\emph{geodetic}: the angle between the equatorial plane and the ellipsoid \emph{normal}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ellipsoid}), not the geocentric latitude $\psi$ measured to the Earth's
centre $O$, since the normal does not pass through it. The gap
$\varphi-\psi\approx(e^2/2)\sin2\varphi$ (Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}) peaks at
$\varphi=45^\circ$ and reaches $\approx0.19^\circ$, which if confused would misplace a fix
by up to ${\sim}\SI{20}{\kilo\metre}$. No conversion is needed here: WGS84 --- hence the GPS
receiver and the IGC \texttt{B} records (Sec.~\ref{sec:igcformat}) --- already reports
geodetic latitude, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:ecef} with $N(\varphi)$ is precisely the transform
that consumes it; these are also the $(\varphi_0,\lambda_0)$ that orient the local frame of
Step~2 (Figure~\ref{fig:enu-rotation}).}

\paragraph{Step 2: ECEF to local ENU.} \rev{Taking the first fix $(\varphi_0,\lambda_0,h_0)$
of the \emph{trimmed} track as the origin --- close to but not the take-off point, since
the ground phase has been removed (Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming}) --- the ECEF displacement
$\Delta\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}_0$ is rotated into the local East--North--Up (ENU)
frame tangent to the ellipsoid at that point,}
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}E\\N\\U\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\sin\lambda_0 & \cos\lambda_0 & 0\\
-\sin\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0 & -\sin\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0 & \cos\varphi_0\\
\cos\varphi_0\cos\lambda_0 & \cos\varphi_0\sin\lambda_0 & \sin\varphi_0
\end{pmatrix}
\Delta\mathbf{X},
\end{equation}
derived in Appendix~\ref{app:geodesy}: an orthogonal matrix of unit determinant, so lengths
and angles are preserved and only the axes change. This ENU frame is the local Cartesian
frame the whole analysis is carried out in ($E$ east, $N$ north, $U$ along the local
vertical); the ECEF coordinates of Step~1 are only an intermediate representation, no
observable is ever expressed in them. The rotation preserves the three-dimensional ECEF chord. Keeping only $(E,N)$
projects that chord onto the tangent plane and introduces a different approximation.
For a point at surface distance $d$ from the origin on a sphere,
\begin{equation}
 r_{EN}=R_\oplus\sin(d/R_\oplus),\qquad
 d-r_{EN}=\frac{d^3}{6R_\oplus^2}+O(d^5/R_\oplus^4).
 \label{eq:enu-projection-error}
\end{equation}
The horizontal shortening is about \SI{0.03}{\metre} at \SI{20}{\kilo\metre} and
\SI{0.51}{\metre} at \SI{50}{\kilo\metre}; it reaches about \SI{33}{\metre} at
\SI{200}{\kilo\metre}. The often-used $d^3/(24R_\oplus^2)$ expression instead compares
an arc with its full three-dimensional chord. Neither estimate is a uniform error
bound for every pair of fixes in a fixed frame.

The transform uses the logged GNSS altitude as its height input. Since the recorder's
vertical datum is not yet harmonized, this is an approximation to ellipsoidal height.
A height error $\delta h$ contributes a horizontal error of order
$|\delta h|\sin(d/R_\oplus)\simeq |\delta h|d/R_\oplus$: for example,
\SI{100}{\metre} at \SI{50}{\kilo\metre} gives about \SI{0.8}{\metre}. This is an
illustrative scale, not an archive-wide upper bound.
The vertical coordinate remains $z(t)=h_{\mathrm{GNSS}}(t)$ rather than the rotation's
$U$ component, which also contains the curvature of the reference surface. The origin
altitude is not subtracted; vertical velocities depend on increments, whereas absolute
height retains the datum limitation stated in Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}.

\subsection{Uniform sampling within a flight}
\label{sec:uniform}

The smoothing and derivative estimates in the next section require equally spaced
observations. Each flight keeps its native interval,
$\Delta t=\operatorname{median}_i(t_{i+1}-t_i)$: a flight recorded every second
stays at one second, and a flight recorded every five seconds stays at five seconds.
Figure~\ref{fig:sampling} shows these intervals in the archive.

\paragraph{Fill a short gap; split at a long one.}
For a gap $g=t_{i+1}-t_i$ between surviving fixes, interpolation is allowed when
$g\le g_{\max}$, with
\begin{equation}
 g_{\max}=\min\!\left(
   \PreprocMaxGapFactor\,\Delta t,\;
   \max\!\left(\SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second},\,2\Delta t\right)\right).
 \label{eq:uniform-gap-bound}
\end{equation}
The relative limit scales with the recording interval; the
\SI{\PreprocMaxGapS}{\second} cap limits the time reconstructed at common cadences.
The $2\Delta t$ floor allows one missed fix even for slower loggers.
Thus $g_{\max}=\SI{10}{\second}$ at $\Delta t=\SI{1}{\second}$, and
$g_{\max}=\SI{20}{\second}$ at $\Delta t=\SI{5}{\second}$.
A longer gap starts a new segment. Boundaries imposed by cleaning also force a split.
The cap is a working choice: it limits interpolation without guaranteeing that all
manoeuvres inside a short gap are resolved.

\paragraph{Reconstructing the coordinates.}
Within segment $s$, the grid is $t_k^{(s)}=t_{\mathrm{start}}^{(s)}+k\Delta t$,
ending at or before its last fix. An already uniform, complete series is unchanged.
Otherwise, $E$ and $N$ are evaluated with a shape-preserving cubic interpolant
(PCHIP), and $z$ with linear interpolation. Linear filling avoids overshoot in altitude
but can smooth away a climb--glide transition. Outside the first and last finite
altitude readings, the implementation carries the nearest endpoint value.

A missing altitude at an existing fix is treated separately
(Fig.~\ref{fig:uniform-sampling-schematic}c). It opens no time gap, so the horizontal
trajectory stays connected and the altitude is filled without a duration limit.
The flag \texttt{interpolated} records grid times with no fix within $\Delta t/2$;
\texttt{z\_reconstructed} additionally marks those whose nearest fix had no altitude.
Smoothing propagates altitude reconstruction through the derivative windows into
\texttt{z\_derivative\_reconstructed}. These flags allow the phase analysis to exclude
unsupported vertical dynamics
(Chapter~\ref{ch:phases}); numerical completeness alone does not establish accuracy.

\paragraph{Which segments are retained.}
A segment must span at least \SI{\PreprocMinSegmentS}{\second}, have at most
\SI{\PreprocMaxMissingPct}{\percent} of its grid times flagged as
\texttt{interpolated}, and yield finite values in all three coordinates.
The temporal-coverage gate does not limit altitude-only reconstruction.
A rejected segment leaves the other segments available; the parent's flight-level
cuts are not reapplied.

Segments retain the flight's spatial origin and elapsed time. Time-lagged displacement
pairs and analysis windows are formed within segments. Splitting therefore changes the
available coverage and can truncate flight phases; it must be accounted for when
comparing observables. Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} describes gaps in the raw archive;
observables that depend on the recording interval, such as
fix-to-fix turning angles, require a common temporal scale or a comparison within
cadence groups.

\subsection{Smoothing and differentiation}
\label{sec:savgol}

Position errors are amplified by differentiation. We estimate position, velocity and
acceleration from the same local polynomial, fitted independently to $E$, $N$ and $z$
within each retained segment. For $w=2m+1$ equally spaced samples around fix $i$, the
Savitzky--Golay fit~\cite{savitzky1964} is
\begin{align}
 P_i(u)&=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_k(i)u^k,\qquad u=-m,\ldots,m,\label{eq:savgol-poly}\\
 \mathbf a(i)&=\underset{\mathbf a}{\arg\min}\,
       \sum_{u=-m}^{m}[x_{i+u}-P_i(u)]^2.\label{eq:savgol-lsq}
\end{align}
Here $u$ is a sample offset. The physical quantities at the window centre are
\begin{equation}
 \widehat x_i=a_0(i),\qquad
 \widehat v_i=\frac{a_1(i)}{\Delta t},\qquad
 \widehat a_i=\frac{2a_2(i)}{\Delta t^2}.
 \label{eq:savgol-physical}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{The weights.}
With $V_{u,k}=u^k$ and $\mathbf x_i=(x_{i-m},\ldots,x_{i+m})^\top$, the mathematical
least-squares solution is
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf a(i)=(V^\top V)^{-1}V^\top\mathbf x_i.
 \label{eq:savgol-solution}
\end{equation}
The inverse is a formula for the weights, not a recommendation to form a matrix inverse
numerically. The implementation uses SciPy's Savitzky--Golay coefficients. For fixed
$(w,p)$, row $k$ defines weights $c_k(u)$ with
\begin{equation}
 a_k(i)=\sum_{u=-m}^{m}c_k(u)x_{i+u}.
 \label{eq:savgol-convolution}
\end{equation}
This forward-offset convention is a correlation; convolution uses the reversed weights.
For an odd derivative the reversal changes their signs. Table~\ref{tab:savgol-kernels}
gives the forward-offset weights, before conversion to seconds.

The order $p=\PreprocSavgolOrder$ allows nonzero acceleration and locally varying
acceleration. A quadratic already estimates velocity and acceleration; a cubic is a
modelling choice, not a mathematical necessity. On a symmetric window, adding a cubic
term changes the first-derivative weights but leaves the value and second-derivative
weights of the quadratic fit unchanged.

\paragraph{Window length and cadence.}
A raw spectral diagnostic suggests a change in slope around \SI{0.2}{\hertz}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:savgol-spectrum}). It motivates the working timescale
\begin{equation}
 \tau_c=1/f_c=\SI{\PreprocSavgolTauCS}{\second},\qquad
 w=\max\!\left(\left\lceil\frac{\tau_c}{\Delta t}\right\rceil_{\mathrm{odd}},
               \PreprocSavgolMinWindow\right).
 \label{eq:savgol-window}
\end{equation}
\label{eq:savgol-tauc}
Horizontal and vertical timescales are separate configuration entries, currently equal.
The code converts this timescale to a \emph{number of samples}: the distance in time
between the first and last sample of a window is $(w-1)\Delta t$. Thus $w=5$ spans
\SI{4}{\second} at a \SI{1}{\second} cadence, and \SI{20}{\second} at a
\SI{5}{\second} cadence. Slower recording therefore changes the physical smoothing
scale and can remove motion that faster loggers resolve. Comparisons of short-time
observables must control for cadence.

The spectral knee is neither a fitted receiver-noise model nor the cutoff frequency
of this filter. For the value weights, the interior frequency response is
\begin{equation}
 G(f)=\sum_{u=-m}^{m}c_0(u)e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}fu\Delta t},\qquad
 S_{\widehat x}(f)=|G(f)|^2 S_x(f).
 \label{eq:savgol-response}
\end{equation}
Its shape follows from the kernel (Fig.~\ref{fig:savgol-response}). Rounding a field to
steps of size $q$ has variance $q^2/12$ only under a uniform-error approximation;
a flat quantization spectrum additionally requires temporal independence. Similar
high-frequency spectra do not establish those assumptions or identify the source of
the observed knee.

 not estimates with an uncertainty interval. Recording precision, sensor error,
 interpolation and real motion can all affect these spectra.}
 \label{fig:savgol-spectrum}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{What smoothing changes in an MSD.}
For an interior sample with independent, zero-mean measurement error of variance
$\sigma^2$ per coordinate, the filtered error is
$\eta_i=\sum_u c_0(u)\varepsilon_{i+u}$. Extending the weights by zero outside the
window gives its covariance and its contribution to a one-coordinate MSD:
\begin{align}
 C_\eta(\ell)&=\sigma^2\sum_u c_0(u)c_0(u-\ell),\\
 B(\ell)&=\mathbb E[(\eta_{i+\ell}-\eta_i)^2]
       =2\sigma^2\!\left[\sum_u c_0(u)^2-\sum_u c_0(u)c_0(u-\ell)\right].
 \label{eq:savgol-msd-noise}
\end{align}
For $w=5$, $p=3$, $\sum c_0^2=17/35$. At lags $\ell\ge5$ the noise contribution
is therefore $34\sigma^2/35$ per coordinate, compared with $2\sigma^2$ before
smoothing. A horizontal MSD adds the contributions of both coordinates. At shorter
lags, overlapping windows correlate the errors, so the bias is lag dependent.
These expressions require independent errors; they are not an empirical GNSS
calibration. The filter also changes the true motion unless it is polynomial of degree
at most $p$ on every relevant window. A Hurst exponent alone supplies no bound on that
change.

 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/savgol_response}
 \caption{Exact effects of the five-sample cubic filter away from segment edges.
 Left: power transmission $|G(f)|^2$ against normalized frequency $f\Delta t$.
 Right: additive one-coordinate MSD from independent unit-variance measurement noise.
 The remaining bias depends on lag and does not equal the unfiltered $2\sigma^2$.}
 \label{fig:savgol-response}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Edges and validation.}
At either end of a segment, the filter fits the nearest full window and evaluates that
polynomial inside its fitted interval. These are off-centre evaluations, not
extrapolation beyond the observations. The first and last $m$ samples carry an
\texttt{edge} flag because their weights and error variance differ from the interior.
The filter can overshoot despite earlier cleaning, so speed bounds are not guaranteed
by smoothing.

The kernel identities and derivative units are checked in automated tests. Archive-level
validation remains separate: after rebuilding the cleaned archive, compare the MSD and
phase features at neighbouring odd window lengths, within each cadence class, and with
edge and reconstructed samples excluded. A flat residual spectrum is not a sufficient
criterion: even white input noise has residual PSD $|1-G(f)|^2S_\varepsilon(f)$.
Until these comparisons are reported, the adopted window is a working choice rather
than a certified optimum.

\subsection{Notation}
\label{sec:notation}
Throughout:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Horizontal position.} $\mathbf{r}(t)=\big(x(t),y(t)\big)=\big(E(t),N(t)\big)$,
        in this local frame. Before smoothing, the first trimmed fix defines
        $\mathbf{r}(0)=\mathbf{0}$; the filtered output can differ slightly at this edge.
  \item \textbf{Vertical position.} $z(t)$, the adopted altitude channel
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:altchannel}). Unlike $\mathbf{r}$, it is \emph{not} re-zeroed: it
        is kept at its measured value, so the height at the start of free flight is
        retained (Sec.~\ref{sec:enu}).
  \item \textbf{Time.} $t$, the elapsed flight time, with the clock reset to $t=0$ at the
        start of free flight (the first fix of the trimmed track, Sec.~\ref{sec:trimming}).
        A segment created by a split at a long gap keeps its parent flight's origin and
        clock (Sec.~\ref{sec:uniform}).
  \item \textbf{Velocity.} $\mathbf{v}(t)=\dot{\mathbf{r}}(t)$, the horizontal velocity,
        with magnitude the horizontal speed $v_{xy}=|\mathbf{v}|$; $v_z=\dot z$, the
        vertical velocity.
  \item \textbf{Displacement.} $\Delta\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}(t_0+t)-\mathbf{r}(t_0)$, the
        horizontal displacement over a lag $t$.
\end{itemize}
A terminological rule holds throughout the document: a bare \enquote{position} means
$\mathbf{r}$, the horizontal one; a statement about the full three-dimensional point says
\emph{3D position} explicitly.

Two decisions organise the chain.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The order.} Every check on the scalar record as logged (times, geodetic
        coordinates, altitude) comes first (stages~1--4), and the vector geometry is
        built only from what survives (stages~5--7). Converting coordinates does not
        clean a record: it recomputes each fix in a new frame, corrupt or not. Running
        it before the cleaning stages would therefore buy nothing, and would cost
        rework whenever trimming moved the fix the frame is anchored to, or filtering
        dropped the record outright.
  \item \textbf{The level.} Each stage acts per fix, per flight or per segment, and
        every parameter it adapts is read off the record it is acting on rather than off
        the archive around it. No flight's cleaning therefore depends on
        which other flights happen to be in the dataset. What is shared across flights
        is only the thresholds of Table~\ref{tab:workingparams}, fixed a priori.
\end{itemize}

\begin{revblock}
\paragraph{The analysis ensemble, defined.}
\label{par:ensemble}
Everything downstream reads one object, and this is it. The \emph{analysis ensemble} is
the set of trajectories written to \path{fixes.parquet}: one row per grid point of every
\emph{retained} segment of every \emph{retained} flight, carrying the elapsed time $t$ in
the parent clock and the local Cartesian coordinates $(E,N,z)$ with their first two
derivatives. A flight is retained when it passes stages 1--4 of
Figure~\ref{fig:pipelinemap} and at least one of its segments survives stage 6; a segment
is retained when it is long enough and dense enough on its own uniform grid. That leaves
\num{\StatPipeParaKept} paraglider flights of \num{\StatPipeParaAttempted}
(\SI{\StatPipeParaKeptPct}{\percent}) in \num{\StatMsdParaSegments} segments, and
\num{\StatPipeHangKept} hang-glider flights of \num{\StatPipeHangAttempted}
(\SI{\StatPipeHangKeptPct}{\percent}) in \num{\StatMsdHangSegments}. No consumer re-applies
a retention rule: a flight or segment that should not be analysed has no rows to read.
Every segment keeps its parent's origin and clock, so a position is always measured from
that flight's take-off and a time is always elapsed since it, whether or not the record is
continuous up to that point. Section~\ref{sec:prelim} characterises this ensemble,
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} analyses it, and \path{docs/guide/data-on-disk.md} states which
columns each estimator reads and what a segment boundary means to it.
\end{revblock}

\section{Preliminary characterization}
\label{sec:prelim}
This section describes the retained flights: their duration, path length, recording
interval and geographical coverage. These measurements require no phase segmentation.
Directional differences and their relation to geography are examined with the transport
observables in Sec.~\ref{sec:transport-anisotropy}.

\paragraph{Trajectory statistics.}
For each retained flight: airborne duration $T$, total horizontal path length, number of
valid fixes and native sampling interval $\Delta t$. Their distributions
(Fig.~\ref{fig:prelim-ensemble}) describe the dataset the results rest on and allow unusually large values to be
inspected. The native interval $\Delta t$ determines the per-flight smoothing
window (Sec.~\ref{sec:savgol}). Over the \num{\StatPrelimParaFlights} retained paraglider
flights the median airborne duration is \SI{\StatPrelimParaMedianDurH}{\hour}
(interquartile range \SIrange{\StatPrelimParaDurationIqrLowH}{\StatPrelimParaDurationIqrHighH}{\hour}),
the median flown path \SI{\StatPrelimParaMedianPathKm}{\kilo\metre}
(\SIrange{\StatPrelimParaPathIqrLowKm}{\StatPrelimParaPathIqrHighKm}{\kilo\metre}) and the
median record \num{\StatPrelimParaMedianFixes} fixes;
\SI{\StatPrelimParaOneHertzPct}{\percent} of them log at \SI{1}{\hertz}. The
\num{\StatPrelimHangFlights} hang-glider flights have larger median duration and path length---%
\SI{\StatPrelimHangMedianDurH}{\hour} and \SI{\StatPrelimHangMedianPathKm}{\kilo\metre} in
the median---and slower-logging, with a median $\Delta t$ of
\SI{\StatPrelimHangMedianDtS}{\second} against \SI{\StatPrelimParaMedianDtS}{\second} and
only \SI{\StatPrelimHangOneHertzPct}{\percent} at \SI{1}{\hertz}.

\label{sec:dataset-checks}

For paragliders one criterion carries the cost, the resampling sparsity rule:
\StatDataParaWorstCutRemoved\ flights, \SI{\StatDataParaWorstCutPct}{\percent} of the

using the bands $<\SI{300}{\metre}$, \SIrange{300}{800}{\metre},
\SIrange{800}{1500}{\metre} and $\ge\SI{1500}{\metre}$ adopted in the regional
classification of Hern\'{a}ndez-Aguayo, Cristelli and Benzaquen
\cite{hernandezaguayo2026}. These altitude bands are a simplification, not the full
mountain classification of Kapos et al.~\cite{kapos2000}.
The present altitude is a recorder measurement before trimming, not a terrain-model
elevation: the logger can start away from launch, already airborne, or with an
unsettled sensor. GNSS is preferred, with a pressure-altitude fallback in this
exploratory map only. The historical terrain names in its legend identify the bands;
they do not establish local relief, slope or mountain geometry. Datum and atmospheric
offsets can also move a fix across a band boundary.

\section{Chapter summary}

The recorder archive has been converted into trajectories with explicit temporal support,
local horizontal coordinates and traceable quality decisions. Cleaning separates isolated
position defects, repeated-position runs and sustained recording failures; it uses a
barometric witness only where that channel is available over the interval being tested.
The 10-m/s windowed vertical-speed bound is an operational selection criterion, not a
measured physical ceiling for every soaring flight.

Gaps, reconstructed altitude and derivative support remain visible in the retained data.
Uniform sampling makes within-segment lag statistics possible, while the masks prevent
interpolation or uncertain derivatives from silently becoming evidence for a phase.
The geographical and temporal composition of the archive also remains heterogeneous.
Chapter~\ref{ch:global} therefore treats weighting, lag support and environmental
conditioning as part of the definition of each observable, rather than as incidental
details of its calculation.
